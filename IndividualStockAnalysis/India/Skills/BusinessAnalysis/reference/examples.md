# Worked examples from the document

The illustrative companies cited in `Company_analysis.docx`, mapped to the
parameters they exemplify. Use these as calibration anchors when scoring.

## Capital allocation — M&A done well `[CAP.mergers_acquisitions]`
- **Essilor** — leader in lenses; focuses on small bolt-on acquisitions; added
  ~3% annually to sales.
- **Luxottica** — buys already-successful brands (e.g. Oakley) and uses its
  distribution to sell more.
- **Diageo** — leverages network effects and a complementary distribution network.
- **Assa Abloy** — global leader in locks/door-opening; ~120 acquisitions to
  expand distribution reach and access technology; improved the acquiree's OPM.

## Return on capital — asset-light + moat `[ROC.asset_turn]`
- **Domino's** — high asset turns and low asset intensity, but protected by a
  strong brand (asset-light alone isn't enough).

## Growth `[GRW.*]`
- **Unilever** — geographical expansion; ~60% revenue from emerging markets.
- **Nike** — premium brand transitions easily to new markets.
- **Marriott** — 2009→2015 became ~6x by riding the cyclical recovery (invest at
  the bottom of the cycle).

## Management `[MGT.*]`
- **Handelsbanken** — decentralised, profit-sharing plan, embraces risk aversion
  (independent, long-term, tenacious).
- **Rolls-Royce** — stuck with the civil-aircraft-engine plan long-term: sell
  engines first, then earn recurring servicing revenue.
- **Atlas Copco** — talent development; four former execs went on to lead other
  majors; rotates executives every ~3 years across roles.

## Industry structure `[IND.*]`
- **Tobacco** — mini-monopoly in the customer's mind (brand loyalty).
- **Ambev** — ~50% EBITDA margins in Brazil; logistics barriers to entry (partial
  monopoly / broken competition).
- **Atlas Copco (compressors)** — good service margins AND good upfront product
  margins (switching costs).
- **Coca-Cola (North America)** — taught consumers to buy in bulk on sale, hurting
  profitability (irrational industry behaviour).
- **LVMH** — did not discount champagne even in 2008 (pricing discipline).
- Humble niches — **locks, lenses, bathroom fittings** (security by obscurity).

## Customer benefits `[CUS.*]`
- **L'Oréal** — a small tub retails ~$90, ~5x a mass offering like **Nivea**;
  consumers never test the comparison (intangible benefits).
- **Industrial gases** (oxygen, hydrogen, CO2) and **baby food** — assurance
  benefits; buyers are price-insensitive.
- **SGS and Intertek** — testing companies; complex global supply chains; high cost
  of deviation; only a few trusted testers can exist ("when it pays to be sure").

## Competitive advantage `[MOAT.*]`
- **Jet engines** (post-1960s) — incremental innovation; slow but substantial
  fuel-efficiency gains.
- **Google** — advantage in data collection and manipulation.
- **Syngenta** (Novartis + AstraZeneca) — world leader in crop protection; broadest
  crop presence; ~$300M and ~10 years per API; ~$4B R&D over 3 years; ~$3B peak
  sales potential; farmers keep buying seeds even in downturns.
- **Facebook** — network effects; killed MySpace and MSN chat (innovation-pace
  risk). UK housing agents formed a rival network (too-strong network effects
  backfiring).
