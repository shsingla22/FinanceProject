# Mangalore Refinery & Petrochemicals Ltd. (MRPL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

MRPL is an Indian oil refinery company at Mangaluru on the west coast of Karnataka, and is a subsidiary of state-owned ONGC (a Schedule 'A' Government of India enterprise). It buys crude oil and other feedstocks — imported barrels from varied sources plus domestic crudes such as ONGC's East Coast KG98 and Reliance BP's KG D6 — and processes them at scale (about 16.6 million tonnes in FY24, roughly 4.3-4.6 million tonnes a quarter) into fuels like diesel, petrol/MS and jet fuel (ATF), plus petrochemical and aromatic products. Money is made on the gap between what crude costs and what the refined products fetch — management calls this the gross refining margin, quoted in dollars per barrel (e.g. $10.36/bbl in FY24, $3.88/bbl in the shutdown-hit Q1 FY26) — so profits swing with product 'cracks', crude price moves (which cause inventory gains/losses), throughput and fuel-and-loss efficiency. Products are sold in bulk into the domestic market (with a marketing focus on southern and western India), through its own growing chain of petrol pumps, and exported abroad, including diesel to Europe; quarterly revenue runs around Rs. 21,000-26,000 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Refining (the refinery / three crude trains)** — The core business by far — turning crude into mostly high-value 'distillate' fuels like diesel, petrol and ATF (distillate yield around 79-81%), and management notes MRPL is a unique Indian refinery with three separate crude trains giving maintenance flexibility, with quarterly EBITDA of Rs. 1,565 crores in Q2 FY26 and Rs. 2,824 crores in Q3 FY26 driven almost entirely by this.
- **Aromatics / paraxylene (PX) complex** — A petrochemical unit currently run in 'reformate mode', making reformate that is sold on as a feedstock and extracting benzene as a by-product plus newer products like toluene; it is a small earnings contributor — about half a dollar per barrel of margin in Q1 FY26 — and the PX/PTA/polyester chain was described as under pressure.
- **Polypropylene** — A plastics/petrochemical unit that management says runs at or slightly above its design capacity (over 100%), part of the roughly 10% 'petchem intensity' the company aims to raise to 12.5% via about Rs. 8,000 crores of investment over five years.
- **Retail marketing (own petrol pumps)** — MRPL's own branded fuel outlets, which grew from 100 in FY24 (selling about 150 kL per outlet per month, 154,000 kL total, earning Rs. 76 crores over the refinery transfer price) to 200 by Q3 FY26 with about 250 targeted by year-end — small versus refining but a deliberate push for extra marketing margin.
- **Specialty and new products** — Smaller niche lines the calls flag as growth ideas: Mineral Turpentine Oil (a paint-industry raw material, launched Q4 FY24), toluene, a patented Isobutyl Benzene (IBB) pilot plant for a pharmaceutical base, and India's first Bio-ATF plant at Rs. 364 crores to supply CORSIA-compliant blended jet fuel from 2027.
- **Energy and efficiency projects** — Not a revenue line but repeatedly cited as a profit driver — cutting 'fuel and loss' (about 11% down to a targeted 9.5-10%) via a grid-power import project, use of natural gas/RLNG as both fuel and feed (currently ~0.5 MMSCMD against a 0.7-1.0 capability), and an expanded 40 MLD desalination plant that ended summer water constraints, alongside a net-zero-by-2038 goal.

### The story in depth

Mangalore Refinery & Petrochemicals scores 29 out of 100 on the combined rating — Weak, one star — built from 38 for business quality, zero for multibagger fit and 48 for risk safety. The reason sits in one place: the company sells a product nobody can tell apart from anyone else's, at prices set somewhere else. After paying for its direct inputs the company keeps about ₹12 of every ₹100 of sales, which is thin. There is no network effect of any kind, because refined fuel is fungible and nothing about the business gets stronger as it gets bigger. Every earnings swing across the five calls is explained by how much crude went through the plant and by externally-set crack spreads — never by MRPL choosing a price or a better product mix. The numbers show what that feels like: a record refining margin of $11.35 a barrel and the highest-ever pre-tax profit in FY24, then $3.88 a barrel and a ₹272 crore loss in the June 2026 quarter. Sales have grown roughly 4% a year on average and lumpily. So the weakest quality area is competitive advantage at -1.33, and growth and industry structure both sit at about -0.67.

The pattern screen tells the same story from the other side, and this is why it contributed nothing to the score: not one of the eleven patterns is a clean fit. Three are outright misses, and all three miss for the same reason as the quality checks — no pricing power (cracks quoted at 15 to 21 then back to 14-15, Russian discounts anywhere from half a dollar to four dollars), no brand (no heritage, no advertising, no premium, and roughly 40% of turnover is undifferentiated exports), and no toll-road position (fuel is a big negotiated cost for customers, not a small unavoidable one). The remaining eight are partial, and the genuine ones are operational rather than strategic. Cost discipline is real and itemised: fuel and loss cut from 11.13% to 10.06%, a best-ever MBN of 67, a grid-power project aimed at pushing fuel and loss below 10%, and desalination removing the summer water constraint. There is a steady habit of small delivered innovations — MTO from FY24, toluene extraction from FY25, the patented Isobutyl Benzene unit promised in May 2024 and mechanically completed by October 2025, plus being the first Indian refinery certified AS9100D for jet fuel. And the plant itself — 15 MMTPA on the coast, three crude trains, its own desalination, aromatics and polypropylene — is expensive and slow to copy. Notice that the most convincing pattern, forward integration into retail (100 outlets in FY24 rising to 200 by January 2026, with depots at Mumbai and Vizag and the Devangonthi-Devanhalli pipeline), exists precisely because of a risk: management concedes new refining capacity elsewhere in India is eroding the base-refinery model, and that is the stated reason for the pivot. The strength and the threat are the same fact.

The risk review closes the loop. Two channels are high risk and both are traits already visible in the quality checks. Cyclicality is the commodity-price-taker problem seen through earnings, with the swing from record FY24 to the Q1 FY26 loss as the exhibit; its offset is the retail build-out that management says lends 'a sense of stability' — again, the pattern and the risk are one story. Government dependency is the mirror of the -1 on independent, long-term thinking: as a Schedule 'A' government enterprise and ONGC subsidiary, crude sourcing, pricing and direction are set outside the company, the sustainable aviation fuel project was mandated to it, and Russian barrels at 30-40% of the basket were dropped wholesale once sanctions bit — which is also the Stakeholder Concentration watch item. The same public-sector structure produces the leadership churn that drags the people check to -1 and holds the corporate culture pattern at partial: six chairmen across FY22-FY25 and a CFO change mid-stream. Where the company earns credit is the balance sheet, and that is what keeps overall resilience SOUND despite the loss — ₹5,058 crore of repayments in FY24 took debt-to-equity from 1.70 to 0.94, and after the Q1 FY26 setback debt was cut to ₹9,290 crore and 0.63 times by Q3 FY26, all organic. But that same choice cuts both ways: cash went to debt rather than to owners, leaving distribution ad hoc (a first-ever interim dividend in the peak FY24 year, then silence after the loss) and growth capex unconverted — the ₹8,000 crore five-year petrochemical programme announced in May 2024 was still 'under review' by October 2025 while actual spend stayed near ₹1,000 crore of largely routine work. Prudence and passivity look identical on this balance sheet, and today the record supports both readings.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 1.1× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 69% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 5% to 16% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** MRPL is a pure commodity refiner whose earnings swing violently with crude prices, cracks and inventory valuation: record GRM of $11.35/bbl and highest-ever PBT in FY24 collapsed to $3.88/bbl and a PAT loss of Rs.272 crore in Q1 FY26, then rebounded to EBITDA of Rs.2,824 crore in Q3 FY26 as HSD cracks went from ~15 to 21 and back to 14-15. Management repeatedly frames the outlook in terms of crude prices and cracks and declines to forecast ('hazarding a forecast may not be appropriate'), which is exactly the peak-versus-trough opacity the channel warns about.

> "Gross refining margin averaged at $3.88 per barrel, down from $4.7 per barrel in Q1 previous year, and $6.23 per barrel in Q4 2024-25" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/operations-linked domestic fuel demand (diesel +2-3%, gasoline +7%) and the build-out of retail outlets, which management says gives 'a sense of stability' versus volatile export sales — but at ~1.5-2% of volumes this is not yet material*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 5 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between -6% and 11% (±5.1 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 2% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 3%).

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** MRPL is a Schedule 'A' Government of India enterprise and ONGC subsidiary whose crude sourcing, pricing and strategic direction are set outside the company: Russian barrels at 30-40% of the basket were dropped entirely once sanctions bit, domestic realisation is RTP-linked to administered/international benchmarks, SAF blending and CORSIA compliance are mandates ('which has been mandated to us'), grid power depends on policymakers, and even the HPCL merger question was deflected to ONGC. Governance is equally policy-driven — seven different chairmen across FY22-FY25, all ONGC/government appointees.

> "currently there are no Russian crude which is being imported and we will continue to comply with any of these international sanctions regime or government guidelines" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Diversification into own-brand retail marketing and depots/pipelines to capture margin beyond the regulated refinery-transfer-price stream*

## What to watch
