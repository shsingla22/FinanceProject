# Abbott India (ABBOTINDIA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Abbott India Limited sells branded prescription medicines, vaccines and over-the-counter health products in India. Its customers are reached through doctors who prescribe its brands and retail pharmacies (increasingly including online pharmacy and retail-chain channels), with management also pushing into government/public-market channels such as ESIC and state and central immunisation programmes. It makes money by selling branded formulations — mega-brands like Thyronorm, Duphaston, Duphalac, Cremaffin, Udiliv, Digene and Influvac — where it invests in disease awareness, free/subsidised diagnostic testing (25 lakh thyroid tests a year, FibroScan for liver) and patient-adherence programmes to expand the category rather than just fight for brand share. It operates only in India (about 70% of sales from metros and class-one towns, now expanding into class two/three and 'extra urban' areas), is the only multinational in the top 10 of the Indian pharmaceutical market, and grew top line 14.1% last year (7.6% volume, rest price) with a stated goal of becoming a $1 billion business by 2025. About 26% of sales by value come from price-controlled NLEM products.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Women's Health** — Sells women's health medicines led by the long-standing brand Duphaston (plus newer launches Femoston for post-menopausal problems, Preservgest for pregnancy maintenance and Riligol, a heat-stable carbetocin for postpartum bleeding marketed with Ferring); the portfolio grew over 23% and Duphaston, after 60 years as the only brand, now faces about 41 competitors — it has lost share in gynaecology but held share in IVF and still grows.
- **Gastroenterology (GI)** — The company's strongest growth driver at about 27% growth, with market leadership and brands including Udiliv (chronic liver disease), Duphalac and Cremaffin/Cremaffin Plus (constipation), Digene and the new Digeraft and Digecaine antacids, plus second-line 'hidden gems' Creon, Ganaton, Librax and Heptral; management is adding headcount and launching another business unit inside GI.
- **Metabolics** — Mainly hypothyroidism treatment through Thyronorm, where Abbott is market leader with roughly 53% share and is still gaining share against about 11 competitors, supported by the MITA ('Making India Thyroid Aware') campaign and ~25 lakh thyroid tests a year; the full 12.5–150 mg range sits under NLEM price control.
- **Vaccines** — Four products — Influvac for influenza (a 100-crore-plus brand within a roughly 200-crore vaccine business), Enteroshield for typhoid, Rotasure for rotavirus, and the newly launched Havshield for Hepatitis A which should cross $1 million in its first year; growth focus is shifting to adult vaccination, which management sees as the bigger opportunity versus pediatric.
- **Over-the-counter (OTC) / direct-to-consumer** — A consumer-facing portfolio covering antacids, laxatives and some pain products, sold with heavy digital and direct-to-consumer marketing as media habits move online.
- **Top 10 brands and the 'next 10'** — The top 10 brands are each $15 million-plus and, with the top 20 brands, contribute roughly 90–93% of the business, while a 'next 10' tier of $5–15 million brands (Arachitol, Creon, Ganaton, Prothiadin, Zolfresh) is expected to grow into that top tier.
- **New product introductions** — About 35 products launched in the last three years with roughly 75 planned over the next five, coming from lifecycle extensions of mega-brands, molecules going off patent (management cites 500-plus loss-of-exclusivity opportunities in branded generics), and adjacent therapy areas.

### The story in depth

Abbott India comes out of the three reviews as a high-return, low-capital branded medicine business that earns its money through doctors rather than directly from patients. Every ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.07 of sales, two-thirds of manufacturing is outsourced, only about three days of cash sits between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and the company keeps ₹47 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs — which turns into ₹27 of operating profit per ₹100 of sales and a 27% cash return on the capital employed. Sales have compounded at roughly 11% a year with unusually little variation from year to year, in end markets that are themselves expanding — a $20bn Indian pharma market growing 10-12%, a very large untreated hypothyroid population, an emerging adult-vaccination category, and 500 molecules losing exclusivity over the next five to ten years. The combined rating of 70 out of 100 ('Strong') is built from 66 on business quality, 82 on multibagger-pattern fit and 64 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound.

The patterns and the strengths are two descriptions of the same engine. The 'Friendly Middleman' pattern is a strong fit precisely because the prescriber, not the patient, makes the choice — which is also why advertising money goes into category-building and doctor education (thyroid awareness with 25 lakh TSH tests a year, Fibroscan diagnostics, epilepsy films, the a:care adherence app) rather than into discounting. 'Brand Strength' is a strong fit for the same reason the mini-monopolies check scores highly: ten mega brands built on a 110-year heritage, with Thyronorm at about 53% share, Vertin above 40%, Digene as the default antacid and Digeraft already leading its new sub-category, stretched into adjacencies under the same names. Pricing power and innovation are rated one notch lower and honestly so — 7.6 of 14.1 points of growth was volume with the rest price, but 26% of revenue sits under government price control with increases capped at wholesale inflation, and the new-product engine (35 launches in three years, 75 planned over five) is largely in-licensed rather than self-discovered. The company explicitly refuses to compete on price, which is why the low-cost pattern is no fit at all and why margin is banked rather than passed on — though margins have swung about ±4.9 points a year, a sign that key costs are not fully within management's control.

The risks are the flip side of exactly these traits, which is why competitive advantage is the one quality area scoring negative (-0.67) against customer benefits at 1.5 and growth and return on capital at 1.33 each. Because the doctor prescribes and the chemist discounts while the patient pays, a cheaper copy can win the sale without ever winning the argument on quality — that is the shared root of both elevated risks. Duphaston went from a 60-year monopoly to one of 42 brands in two years as 41 cheaper dydrogesterone copies arrived, and management now describes gynaecology as a share-of-voice contest against rivals with larger field forces. The same episode is what holds 'Market Share Gainers' and 'Cost to Replicate' to partial rather than strong: the prescriber franchise and category-creation machinery are genuinely hard to rebuild, but Duphaston proved they are not impossible to attack. Brand concentration cuts the same way — the top 10 brands are about 70% of sales and the top 20 about 90%, which is what makes brand strength a strong fit and stakeholder concentration a watch item at the same time, alongside the Novo Nordisk insulin distribution business investors have asked to see separately. Price control is the other double-edged item: Thyronorm's entire strength range sits under it, capping the very pricing power the pattern screen credits. Slower-burning watch items are therapeutic-class obsolescence that management itself names (antacids giving way to PPIs and H2 blockers, phenytoin being succeeded by newer molecules) and demand shifting to online pharmacies and self-medication, which the company is answering with prescription-to-OTC switches and convenience formats. Set against these, cyclicality and fashion risk are both low — this is repeat, chronic, diagnosed-condition demand across around 60 brands, not a trend bet — and the IVF business, where 41 cheaper entrants failed to take share because the stakes are high and relationships are trust-based, shows what the franchise looks like when the buyer genuinely values certainty over price.

## What to watch

- Whether Duphaston's share loss stays contained or the same 'Good Enough Goods' pattern spreads to another mega brand — the top 10 brands are about 70% of sales, so one more repeat would hit hard
- Any widening of price control beyond the current 26% of revenue, especially on Thyronorm, which would cap the 'Pricing Power' pattern further (Government Dependency risk)
- Whether IVF share holds against cheaper entrants — it is the clearest evidence that customers will pay for assurance, and the main answer to the 'Cost to Replicate' limits
- Operating margin stability: swings of about ±4.9 points a year already flagged key costs as outside management's control, and the 27% return on capital depends on them
- Traction of the 75 planned launches and the prescription-to-OTC switches — the test of whether 'Innovation Dominance' and the shift to self-medicating, online-buying patients are being met rather than merely acknowledged
- Management churn at the top, the reason 'Corporate Culture' scores only partial despite plain disclosure of bad news and conservative capital allocation
