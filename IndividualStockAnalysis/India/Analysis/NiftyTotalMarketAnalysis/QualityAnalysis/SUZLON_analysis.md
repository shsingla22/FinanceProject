# Suzlon Energy Ltd. (SUZLON) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

Suzlon Energy Limited designs, manufactures and installs onshore wind turbines in India, and then services them for the life of the project. Its customers are power producers and buyers of wind power — independent power producers like ReNew, Adani and Sembcorp, utilities like Tata Power and Torrent Power, government/PSU tenders such as NTPC, and commercial & industrial (C&I) captive users like Aditya Birla, Bajaj, ONGC and Shree Cement. It makes money mainly by selling and erecting turbines (its S120 and now flagship S144 model, plus new S175/S163 for export), increasingly bundling in the full EPC scope (land, infrastructure, balance of plant), and on top of that earns recurring fees from operating and maintaining a large installed fleet, plus sales of castings and forgings from its in-house foundry unit. It is over 30 years old, is the Indian market leader with roughly a third of cumulative installed wind capacity, runs about 4.5 GW of annual manufacturing capacity (nacelles at Daman, plus blade and tower plants), and while it is overwhelmingly an India business it has had presence in 17 countries and is now re-entering Europe and other export markets.

**The parts of the business:**

- **WTG (Wind Turbine Generator) manufacturing and sales** — Designs and builds wind turbines tailored to Indian wind conditions and delivers/erects them for customers — this is by far the biggest part of the company, at INR 14,040 crores of FY26 revenue out of INR 16,679 crores total, on record deliveries of 2,456 MW, with a contribution margin around 24.5%.
- **EPC / project execution offering** — Taking on the wider turnkey scope around the turbine rather than just supplying machines; management says this has grown from about 20% to 28% of the order book and they intend to push it to 50% by FY28 because it wins more orders and improves competitiveness.
- **OMS (Operations and Maintenance Services)** — Runs and maintains the installed turbine fleet under long-term service contracts — about 15.7 GW under management in India with machine availability consistently above 95%, a steady, recurring, high-margin business (roughly 40.5% EBITDA margin in FY26) that makes Suzlon one of India's largest wind fleet operators.
- **Renom** — A services arm whose assets under management keep growing on steady fleet additions and a healthy order pipeline; mentioned as part of the OMS story but never sized separately in these calls.
- **SE Forge — forging and foundry** — An in-house unit making castings and forged components, sold domestically and increasingly for export; small relative to the group at INR 597 crores of FY26 revenue (up 22%) and INR 119 crores EBITDA (up 61%), but management calls it a growth lever.
- **Project development / land and PPA rights** — Suzlon builds a pipeline of wind sites (management cites a 25 GW-plus development pipeline) and holds developmental rights it can monetize — for example a 2,100 MW project implementation agreement with the Andhra Pradesh government, of which 775 MW has a signed PPA awaiting tariff approval and the remaining 1,325 MW is being monetized over roughly six months.
- **Order book and pipeline** — Not a separate business, but the calls treat it as the core measure of the franchise: the order book grew from 759 MW in Sept 2022 to a record 6.4 GW in Q3 FY26 and stood at 5.9 GW at FY26 close, of which 66% is C&I and PSU customers and over 5.4 GW is the S144 turbine.

### The story in depth

Suzlon's strongest marks all describe the same thing: a company that owns the whole job rather than just the machine. It scores its best on convenience benefits (+2) for an end-to-end model — turbine, engineering and construction work, long-term maintenance, plus a development arm that pre-acquires land and grid connectivity so a customer gets a ready-to-build site — and on distribution advantage (+2) for a service and execution network described as one of India's largest managed wind fleets. That same trait is what produces the screen's only clean pattern hit: recurring revenue on top of an upfront product sale, with the serviced fleet climbing from 13,000 MW in November 2022 to 15.7 GW by June 2026 and carrying about a 40.5% margin, which the risk review names as the main cushion against everything else. It also underpins the partial hits on forward integration (construction work rising from 20% to 28% of the order book against a stated 50% goal by FY28, plus a 25 GW land pipeline) and on cost to replicate (20-plus years of site records, in-house castings, turbines designed for Indian wind conditions). The company entered at the bottom of the cycle (+2) when the industry was at roughly 2.3 GW and it was losing money, built 4.5 GW of capacity and three new blade plants out of its own cash rather than borrowing (+1), and cut debt from about INR13,000 crores to net cash of about INR2,384 crores.

The trouble is that the traits driving the good scores are the same ones driving the worst risks. The bottom-of-cycle timing that earns a +2 is, viewed from the other side, the Cyclicality risk rated HIGH: this is capital equipment sold into someone else's expansion budget, and industry installations moving from 2.3 GW to 6.3 GW can move back. The structural end-market growth that also earns a +2 — India's 100 GW wind target, round-the-clock power rules, repowering — is exactly what makes Government Dependency HIGH RISK, because those targets, the auctions, the tariff rulings and the open-access rules are all set by policy. The low-cost Indian manufacturing behind the partial 'low price plus' fit does not translate into the ability to charge more: pricing power is a clear no-fit, with quarterly turbine margins falling from 16% to 13.7% and roughly 190 basis points of that traced to a lower average selling price, and management conceding it does not price differently across customers. The same softness shows up in the industry-structure score of -1, where margins swing by nearly 18 points, and in three linked WATCH risks — new entrants, 'good enough' product, and technology — all pointing at Chinese rivals offering 5 MW platforms with 8-10% better cost-of-energy while Suzlon is still at prototype stage on that size. Scale is growing fast (deliveries up 58% to 2,456 MW, order book from 3.8 GW to 6.4 GW) but disclosed market share is flat, 33% in 2022 against 32% in 2024, so the company is growing with the market rather than taking from it. Behind that sits the weakest check of all: sales have averaged roughly -2% a year and arrive lumpily (-2), and about 117 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers (-1), with receivables build-up blamed mainly on public-sector contracts — which is also why customer concentration is rated ELEVATED, one large buyer's absence being enough to move a quarter's margin.

Put together, this is a genuine operating turnaround inside an industry that does not reward its winners with durable pricing, which is why the combined rating lands at 40 out of 100, 'Mixed', with resilience marked STRAINED. Competitive advantage is the one area scoring clearly well (1.5), customer benefits next (1.0), while growth, capital allocation, return on capital and management all sit at or near zero and industry structure is slightly negative. The pattern screen tells the same story in a different language: eleven patterns produce one likely fit, seven partials and three outright no-fits, meaning almost every attractive feature is real but incomplete — innovation happens but R&D spending is never disclosed, the brand has 30 years of heritage and one-in-three installed turbines but does not command a price premium, the culture has delivered on stated promises but the numbers behind them stay cyclical. And the story is not a secret: the 'security by obscurity' check scores -2 because this is a widely followed listed turnaround with analysts on every call, so there is no hidden-gem discount to collect. What is left to be paid for is execution — converting the maintenance annuity, the construction and land businesses and the record order book into steadier revenue, better working capital and defended share against a cheaper, larger foreign platform.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.1× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 2 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Suzlon sells capital equipment into its customers' capacity-expansion capex, so volumes and margins swing violently — industry installs moved 2.3GW (FY23) to 6.3GW (FY26), the company itself went from negative EBITDA and INR13,000cr debt in FY20 through restructuring to record FY26 profits, and WTG contribution margin bounces (23% in Q1FY25, 13.7% in Q3FY26) on customer mix and project readiness; management repeatedly concedes quarterly output depends on which customer projects are scheduled.

> "It could be 100% and it could be as low as 50% to 60%. It would vary from quarter-to-quarter depending upon which projects are scheduled to be delivered during that quarter." — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/operations-linked OMS business of 15.7GW under management with ~40.5% EBITDA margin and recurring service annuity provides a non-capex, stable earnings base*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 5 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from -29% to 20% (swinging ±17.6 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 3% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 9%).

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Fortunes are set by policy at nearly every level across all sixteen calls: the 500GW/140GW wind targets, wind RPO, SECI/GUVNL/MPUVNL reverse auctions, PSU tenders (NTPC), APERC tariff fixation, green open-access rules, repowering policy, CERC's DSM tightening, and an AP project implementation agreement that was denied for years 'because of government change' and only revived in 2026; management even notes central bidding 'has taken a pause' pending PPA resolution.

> "it was denied by the government for some time because of government change and now after new government has come in, we're glad to inform you that in April, they've extended the PIA by another 2 years" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Order-book diversification toward C&I/captive (51-66%), which reduces direct auction dependence, plus emerging export markets (Europe, Australia, Middle East)*
