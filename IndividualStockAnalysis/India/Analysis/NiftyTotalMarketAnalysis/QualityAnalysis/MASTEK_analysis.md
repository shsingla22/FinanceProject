# Mastek (MASTEK) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Mastek is an Indian-listed IT services company that gets paid by large organisations to build, modernise and run their business software — things like case-management systems for government departments, claims platforms for health insurers, cloud finance and budgeting systems, data/analytics platforms and, more recently, AI projects. It earns money mainly by selling people and projects: fixed-price and time-based transformation work implementing packaged platforms (Oracle Cloud, Salesforce, Microsoft, AWS, Snowflake), custom 'digital engineering' build work, plus longer-running annuity-style managed services contracts that renew — management stresses it grows by mining a small set of big accounts (top 30 clients were about 57% of revenue in FY25) rather than adding clients. Its biggest market by far is the UK, where the central and local government public sector and the NHS are described as the 'backbone' of growth, followed by North America and a smaller AMEA (Asia-Pacific and Middle East) region; delivery is largely from India. Scale: FY26 revenue of about $421 million (INR 3,699 crore) with an operating EBITDA margin near 15.8%, a 12-month order backlog of roughly $300 million, and quarterly revenue of about $105 million in the June 2026 quarter.

**The parts of the business:**

- **U.K. & Europe** — The company's largest and most established geography and 'strong foundation', growing about 11% in pound terms in FY26, and the home of both its public sector and its fastest-growing healthcare work.
- **U.K. public sector / secure government services** — Long-running contracts with central and local UK government — including immigration and law-enforcement case-working platforms, the Home Office, HMRC and framework panels like the Digital Specialists and Programs framework — described as the backbone of Mastek's growth and a source of stable, renewable, predictable revenue.
- **Healthcare & Life Sciences** — Called one of its most important sectors, spanning NHS programmes in the UK (which management says grew roughly 24% in dollar terms in FY26), US health plans and providers, senior-living operators and Oracle Health-based work in AMEA, covering claims modernisation, cloud finance and data modernisation.
- **North America** — A smaller region that went through a full leadership and strategy 'reset' in FY26 after delivery and deal-delay problems, now focused on Healthcare & Life Sciences, BFSI and Retail, and returning to order-book growth including a $25 million Salesforce Agentforce AI transformation deal.
- **AMEA (Asia-Pacific and Middle East)** — The smallest region, deliberately pruned from about 250 accounts to 175 to focus on bigger clients, centred on healthcare via Oracle Health, and currently hurt by Middle East geopolitical turbulence that is causing idle-staff costs and slow collections.
- **Financial services / BFSI** — A vertical the calls describe as the 'star' of the March 2026 quarter, with large new contracts signed with two North American financial institutions plus UK and AMEA clients that are now converting into revenue growth.
- **Retail and manufacturing** — A steady, smaller vertical covering work such as digital engineering pods and a customer loyalty programme for a US recreational-vehicle maker, and cloud/managed services for a European battery manufacturer.
- **Oracle Cloud transformation** — Implementing and modernising Oracle cloud applications — budgeting, forecasting and health systems — packaged with an in-house accelerator framework called Glide, and a key driver of the US healthcare push.
- **Salesforce practice (MST Solutions)** — A US Salesforce consulting and integration business acquired in July 2022 (about 250 staff in India and 75 in the US) covering sales, marketing, service and integration, which now underpins deals like claims platforms and the $25 million Agentforce programme.
- **Data, analytics and AI** — A named strategic priority built partly on the BizAnalytica acquisition, selling data platforms and data-modernisation work plus AI programmes — over 27 new AI programmes in the March 2026 quarter and 40-plus AI-led wins in the June 2026 quarter — with partnerships cited with NVIDIA, Microsoft and AWS.
- **Digital engineering** — Custom application build and modernisation work — dedicated developer 'pods', platform integration and large multi-system transformation programmes such as consolidating 40-plus systems for the University of Nottingham.
- **Managed services / Cloud Enhancement Services** — The annuity part of the business, where completed implementations are converted into ongoing support and enhancement contracts; management flags growing this alongside fixed-price work to make revenue more predictable.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

## The verdict: Mixed — 46 out of 100 ★★☆☆☆

In one breath: the quality framework finds an average business today (54/100); no winning pattern is strongly confirmed yet; the risk review found 1 high risk (Government Dependency). Weighing those together gives 46 out of 100 — mixed.

### The story in depth

Mastek comes out of the three reviews as a genuinely mixed case: 46 out of 100, or two stars, built from 54 for business quality, 33 for fit with the multibagger patterns and 48 for risk safety, with the underlying business judged SOUND rather than fragile. The quality scoring splits sharply by area. Management is the clear strength (0.67), driven by unusually plain-spoken disclosure — the team calls its own EBITDA result a disappointment, walks back through prior-quarter problem items one by one, and volunteered both the labour-code true-up and the INR 91 crore transfer without being asked. That candour sits alongside real cash discipline: 16 rupees of operating cash for every 100 rupees of capital employed, operating cash up 35 percent, debt repaid, and a rising dividend of INR 16 final plus INR 8 interim. But almost everything about the industry around it scores zero — competitive advantage, growth, industry structure and capital allocation all came out blank, and the industry-structure check on oligopoly scored the worst mark on the sheet (-2) because IT services here is fragmented and the buyers, not the sellers, set the terms.

The pattern screen tells the same story from the other side, and it is where the strengths and risks turn out to be the same fact wearing two hats. Only one of eleven patterns is a likely fit — Market Share Gainers, on the strength of a steadily better client roster (Fortune 1000 clients going 19 to 21 to 36) and UK public-sector framework wins where the company says it replaced a competitor. Seven patterns are partial and three are outright misses. The most durable thing the screen found is the Cost to Replicate position in UK central government: Home Office, HMRC, NHS England, the DSP framework shortlisting, multi-year renewals, security clearances, and roughly 20 percent of UK operating profit. That same position is also the single highest-rated danger in the risk review — Government Dependency, marked HIGH RISK, with management openly tracking elections and the Labour manifesto. And it feeds a second risk too: results keep hinging on individual named relationships, with the recent top-five client decline of 4 percent quarter-on-quarter and 12 percent year-on-year traced to an NHS England project closing out, and the top 30 accounts making up about 57 percent of revenue. The one moat, the one pattern that fits, and the two biggest risks are all the same UK government franchise.

The second place where a strength doubles as a threat is pricing and technology. On the plus side, cost discipline is real — Project Nucleus on order-to-cash, AI applied to delivery, 12 percent more revenue per person, and EBITDA held around 15.8 to 16.1 percent through a price war — which is why the Low Price Plus and Innovation Dominance patterns each score partial rather than nil. On the minus side, that same AI shift is what the risk review flags as ELEVATED under Technological Innovation, with analysts asking directly whether billing by the hour survives and management conceding the model must move to outcome-based pricing, and as a WATCH under New Entrants, where flat margins are attributed to AI-led pricing pressure. Meanwhile the discounting picture is unambiguous: renewals cut by around 15 percent and in cases 20 to 25 percent, peers reportedly going to 50 percent, clients asking for budget reductions. That single fact sinks three patterns at once — Pricing Power, Brand Strength and Toll Roads all read NO FIT — and pulls down the checks on intangible benefits, barriers to entry and pricing-led growth, while showing up in margin as a slide from 19.2 percent in FY23 to 15.4 percent in Q1 FY27. Against that, the forward signals are genuinely better: a 12-month backlog of $310 million, up 13.3 percent in dollars and 25 percent in rupees, three straight quarters of North America order-book growth and a $25 million AI deal. The catch is that North America has been promised since 2022 and reset repeatedly, which is why the geography check scores -1 and why FY26 growth was only 3.1 percent in dollars. The honest reading is a well-run, honestly-reported, cash-generating business with one real but concentrated franchise, no pricing power, and a recovery that is visible in the order book but not yet in the revenue line.
