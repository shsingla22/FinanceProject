# Fujiyama Power Syste (UTLSOLAR) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Fujiyama Power Systems (brands: Fujiyama Solar and UTL Solar) is an Indian power-electronics and energy-solutions company that sells complete rooftop solar systems rather than just panels — a system being a solar panel, a solar inverter and a battery sold together. Its customers are overwhelmingly ordinary households (B2C was over 90% of H1 FY26 revenue), especially in Tier 2 and Tier 3 towns where the grid is unreliable, so systems are bought mainly as off-grid/hybrid backup power rather than for government subsidy; it added over 400,000 households in FY26 counting inverters sold. It makes money by manufacturing the components in-house and selling them through a large network of distributors, dealers and exclusive 'Shoppe' franchise outlets (over 8,900 channel partners as of March 2026), with margins helped by backward integration into its own cells, panels and batteries. It operates in India, with plants at Dadri (Uttar Pradesh), Ratlam (Madhya Pradesh) and Bawal, and posted FY26 revenue of Rs. 26,545 million, up 72.3%, with an 18.5% EBITDA margin and Rs. 3,041 million profit.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Solar panels (modules)** — In-house manufacturing and sale of solar panels — the largest single product line, roughly Rs. 2,980 million of the Rs. 5,885 million Q3 FY26 revenue, with capacity grown from 439 MW to 1,639 MW over the year plus a new 2,000 MW plant commissioned at Ratlam.
- **Power electronics (solar inverters)** — Manufacture and sale of solar inverters and related power electronics, about Rs. 1,600 million of Q3 FY26 revenue, with capacity expanded from 1,143 MW to 2,180 MW during FY26; inverters sold are also how the company counts households added.
- **Batteries (lead-acid and lithium-ion)** — Battery manufacturing for the backup systems, about Rs. 1,000 million of Q3 FY26 revenue; lithium capacity rose from 45 MWh to 545 MWh in FY26, while the 1.3 GW lead-acid line at Bawal is suspended after a fire and supply is being covered by third-party partners.
- **Solar cells (backward integration)** — A 1,000 MW Mono PERC solar cell plant at Dadri, built for about Rs. 300 crores and commissioned in Q3 FY26, whose output is used entirely in-house to cut reliance on imported cells and to qualify for subsidy-based (DCR) government rooftop business; a further 1,200 MW TOPCon cell facility is planned at Ratlam.
- **Distribution network and Shoppe outlets** — The route to market — distributors, dealers and exclusive Shoppe franchise stores that handle system sizing, installation and financing, backed by field service engineers; the network crossed 8,900 partners by March 2026 and management calls it a hard-to-copy differentiator.
- **B2C versus subsidy/on-grid business** — The core is direct-to-consumer off-grid and hybrid backup systems (over 90% of H1 FY26 revenue), with on-grid subsidy work under the PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana a newer and growing addition that picked up in Q4 FY26 once in-house cells made the company eligible.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Fujiyama Power Systems sells rooftop and backup solar systems to Indian households, and almost everything good about it traces back to one thing: the way it reaches those households. It has 8,900-plus channel partners, exclusive Shoppe outlets that handle sizing, installation and financing, 500 SKUs held in stock with a 24-hour dispatch commitment, and 600-plus field service engineers. The quality framework gives its top marks to exactly this cluster — distribution advantage, convenience and assurance benefits — and notes that customers come back because of on-time service and trust rather than price. That network is being pointed at a market in genuine secular expansion: India's 300GW-by-2030 target, roughly 25GW of untapped PM Surya Ghar opportunity, worsening grid reliability and a projected 40-45% annual growth rate for rooftop solar. The company grew revenue 72.3% and added over 400,000 households in the year, comfortably faster than that industry rate, with management saying half its geography is still untapped. The combined rating lands at 51 out of 100 — 'Decent', three stars — built from 62 for business quality, 38 for multibagger fit and 48 for risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound.

The same channel that is the biggest strength is also what shapes, and limits, the investment patterns. It is the reason 'Friendly Middleman' is a strong fit — dealers and franchisees sell and install, service engineers train them, and the company deliberately hands the annual maintenance margin to the channel to keep partners loyal. It is also part of why 'Cost to Replicate' and 'Market Share Gainers' only partially fit: thirty years of Tier 2/3 town relationships is a real time-based barrier, but management itself concedes the field is open to well-funded entrants, gives no explicit share figures, and the Chairman candidly credited past growth to the market expanding rather than to share taken. And the very decision that buys dealer loyalty is what rules out 'Recurring Revenue' entirely — management explicitly disclaims service and maintenance income, and working capital is tied up in inventory (108 days) rather than funded by customers. The brand fits the same way: real trust and word-of-mouth from a 30-year history and roughly a million customers, but no price premium, which is consistent with 'Pricing Power' being no fit at all — management says input cost moves are simply passed through and confirms the whole 70% revenue increase was volume, with no price improvement. Margin gains came from backward integration and cost discipline instead, which is why 'Low Price Plus' is the more accurate description. Alongside this, corporate culture rates as a strong fit for an unglamorous but valuable reason: things promised in December were delivered by February, and bad news — the Bawal fire, BIS seizures on 10-15 SKUs, the Ratlam delays — is disclosed promptly and specifically.

The risks are largely the flip side of the growth engine rather than separate problems. To keep supplying its own systems and to meet local-content rules, the company has been spending heavily: roughly Rs 300 crore on a 1GW cell line at Dadri delivered in six months under a Rs 400 crore budget, Rs 272 crore at Ratlam, and a further Rs 350 crore planned for a TOPCon line. The framework scores that capex positively as growth spending, but it is the direct cause of the two high risks. On technology, the Dadri line was built on Mono-PERC just as, in an analyst's words, everyone had shifted to TOPCon, and the later Rs 350 crore TOPCon announcement confirms the newly commissioned asset is already behind the curve — cushioned only by the facts that cells are a small input, the line was cheap and fast, and it can be converted. On policy, government decisions moved both the profit line and the capex plan across all three calls: a GST cut from 12% to 5% froze about a month of sales and drove a sequential revenue decline, while DCR and ALMM rules made the TOPCon spend mandatory. This spending also shows up in the numbers the framework marks down hardest: total assets more than doubled to Rs 23,436 million against 72% revenue growth, so the asset-light profile is deteriorating, and the headline return on capital is negative — minus Rs 6 of operating cash for every Rs 100 of capital employed, with the return-on-capital area scoring zero. Two further watch-level risks sit close to the strengths already described: new entrants such as Tata Power, which management answered with market-size optimism rather than a defended position, and 'good enough goods', where a dealer-specified sale to price-sensitive buyers of an increasingly commoditised product is held together mainly by the service economics deliberately given to that dealer. The comfort is that demand is functional and repeat-driven — backup power and lower bills, not fashion — spread across a million households with no single customer dependence, which keeps the overall resilience judgment sound.

## What to watch

- Cash return on capital — the 'Return on capital' check is at minus 6%; whether the Dadri, Ratlam and TOPCon lines start converting the 72% revenue growth into positive operating cash is the single biggest swing factor in the rating.
- Asset turn — currently Rs 1.68 of sales per Rs 1 of assets but deteriorating as assets more than doubled to Rs 23,436 million; further decline would undercut the asset-light case.
- Technological Innovation (high risk) — whether the Mono-PERC line at Dadri is actually converted to TOPCon, or the Rs 350 crore new line simply strands the earlier Rs 300 crore spend.
- Government Dependency (high risk) — the next GST, DCR or ALMM change; the last one froze roughly a month of sales, and any further mandatory capex would repeat the pattern.
- New Entrants and Good Enough Goods (both watch) — the first sign of discounting from larger entrants, or of dealers switching away, would test whether the 8,900-partner channel behind the 'Friendly Middleman' fit is a real barrier or a rented one.
