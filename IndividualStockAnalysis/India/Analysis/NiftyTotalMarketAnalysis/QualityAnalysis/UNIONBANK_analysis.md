# Union Bank of India (UNIONBANK) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

Union Bank of India is an Indian public-sector commercial bank, listed in Mumbai (BSE code 532477, NSE symbol UNIONBANK), that takes deposits from the public and lends the money out. It gathers current and savings accounts (CASA) and term deposits from households and businesses through a very large branch network (about 8,700 branches on the 2022 call, reaching places as remote as Lakshadweep and rural/semi-urban 'RUSU' locations), and lends to retail customers, farmers, small businesses and large companies. It earns mainly the spread between lending and deposit rates — net interest income of roughly Rs. 7,600 crore a quarter in 2022, with interest income around Rs. 1.06 lakh crore for the year to March 2026 and a net interest margin of about 3% — plus fee income from services such as corporate/NACH mandate registrations and income from its treasury (investment) book. Total business was about Rs. 21.4 trillion in mid-2024 and Rs. 23.85 lakh crore by March 2026, with net profit of Rs. 18,697 crore for FY26 and a dividend of Rs. 5 per share.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Deposits (CASA and term deposits)** — The bank's funding business — current and savings accounts plus retail and bulk term deposits; deposits were about Rs. 12.24 trillion in June 2024, CASA plus retail term deposits are kept at roughly 72% of total deposits, and management has been deliberately shedding bulk deposits (Rs. 18,000-20,000 crore) in favour of stickier CASA and retail term money.
- **RAM advances (Retail, Agriculture and MSME)** — Loans to individuals (home, vehicle, gold and education loans), farmers and small/medium businesses — described as the growth engine, rising from about 55% of total advances in 2022 to roughly 57.5% of domestic advances by March 2026, and pushed through dedicated retail loan points (215 opened by 2022), 125 MSME loan points and about 150 MSME-focused branches.
- **Large corporate advances** — Big-ticket lending to large companies and sectors such as roads/HAM projects, commercial real estate, pharma and chemicals, run through large corporate branches; it makes up the remaining roughly 43-45% of the loan book and grew more slowly than RAM (7.8% in mid-2024) as low-yielding exposures were deliberately run off.
- **Treasury / investments** — The bank's securities and investment book, which produces trading and investment income that swings with interest rates (Rs. 1,186 crore in the March 2022 quarter falling to Rs. 220 crore in the June 2022 quarter); by FY26 about Rs. 25,000 crore was shifted out of the treasury book into lending.
- **Recovery / stressed assets (SAMV)** — A dedicated function that chases bad loans through NCLT insolvency cases, the NARCL bad bank, DRTs, one-time settlements and sale of collateral — a big value driver given gross NPAs fell from 10.22% (June 2022) to 2.82% (March 2026), with gross recoveries of about Rs. 3,368 crore in a single quarter.
- **Ecosystem banking vertical** — A newer relationship-management arm of about 1,200 staff under a Chief General Manager across 118 centres and 12 hubs, set up to source business (especially CASA and public-sector/corporate relationships) separately from branch operations, and credited with integrating 200+ corporates a quarter and sustaining CASA balances.
- **Fee-based / transaction banking** — Income from services around customer accounts and mandates — for example NACH registrations rising from 12,000-13,000 to 18,000 a day — which management points to as the source of fee income alongside high customer 'stickiness' of 95-99% in advances, savings and current accounts.
- **Capital and funding instruments** — The bank raises capital and wholesale funds beyond deposits — a Rs. 10,000 crore FY25 plan (Rs. 6,000 crore equity plus Rs. 4,000 crore AT1/Tier 2 bonds), and in FY26 about Rs. 18,000 crore of refinance and Rs. 3,000 crore of infrastructure bonds — keeping capital adequacy at 18.10% and CET1 at 15.69% as of March 2026.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

## The verdict: Mixed — 43 out of 100 ★★☆☆☆

### The story in depth

Union Bank of India comes across as a large, competently run public-sector lender that has fixed its past problems but has not built anything that would let it charge more or grow faster than the industry. The clean-up is real and is the single most impressive thing in the record: gross bad loans fell from 13.60% in June 2021 and 10.22% in June 2022 to 2.82% by March 2026, provision cover reached 93.5%, and quarterly profit rose from Rs.1,558 crore to over Rs.5,300 crore. Management gets credit for how it did this — the quality framework scores it positively on disciplined stewardship (no acquisitions, capital adequacy of 18.46% with CET1 at 15.69%, the deliberate shedding of about Rs.70,000 crore of bulk deposits, and a voluntary Rs.700 crore counter-cyclical provision) and on being low-profile and technocratic, with earnings calls full of ratios and process detail rather than personality. The multibagger screen echoes this in the two patterns it partly recognises: 'Corporate Culture' (explicit guidance-versus-actual scorecards, prompt disclosure of frauds and one-off provisions, conservative buffering) and 'Low Price Plus' (cost of deposits down 18 basis points, cost-to-income down roughly 500 basis points on the back of the bulk-deposit churn). Even so, both are only PARTIAL, because the same record that shows the repair also shows how bad the starting point was.

The weakness is that none of this repair work has turned into an advantage over competitors, and the screen and the framework say so in almost identical words. Five of the eleven patterns come back NO FIT — pricing power, brand strength, market-share gains, cost to replicate, recurring revenue — and they line up one-for-one with the framework's negative checks. There is no pricing power because loan yields reset automatically with the RBI's repo rate; margin fell from 3.05% in July 2024 to 2.70% and then 2.64%, and management's stated ambition is only to 'defend' margin, never to expand it, which is exactly why the framework scores 'Pricing, Mix and Volume' and 'Intangible benefits' at -1. There is no brand or mini-monopoly because the products are commodities: management itself says MSME customers rotate between banks purely for interest-rate arbitrage, which is why the framework's harshest single score (-2 on mini-monopolies) and the risk review's 'Good Enough Goods' finding are the same observation seen from two angles. And there is no share gain: deposits grew 8.5% against system growth of 10.6% and advances 11.5% against 13.9%, with FY26 deposits up just 2.72%, while management frames the goal as keeping share 'at the existing level'. The framework's area scores tell the same story bluntly — Return on Capital at -1.0, Industry Structure at -0.83, Competitive Advantage at 0.0 — leaving Capital Allocation at 0.5 as the only genuinely positive area. Growth itself is not the problem; Indian bank credit is a secularly expanding market growing 12-14%, and the bank is investing to serve it through 215 retail loan points, MSME loan points, 12 premium branches and a 1,200-person ecosystem-banking team. The problem is that every one of those levers is buyable by a competitor, which is precisely why the 'Cost to Replicate' pattern fails.

The risks are mostly the strengths viewed from the other side. Earnings are rated HIGH RISK for cyclicality because the profit growth has come from the same falling-bad-loan cycle that produced the turnaround story — a repair that cannot repeat once bad loans are already at 2.82%. Government ownership is the second HIGH RISK, and it is the source of both the low-profile discipline the framework praises and the framework's -1 on independent, long-term leadership: externally appointed, short-tenure management (the MD changed from Manimekhalai to Asheesh Pandey mid-period), a share sale sized to bring the government stake down to at least 75%, loans repriced off the repo on a fixed monthly reset, and priority-sector obligations. The remaining four WATCH risks each pair with a defence the bank has already built. Single large corporate accounts still swing quarterly results — Future Retail provisioning, Rs.658 crore of the March 2026 recovery line from the Sterling Biotech settlement, resolution timing on roughly Rs.10,000 crore of accounts — offset by roughly 8,700 branches and retail/agri/MSME at 57.5% of advances. Savers drifting out of low-cost deposits pushed the CASA ratio from 36.19% in June 2022 to 32.51% by September 2025, offset by a deliberate rebuild back to 35.21% and Rs.1,10,000 crore of low-cost and retail term deposits added in six months. Deposit competition from all quarters, with the bank conceding it lags peers on business growth, is offset by new channels including 118 ecosystem-banking centres. Technology shows up mainly as fraud exposure — 70-80% of reported frauds are digital — offset by a 24/7 cyber security centre, AI transaction monitoring and corporate connections claiming 95-99% stickiness. Only fashion risk is genuinely LOW, because demand for deposits and loans is functional and repeat. Netting it out, the combined rating of 43 out of 100 (64% weight on business quality at 45, 36% on risk safety at 40) and a Mixed, two-star grade reads as fair: a well-repaired balance sheet with honest, disciplined management, attached to a commodity franchise that is slowly losing share and whose pricing is set by someone else.
