# CRISIL Ltd. (CRISIL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

Crisil sells credit ratings, research, benchmarking data, analytics and consulting — essentially independent opinions and data that help others judge credit risk and make business decisions, under the stated purpose of "making markets function better." Its customers are banks, NBFCs, asset managers, global corporate and investment banks, corporates, governments and multilateral institutions; by April 2026 it says it serves 11,400-plus clients across 40 countries with 5,000-plus professionals, having started in India as the country's first credit rating agency roughly 40 years ago. It earns money mainly from fees: fees for assigning new ratings on bonds and bank loans plus recurring annual surveillance fees on existing ratings, and subscription/mandate-based fees for research, benchmarking, risk and consulting work — including analytical work delegated to it by S&P Global Ratings. It is headquartered in Mumbai and listed in India, but the business is heavily international: management said in 2023 that about 65% of revenue was overseas business billed in US dollars, and the company has since added geographies such as Canada (via the PriceMetrix acquisition) and Australia (Peter Lee Associates).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Rating Services (reporting segment)** — One of the company's two reported segments, combining Crisil Ratings and the Global Analytical Center; management describes Crisil Ratings as holding a market-leading position in Indian corporate bond ratings, and the segment grew about 20.2% year-on-year in Q1 2026.
- **Crisil Ratings Limited** — Assigns credit ratings on Indian corporate bonds and bank loans and then monitors them for a recurring surveillance fee — revenue rises and falls with bond issuance volumes and bank credit growth, and calendar 2025 growth came from strong surveillance fees plus momentum in the mid-corporate segment.
- **Global Analytical Center (GAC)** — An offshore analytical unit inside the ratings segment that does ratings work delegated by S&P Global Ratings — mostly surveillance of existing ratings plus newer support engagements — and management flags it as a consistent growth contributor.
- **Research, Analytics and Solutions (reporting segment)** — The second reported segment, housing the global and India non-ratings businesses (Integral IQ, Coalition Greenwich and Crisil Intelligence); it is the part most exposed to global financial clients' discretionary budgets, and it was the main drag when overall revenue growth slowed to 3.8% in FY2024.
- **Crisil Integral IQ (formerly Global Research and Risk Solutions)** — Outsourced research and risk work for global banks and asset managers — credit monitoring, model validation, risk and regulatory support — with recent momentum in buy-side (asset manager) offerings and Gen-AI-based client solutions such as a deployed Gen-AI credit assessment tool.
- **Crisil Coalition Greenwich (formerly Global Benchmarking Analytics)** — Sells benchmarking analytics that let banks and asset managers compare their revenue, market share and client experience against peers, serving corporate and investment banks and commercial/community banks; it has been built out by acquisitions including Peter Lee Associates in Australia and PriceMetrix in Canada for wealth management.
- **Crisil Intelligence (formerly Market Intelligence and Analytics)** — The India-facing research, consulting and data arm — industry research, infrastructure and sustainability consulting, and credit/risk products such as the Credit+ ICON credit assessment solution — and it also produces the public economic forecasts and reports (India GDP, inflation, bank credit) that Crisil is quoted on.
- **Crisil Foundation** — The company's non-commercial CSR arm running financial-literacy and environment programmes such as Mein Pragati (over a million people reached in Assam and Rajasthan) and 669 RBI Centres for Financial Literacy; it is a reputation and community activity, not a revenue line.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

CRISIL comes out of all three reviews as the same business seen from three angles: a regulator-licensed gatekeeper that collects a small fee on a step other people cannot skip. The quality framework gives full marks to its mini-monopoly position in Indian corporate ratings, to the stable few-player industry structure, and to barriers to entry built on SEBI registration and a decade-long published default-rate record. The multibagger screen picks up the same facts and calls them a Toll Road and a Cost-to-Replicate franchise — a newcomer with money still cannot buy a 40-year history, a track record that can only be earned in real time, the S&P Global delegation relationship, or India-wide proprietary data. The financial evidence lines up: about 10% average annual sales growth with little year-to-year wobble, ₹30 of operating profit on every ₹100 of sales, and margins that move only about 1.6 points either way. Steady margins are read the same way in both places — as proof that nobody new is undercutting them. The combined rating of 80 out of 100 (Outstanding) rests mostly on the multibagger fit, which scored a perfect 100, with business quality at 78 and risk safety the weakest leg at 58.

The strengths and the risks are, in several cases, literally the same fact. The strongest and most repeated one is the S&P Global relationship: the delegation work done through the Global Analytical Center is part of what makes the franchise impossible to copy, and it is also the single highest-rated risk in the review, because S&P Global is at once the controlling shareholder and one counterparty — with that delegation named as a growth driver in every call and the ratings segment growing 32.5% and 20.2% in the two first quarters cited. Regulation cuts the same way: SEBI licensing is the moat that creates the toll road, and it is also why the business is exposed to government action, as when the RBI's risk-weight increase pushed bank credit growth down from 16.6% to 11% and the April 2025 rollback was credited for the expected recovery. The surveillance fee is a third example. It is the reason the Recurring Revenue pattern is a strong fit — an upfront rating fee followed by a perpetual annual fee on ratings already outstanding — and it is also the main cushion against the cyclicality risk, visible in the ratings segment growing 20.2% in a quarter when bond issuance fell 12%. That same decoupling is the only real evidence for the Pricing Power pattern, which stays partial because the company's disclosure policy bars segment-level pricing detail.

Where the business is softer, the three reviews again agree. The research and analytics side is bought by financial institutions that are cutting costs and actively looking for cheaper offshoring — that is the Good Enough Goods risk, it is why the Low Price Plus pattern is a clear no-fit (positioning is premium, and client cost pressure is described as a headwind rather than an opportunity), and it is where the damage already showed once, when FY2024 revenue growth slowed to 3.8%. Layered on top is the technology risk, which management itself raised to the front of the list, and which matters most in exactly that business, where the product is analysis produced by people. The offsetting evidence is management behaviour: the quality framework credits them with continuing to invest in Gen-AI and talent straight through the weak 2024 stretch while holding margins, and the risk review credits a steady drumbeat of named AI products. Several patterns sit at partial — market share gains, innovation dominance, pricing power — for the same underlying reason, which is that the company declines to disclose segment-level revenue, R&D spend, or pricing; this is a limit on what can be proven, not evidence against. Overall resilience is judged sound, with the two mildest risk channels (taste-driven demand and shifting customer preferences) rated low because demand is mandated and repeated, and because the shifts under way — passive, private markets, wealth management, the financialisation of Indian savings with mutual fund assets rising from ₹55 lakh crore to ₹83 lakh crore — are ones CRISIL has moved toward rather than been hurt by.

## What to watch

- S&P Global delegation volumes (Stakeholder Concentration, high risk): the ratings segment's 32.5% and 20.2% growth leans on work from a party that is also the controlling shareholder — any slowdown there hits growth and independence at once.
- Whether ratings revenue keeps growing when issuance falls (Pricing Power and Recurring Revenue patterns vs the Cyclicality risk): if segment growth starts tracking bond volumes down again, the annuity argument weakens.
- Research and analytics revenue under client cost-cutting (Good Enough Goods risk, reinforced by the Low Price Plus no-fit): a repeat of the FY2024 slowdown to 3.8% growth would show premium positioning failing where offshoring is bought on price.
- Gen-AI moving from a product line to a substitute (Technological Innovation, elevated): watch whether the named AI launches keep adding revenue, or whether clients begin doing the analysis themselves.
- Regulatory moves on credit and licensing (Government Dependency risk, tied to the Barriers to Entry and Oligopoly checks): another action like the NBFC risk-weight hike would show that the rule that protects the moat also sets the volume.
