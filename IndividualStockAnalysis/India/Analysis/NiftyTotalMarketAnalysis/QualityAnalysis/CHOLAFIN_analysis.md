# Cholamandalam Investment and Finance Company Ltd. (CHOLAFIN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

Cholamandalam Investment and Finance Company Limited (CHOLAFIN) is an Indian non-banking finance company (NBFC) that lends money to retail and small-business borrowers across India, with most loans backed by collateral such as a vehicle or property. Its biggest business is financing the purchase of vehicles — commercial vehicles, passenger vehicles, two-wheelers, tractors and construction equipment, both new and used — mainly for retail customers and small operators rather than the large fleet owners that banks typically fund. Alongside that it lends against property, gives home loans, funds small and medium enterprises, and offers consumer, personal and gold loans. It earns money on the spread between the interest it charges borrowers and its own cost of funds, so its profits depend on loan growth (assets under management), that net interest margin, and how much it loses to bad loans; management tracks AUM (INR 2,42,630 crores as of March 2026, growing about 21% a year), net interest margin, credit cost (~1.5-1.6%) and returns (ROA ~3-4%, ROE ~19-23%).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Vehicle Finance** — Loans to buy new and used commercial vehicles, passenger vehicles, two-wheelers, tractors and construction equipment, aimed at retail customers and smaller operators; it is by far the largest business, with 'Auto' AUM of INR 1,19,558 crores in March 2026 (roughly half the total book) and INR 14,390 crores of disbursements in a single quarter as far back as Q3 FY25.
- **Loan Against Property (LAP), including Affordable LAP** — Loans given against a borrower's property as collateral, largely to small businesses and self-employed customers; it is the second-largest business with AUM of INR 52,295 crores in March 2026, growing 26% year-on-year, and management describes it as a low-loss book (steady-state credit cost around 0.5%) where recoveries come through SARFAESI property auctions.
- **Home Loans** — Mortgage loans for buying or building homes, including self-occupied residential property; a mid-sized, steadily growing business — disbursements went from INR 478 crores a quarter in mid-2022 to INR 1,820 crores in Q3 FY25, with AUM up 23% year-on-year in Q4 FY26.
- **SME (small and medium enterprise loans)** — Business loans to small and medium companies — term loans, equipment finance and (until management deliberately slowed it) supply-chain finance; a smaller book at INR 9,338 crores AUM in March 2026, but growing fast at 41% year-on-year, and behaving much like LAP because loans are collateral-backed.
- **CSEL (Consumer and Small Enterprise Loans)** — Mostly unsecured personal and small-business loans, launched as a 'new business' in 2022; it grew quickly (INR 3,149 crores disbursed in Q3 FY25) but ran higher losses through fintech/partnership tie-ups, so management wound that channel down — from 10 partners to 3 and then a full exit from Q1 FY26 — leaving CSEL AUM up only 4% in Q4 FY26.
- **SBPL (Secured Business and Personal Loans)** — Small collateral-backed business and personal loans, also started as a new business in 2022; still the smallest of the lending lines at INR 3,537 crores AUM in March 2026, but the fastest-growing, up 46-52% year-on-year.
- **Gold Loans** — The newest business, lending against gold as security out of 118 dedicated branches in south and east India; still small but ramping quickly — INR 772 crores disbursed in Q3 FY26 and INR 1,130 crores in Q4 FY26.
- **MSME segment (grouping: LAP + SME + SBPL)** — Management's umbrella label for its property- and business-backed small-enterprise lending, distributed through branches; combined AUM grew 29% year-on-year in Q4 FY26.
- **Consumer segment (grouping: Home Loans + CSEL + Gold Loans)** — Management's umbrella label for its household-facing lending; combined AUM grew 20% year-on-year in Q4 FY26 with disbursements up 45%.

### The story in depth

Cholamandalam comes across, across fourteen quarterly calls, as an unusually well-run lender that is genuinely taking share in a growing market. Its loan book grew from INR86,703 crore in the first quarter of FY23 to INR2,42,630 crore by the fourth quarter of FY26, compounding at 20-34% a year straight through a two-year stretch of vehicle-finance credit stress as well as the recovery, and FY26 segment growth ran ahead of the industry. Two of the eleven multibagger patterns fit outright, and they reinforce each other: Market Share Gainers, and Corporate Culture. The culture is what makes the share gains believable, because the company also gives share up on purpose when the lending gets bad — it cut small commercial vehicle lending in FY24 and wound its fintech partnerships down from ten partners to three to zero, accepting negative growth in the consumer loan book to do it. The same leadership pair fronted every call, disclosed bad news promptly, declined to net gains from a bad-loan sale into its credit-cost line, and flagged the INR35 crore hit and the 65 basis point distortion rather than burying them. That shows up in the scores as Growth at 1.4 and Management at 1.17, the two strongest areas, alongside full marks for candor, distribution reach and long-term tenacity.

The trouble is that the trait doing most of the work is also the source of most of the risk. The moat here is physical, not economic: roughly 65,000 people on the ground, 31,000 of them in collections, reaching Tier 3-5 towns and small road transport operators that banks structurally will not underwrite. That machine is slow and expensive to rebuild, which is why the Cost to Replicate pattern is a partial fit and why the New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks are rated only Watch rather than High. But it is a moat made of feet on the ground, not of a product anyone prefers. Credit here is a commodity — the framework scores intangible benefits and barriers to entry both negative, and the Industry Structure area at -0.5 — so pricing is set by segment risk and funding cost rather than by brand. Vehicle yields fell about 50 basis points on competition in the first quarter of FY23, and the 33-40 basis point margin improvement in FY26 came from cheaper borrowing after RBI rate cuts, not from raising prices. That makes Pricing Power only a partial fit and feeds the Government Dependency watch: the company sells nothing to the government, yet an RBI circular reshaped its reported bad loans, RBI rate cuts drove its margin gain, and a GST rate cut is credited with reviving vehicle demand. Cyclicality is the sharpest example of one fact read two ways — the framework awards full marks for sitting at a cyclical trough with credit losses easing from 2.2% to 2.1% to 2.0% through FY26 and demand re-igniting, while the risk review rates the same cyclical exposure High Risk, because this lender funds its customers' own equipment purchases and every demand discussion turns on freight rates, monsoon, crude and replacement cycles. The Shifting Consumer Preferences watch bites at the same spot: if last-mile logistics keeps formalising toward larger fleets that banks fund, the very niche that constitutes the moat slowly shrinks.

The combined rating lands at 51 out of 100, or Decent, and the arithmetic explains why a company this well managed does not score higher: business quality 57, risk safety 64, but multibagger fit only 30. Six of the eleven patterns are partial and two are outright misses — there is no recurring or subscription revenue at all, only interest income and disbursements, and nothing resembling a small-but-critical toll on someone else's spending, since finance cost is a large, hard-negotiated part of the borrower's outlay. Structurally, this is a balance-sheet lender running six to seven times debt to equity, which is the opposite of the asset-light model the framework rewards; Return on Capital scores -1.0, the weakest area, and Competitive Advantage only 0.33. Cost discipline is real — the operating expense ratio held at about 3.0-3.1% through FY26 while absorbing a gold loan launch across 118 branches, a new labour code and a big collections build-out — but the company deliberately chases high-yield segments rather than competing on price, so that discipline shows up as margin support rather than as a low-cost weapon. Resilience is marked Unknown in the records. The honest reading is a high-quality operator with credible management and genuine share gains, running a business whose economics are set by funding costs, policy and the freight cycle rather than by anything it can price for itself.

## What to watch

- Cyclicality (High Risk): whether credit losses keep easing past the 2.0% level reached in FY26, or the vehicle-finance stress returns once the GST and rate-cut boost fades.
- Good Enough Goods / Pricing Power: whether yields hold when the cost of funds stops falling — the FY26 margin gain of 33-40 bps came from cheaper borrowing, not pricing, and vehicle yields already fell ~50 bps on competition in Q1 FY23.
- Shifting Consumer Preferences (Watch): evidence that last-mile logistics is formalising toward larger fleets banks fund, which would erode the small-operator niche that the Cost to Replicate pattern and the distribution-advantage check both rest on.
- Market Share Gainers / Corporate Culture: whether the discipline survives the growth push — the pattern is a strong fit only because share was surrendered when underwriting worsened (the SCV cut, the fintech and supply-chain exits).
- Government Dependency (Watch) and the barriers-to-entry check: further RBI or GST policy shifts, and renewed bank or fintech encroachment into used vehicles, SUVs and construction equipment.
