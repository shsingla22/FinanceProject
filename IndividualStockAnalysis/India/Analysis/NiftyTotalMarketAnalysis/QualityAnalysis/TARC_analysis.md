# TARC (TARC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

TARC Limited is an Indian real estate developer that builds and sells high-end, luxury residential apartments — mainly 3 and 4 BHK flats in high-rise and low-rise projects with clubhouses, pools and other premium amenities. Its customers are affluent Indian home buyers, and management says the sales strategy is aimed specifically at end users (people who will live in the flats) rather than investors, sold through experience centres and sample apartments. It makes money by selling apartments built on land it already owns cheaply from decades past: buyers pay in instalments as construction progresses (booked as 'presales' and 'collections'), but under Ind AS accounting the revenue only hits the profit and loss account when the project gets its occupation certificate and flats are handed over — which is why the company showed presales of Rs 1,612 crore in FY24 while reported revenue was only Rs 111 crore and it posted a loss. It operates in two focused micro-markets — New Delhi and Gurugram (Delhi NCR) — where it holds a paid-up land bank of about 500 acres, and it carries roughly Rs 1,650 crore of debt that it aims to clear within about two years using project cash flows and land compensation receipts.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Luxury residential development (the entire business)** — The company's only business line — developing and selling luxury apartment projects in Delhi and Gurugram, with three launched projects carrying combined sales potential of about Rs 7,600 crore and FY25 presales guidance of Rs 5,000 crore.
- **TARC Tripundra, Main Bijwasan Road, South Delhi** — Its first luxury project under the new brand, launched October 2022: 187 apartments over about 0.5 million sq ft on a 3-acre plot, over 80% sold, roughly Rs 1,000 crore of revenue potential, with construction in final stages and the occupation certificate and first handovers expected around Q4 FY25 — this is the project that should first turn reported revenue positive.
- **TARC Kailasa, Patel Road, Central West Delhi** — Its largest development, launched January 2024: a high-rise of roughly 400 ultra-luxury apartments over 1.7 million sq ft on about 6 acres, with sales potential of about Rs 4,000 crore; Phase 1 sold out and about 45% of the total project sold by Nov 2024, with 'Kailasa 2.0' sales due to start in January.
- **TARC Ishva (earlier called TARC 63A), Sector 63A, Gurugram** — Its newest launch, in September 2024: about 385-386 apartments over 1.4 million sq ft on roughly 7 acres with a gross development value of over Rs 2,600 crore, already about 40-45% sold, and earmarked to repay the remaining Bain Capital debt.
- **Land bank (about 500 acres in Delhi NCR)** — Paid-up land held mostly in and around Delhi (125 acres in central/south/west Delhi, 250 in north/east Delhi, 100 in Haryana, 25 in Uttar Pradesh), of which roughly 200 acres is expected to be developed over the next three to five years; parcels that don't fit the luxury-residential plan are sold off for cash, as with the earlier warehousing sales to Blackstone and ESR.
- **Delivered projects** — TARC Maceo in Sector 91, Gurugram — a 1.5 million sq ft residential project, 100% sold and handed over in 2023-24 — plus an older track record management describes as developing 20 million sq ft since the 1970s.
- **Debt and financing** — Not a revenue line but central to the story: about Rs 1,650-1,800 crore of borrowings, of which Rs 1,000 crore of Bain Capital debentures were refinanced in September 2024 through Indian banks and NBFCs led by Kotak Mahindra, cutting the all-in cost from roughly 17-18% to 12.75%, with the debt now tied to specific projects rather than the whole land bank.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

TARC is a small Delhi NCR luxury housing developer whose appeal rests on one asset: roughly 500 acres of prime, low-cost Delhi and Gurugram land accumulated since the 1970s, in locations a new entrant could not buy cheaply today. Around that sit the genuine bright spots in the quality work. Customer-facing scores are the strongest area (Customer Benefits 0.75), helped by a product with almost no direct input costs, so nearly all of every ₹100 of sales is left for people, marketing and profit. Marketing spend visibly converts — Kailasa Phase 1 sold out and Ishva sold 40-45% within weeks of launch — and the cash cycle is tight, with only about eight days between paying suppliers and collecting from buyers. Selling has also scaled fast off a very small base: presales rose about 200% to ₹1,612 crore in FY24 and ran 6x higher in the first half of FY25. That is the whole positive case, and the pattern screen reflects how thin it is: not one of the eleven multibagger patterns is a clear fit — five are partial, six do not apply at all — which is why multibagger fit scores just 10 out of 100.

The same traits that create the partial patterns also create most of the risks. The land bank is the single reason 'Cost to Replicate' scores even a partial pass, but management concedes much of it is still agricultural or unzoned, so roughly 300 of the 500 acres cannot be valued and will only convert as and when approvals come — the exact reason Government Dependency sits on watch. That land is also why the balance sheet is so heavy: every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.07 of sales, against FY24 revenue of only ₹111.45 crore. So the moat asset and the worst quality score are the same thing seen from two sides. Pricing behaves the same way. Tripundra is claimed to have appreciated 75% since its October 2022 launch and management guides to 10-15% annual price growth, but they credit an NCR-wide supply shortage and pent-up demand after seven to eight thin years rather than anything company-specific, and they say the 50-100% price surge of the last two years will not repeat. That admission is simultaneously why Pricing Power is only partial, why the company scores badly for entering after a sharp up-move rather than near a bottom, and why Cyclicality is the one high risk in the review. Brand tells a third version of the same story: the marketing language is heavy ('A brand inspired by India', five decades of legacy), but the TARC name only dates from the 2022 demerger, and on the call an investor pointed out that neighbouring Silver Glades had sold about 80% of an ultra-luxury project against TARC Ishva's 40-45%. That single comparison is what holds Brand Strength and Market Share Gainers to partial and what pushes New Entrants, Fashion Risk and Good Enough Goods all to elevated — a young brand charging ₹20,000-25,000 per square foot while a nearby rival sells faster.

What turns a mixed business into a weak overall verdict is governance and returns. The Corporate Culture pattern is not merely absent but contradicted: the first-ever investor call was held in November 2024 despite listing in December 2020, 26 subsidiaries were left unaudited at the quarterly results, investors openly alleged manipulation on the call, and debt rose to about ₹1,650 crore against a stated FY25 mission of being net cash. Management and Competitive Advantage both score negative, and Return on Capital is the weakest area of all — operating profit is negative ₹80 for every ₹100 of sales, margins have swung by roughly 149 points a year, and the business generated only ₹6 of operating cash per ₹100 of capital employed. Concentration compounds it: essentially all near-term value sits in three projects across two micro-markets, with no geographic expansion at all, so the brand has never been tested outside Delhi NCR; funding was anchored on a single financier, Bain Capital, until the ₹1,000 crore refinancing through a Kotak-led group of Indian banks and NBFCs in September 2024, which is the one clear improvement in the risk file. Execution is also running behind the story — FY25 presales guidance of ₹5,000 crore was only 26% achieved at the half-year. The composite lands at 27 out of 100 (business quality 40, multibagger fit 10, risk safety 24), a Weak one-star rating with resilience judged strained: a scarce land bank wrapped in a cyclical, negative-margin, heavily indebted, governance-troubled shell.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Stakeholder Concentration, New Entrants, Fashion Risk, Good Enough Goods, Technological Innovation. ### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 3 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Luxury residential development is a capital-asset business whose demand and pricing track the property cycle: management itself flags 50–100% price appreciation in two years that it does not expect to persist, credits 'pent up demand' after 7–8 years of thin supply, and is levering a ~₹1,650 crore debt book against a 500-acre land bank while reported P&L swung from +₹20 crore PAT (FY23) to –₹77 crore (FY24) on revenue falling from ₹368 crore to ₹111 crore. Buyers are described as people who 'have made money in other asset classes' — i.e. demand is a function of the asset-price cycle, not of operations, and the plan is to plough future cash flow back into buying more land at what are cycle-peak prices.

> "we have seen price increase from 50% to 100% over the last two years in certain micro markets. We don't believe that this is going to continue" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±361 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from -395% to 44% (swinging ±149.4 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 109% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 10% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Stakeholder Concentration — ELEVATED
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Essentially all near-term value sits in three projects in two micro-markets (Delhi/Gurugram), and the capital structure was until September 2024 anchored on a single financier, Bain Capital, branded in the FY24 deck as 'Exclusive Financial Partner', with management still exploring further 'platform deals' with them. The buyer base itself is diffuse (hundreds of individual end users), and the ₹1,000 crore refinancing spread the debt across a Kotak-led group of banks and NBFCs, which materially broadens the funding base.

> "we have been able to earmark on two assets, which is TARC Tripundra and TARC Kailasa" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: ₹1,000 crore refinanced through a Kotak Mahindra-led group of Indian banks and NBFCs, diversifying away from sole reliance on Bain Capital; retail end-user customer base is fragmented*

- ⚠️ Customers now take about 45 days to pay, up from 16 — lengthening collection terms are how weak bargaining power against concentrated customers shows up in the numbers.

### New Entrants — ELEVATED

### Good Enough Goods — ELEVATED

### Government Dependency — WATCH

## What to watch

- Land conversion: whether the roughly 300 unzoned or agricultural acres actually get state approvals — this is what would turn the partial 'Cost to Replicate' pattern into a real one and lift the ₹0.07-of-sales-per-₹1-of-assets problem.
- Governance repair: regular investor calls after the first-ever one in November 2024, audited status for the 26 subsidiaries, and a credible answer to the manipulation allegations — the specific items that make Corporate Culture a 'NO FIT'.
- Debt path versus the stated net-cash FY25 mission: borrowings went to about ₹1,650 crore, so the direction of debt after the ₹1,000 crore Kotak-led refinancing is the test of the Stakeholder Concentration risk.
