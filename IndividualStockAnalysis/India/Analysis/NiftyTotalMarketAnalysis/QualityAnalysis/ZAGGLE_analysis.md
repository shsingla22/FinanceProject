# Zaggle Prepaid Ocean (ZAGGLE) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Zaggle is an Indian company that sells software for managing company spending, bundled with payment cards it issues together with banks — a mix of SaaS and fintech. Its customers are businesses (corporates), and through them their employees, vendors and channel partners: the platform handles employee expense claims and tax-saving benefits, rewards and incentive payouts, and vendor/supplier payments, all tracked on one dashboard and mobile app. It makes money two ways — a cut of the transactions when users spend on its prepaid, credit and forex cards (issued with bank partners such as ICICI, IndusInd, Bank of Baroda, Yes Bank, DBS and NSDL Payments Bank, on Visa, RuPay and MasterCard networks) and software/platform fees charged to the corporate customers. It operates in India (consolidated FY26 revenue of about INR 1,908 crore, PAT about INR 139 crore, with over 3.7 million users), and as of FY26 is setting up a GIFT City subsidiary for cross-border payments and an Abu Dhabi base to expand into the MENA region.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Save** — Employee expense management, reimbursements and tax-saving benefit wallets (meals, health and wellness, cafeteria, gadgets) linked to prepaid cards — described as a core business, though management notes tax-linked meal benefits for the 5–15 lakh salary band are only about 0.48% of revenue, and draft 2026 tax rules extending benefits to the new regime are called a significant positive for it.
- **Propel** — A points-and-prepaid-card platform corporates use to run channel partner incentives and employee rewards and recognition programmes; presented as one of the three founding product pillars alongside Save and Zoyer.
- **Zoyer** — A data-driven business spend management platform with automated finance features, tied to corporate credit cards to handle vendor payments; launched around FY24 and credited with helping drive that year's revenue growth.
- **Corporate and co-branded cards / payment instruments** — Prepaid, corporate credit and (with Visa) forex cards issued with bank partners that sit underneath all the software products and generate the transaction income; management calls banking and network partners one of its main monetisation pillars.
- **86400 (formerly Mobileware)** — A payments-infrastructure business selling UPI and credit-line-on-UPI technology to banks and fintechs; revenue grew from about INR 17 crore in FY24 to about INR 74 crore in FY26, with FY26 PAT of roughly INR 11 crore.
- **GreenEdge Enterprises** — Runs golf privilege programmes for banks and card networks such as ICICI and Amex and the rewards/benefits programme for NPCI; acquired in FY26, revenue jumped from about INR 36.5 crore in FY25 to about INR 103.7 crore in FY26 and management guides 40–50% growth in FY27.
- **TaxSpanner (being rebranded Z.tax)** — Tax filing and salary-structuring services for individuals and corporates, extending into TDS and GST filing and a gig-worker wellness product; management says FY26 performance disappointed and is rebuilding it around an AI tax copilot for FY27.
- **Zagg.money (formerly Rio.money)** — Newly acquired retail arm selling credit cards and credit-card-on-UPI directly to the company's own 3.7 million salaried users — called the fourth monetisation pillar, with roughly INR 100 crore of capital earmarked and a five-year target of INR 500 crore revenue and INR 65 crore EBITDA.
- **DICE (asset acquisition, announced May 2026)** — A travel-and-expense and procure-to-pay spend management software suite plus its enterprise contracts and roughly 100 AI staff, bought as assets for about INR 68 crore, attractive to management for its high-margin (~95% gross margin) SaaS revenue.
- **Travel and value-added services on the app** — Tie-ups with travel firms (EaseMyTrip, FCM, Riya Travels, TBO Paxes) for bundled booking-plus-expense filing, plus bill payments via BBPS and fixed deposits offered inside the Zaggle app — add-ons that monetise the existing user base rather than standalone segments.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Zaggle is a small company growing fast inside a market that is itself expanding. It sells corporate spend, employee-benefit and expense products in India, where the digitisation of company spending, UPI and card payments is in secular growth, helped by tax rules that extend employee benefits to the new regime — the one area the quality work scores at full marks alongside market-share gain. The company is taking share within that market: revenue grew 40-70% across recent years, FY26 consolidated revenue was ₹1,908 crore, up 46%, corporate customers rose from roughly 1,800 to 3,900, users to 3.9 million, and it claims segment leadership in city-gas fleet cards. Customers stay — churn is under 2% — and much of the selling is done by others: Mastercard has a seven-year deal to recommend the platform to its bank and fintech customers, HDFC bundles Zaggle software with its credit cards, and HRMS partners refer business. That combination is why the Market Share Gainers, Friendly Middleman and Recurring Revenue patterns all register as partial fits, and why the risk review reads demand as durable — cyclicality, fashion risk and shifting customer preferences all score LOW, because the money comes from customers' everyday running costs (reimbursements, branch bills, vendor payments, fleet fuel) rather than their investment budgets. Resilience is judged SOUND.

The trouble is that the same growth engine that drives those patterns also drives most of the risks, because of what Zaggle actually gets paid for. Growth is almost entirely more transactions flowing through the platform, and the mix is getting worse as it grows: low-margin Propel Points are roughly 57-60% of revenue at a 3-6% gross margin that fell from about 10% to 4%, while the high-margin software and platform fees — the part that would make the recurring-revenue story real — are only about 2.2% of revenue. So the valuable software is effectively given away and the commodity redemption pass-through pays the bills, which is exactly why the Good Enough Goods risk sits on WATCH, why Pricing Power and Low Price Plus are outright NO FIT (across thirteen calls there is no discussion of raising prices; take rates deflate instead), and why the Recurring Revenue, Brand Strength and Cost to Replicate patterns stop at partial. The distribution partners that make the Friendly Middleman pattern work are also the concentration problem: program fees, about 41% of revenue, are invoiced to a handful of bank and network partners on spend-linked incentives locked into five- and seven-year contracts that must eventually be renegotiated. And the growth is not yet paying for itself. Operating profit is a steady ₹10 on every ₹100 of sales, but operating cash flow is negative, so every ₹100 of capital in the business produced minus ₹3 of operating cash this year. Spending on new products is genuinely growth spending rather than upkeep, and it is visibly producing revenue (Zoyer, BROME, Fleet), but the return on it is muted by that cash outflow, and the Innovation Dominance pattern is downgraded for the same reason: the product cadence is funded off thin gross margins, not fat ones.

On management, the record cuts both ways and the scores show it. There is real credit for owning bad news — admitting TaxSpanner disappointed, walking away from the EffiaSoft acquisition, re-cutting DICE from ₹123 crore to ₹68 crore as an asset purchase — and two bolt-ons have scaled sharply since being bought (86400 from ₹17 crore to ₹74 crore; GreenEdge from ₹36.5 crore to ₹103.7 crore). Against that, promises have slipped: operating cash flow breakeven was guided repeatedly for FY26 and did not arrive, and management is markedly promotional, with calls routinely listing awards and the Chairman and CEO publicly congratulating each other, which is the single worst-scoring check. That promotional habit also shows up defensively around artificial intelligence — the Chairman volunteers that IT stocks are being written down, the CEO pre-empts the question by noting software is only 2.2% of revenue, and one acquisition was abandoned because of AI — leaving Technological Innovation on WATCH. A further slice of the benefits business exists because of how employee benefits are taxed, so Government Dependency is on WATCH too, softened by an explicit push into products that do not lean on tax treatment. The Forward Integrators pattern — the Rio.money/Zagg.money move to sell retail credit directly to 3.7 million captive salaried users, with over ₹100 crore earmarked — is the clearest attempt to fix the margin problem, but it is nascent. The overall rating of 63 out of 100, 'Decent', is built from a middling business-quality score of 52 and a much higher risk-safety score of 84: the odds of something breaking look low, but the quality of what is being earned is not yet proven.
