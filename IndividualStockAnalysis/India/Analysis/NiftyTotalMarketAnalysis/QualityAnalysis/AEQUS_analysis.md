# Aequs (AEQUS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aequs is an Indian precision manufacturing company that makes complex, high-precision metal and plastic parts and assemblies to other companies' designs, rather than selling its own branded products. Its customers are global aerospace OEMs and Tier-1 suppliers such as Airbus, Boeing, Collins, Safran, SAAB and Honeywell, plus consumer companies including toy brands (Mattel, and formerly Hasbro), a large consumer electronics brand, and cookware partner Tramontina. It earns money by being paid per part shipped under long-qualification, multi-year programmes, running an unusually vertically integrated chain in-house — forging, machining, molding, stamping, surface treatment and assembly — so it captures more of the value per part; roughly 93–94% of sales are US-dollar-linked exports or deemed exports. Operations run from three integrated manufacturing clusters in Karnataka, India (Belagavi, Hubballi, Koppal, 2.22 million sq ft, much of it inside a special economic zone), plus units in Cholet, France and Paris, Texas, USA, with FY26 revenue of INR 12,304 million (up 33%) but a full-year loss of INR 1,133 million.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Aerospace** — Makes critical aircraft parts for aerostructures, actuation, engines, landing gear and interiors — 5,654 part numbers as of March 2026 — and is the mature, profitable engine of the company at INR 10,464 million of FY26 revenue (roughly 85% of the total), 20% segment ROCE and a USD 889 million order book.
- **Consumer** — Applies the same precision capabilities to components and products for consumer electronics, toys and cookware; it grew 84% to INR 1,840 million in FY26 (about 15% of revenue) but is still loss-making (EBITDA loss of INR 783 million) because new capacity is running at only 23% utilisation, with management targeting EBITDA break-even by Q4 FY27.
- **Consumer electronics (within Consumer)** — Newly industrialised stamping, precision machining and injection-molding programmes for a large global electronics brand — the main destination for recent capex, approved for MeitY PLI incentives with the first eligible year being FY27.
- **Toys (within Consumer)** — Precision molded/assembled toy manufacturing, entered around 2016-17 to target an USD 80 billion global market; Mattel began shipping in Q3 FY26 under a long-term agreement, while Hasbro has told Aequs it will stop placing purchase orders.
- **Cookware (within Consumer)** — A joint venture with Brazilian multinational Tramontina aimed at the global cookware market.
- **Joint ventures** — Partnerships that fill in specific process capabilities — Magellan Aerospace (Canada) for surface treatment and Aubert & Duval (France) for forging — which management says deliver strong margins and add about INR 1,162 million of revenue on top of the consolidated figure in FY26.
- **Defence / UAVs** — A newer venture with Accel India and Vagus Defense to design and manufacture unmanned aerial vehicles, mainly for Indian defence needs; the calls describe it as an early-stage strategic priority rather than a revenue contributor.
- **New capacity build-out** — Signed MoUs to invest INR 1,900 crore over 10 years in a new vertically integrated aero-engine and landing-gear ecosystem at Hosur, Tamil Nadu (250 acres), and INR 2,856 crore over five years across the existing Belagavi and Hubballi clusters in Karnataka.

### The story in depth

Aequs is a contract manufacturer with two genuinely attractive things going for it. Its end markets are expanding on their own — a $447bn aerospace and $126bn consumer market by 2030, plus a re-sourcing shift that could take India from 2% of the global aerospace supply chain toward 10% — and the company has spent 15 to 20 years self-funding a vertically integrated special economic zone, moving from 20% to 100% in-country value add and winning an Airbus innovation award along the way. Sales have compounded at about 15% a year, the aerospace order book has risen from $814m in December 2025 to $889m in March 2026 with deliveries stretching to 2031, and the parts portfolio is up 26% year on year to 5,654 items. Profit margins have moved barely 3.4 points over the years, which suggests competitors are not undercutting each other on price. Growth scored highest of all the quality areas, and the risk review judged the business sound overall — but the combined rating still lands at 52 out of 100, or Decent, because the two areas that turn growth into shareholder value, capital allocation and return on capital, scored zero and negative respectively.

The patterns screen explains why. Not one of the eleven patterns is a clean fit; the strongest verdict is a likely fit on engineering investment (725 engineers out of roughly 2,968 staff, an advanced-materials tie-up with IIT Dharwad, an Apple hire to run consumer engineering), and five more land as partial. Crucially, the same trait keeps showing up on both sides of the ledger. The fifteen years of NADCAP certification, per-part customer qualification and single-site integration are what make the business genuinely hard to copy and what make customers sticky — but building that fortress is exactly what makes it asset-heavy, and getting heavier: every rupee of assets now produces only 46 paise of sales, fixed asset turnover has fallen from 1.84x to 1.18x, roughly 259 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and the business generated negative ₹5 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital employed this year. The stickiness is also only half a moat: the screen found no evidence the company can raise prices. Margin improvement is credited entirely to better factory utilisation, pricing is uniform with no premium, and consumer input-cost increases are simply passed through. Because Aequs builds to its customers' drawings and owns no product intellectual property, four patterns — pricing power, brand strength, recurring revenue and forward integration — fail outright, which means the entire investment case rests on volume growth and better utilisation of an already expensive asset base rather than on widening margins.

The risks cluster around the newer, faster-growing consumer side rather than the aerospace core, and they are the flipside of the same growth story. Customer concentration is the one risk rated high, and it has already bitten: Hasbro unilaterally stopped placing orders one quarter after being named a growth customer, and the consumer electronics build is for a single implied customer. Mattel has been signed on a long-term agreement expected to absorb the freed capacity, but the episode shows how quickly a named growth driver can reverse. An analyst directly challenged the consumer margin case by pointing to Chinese suppliers whose margins in the same enclosure products compressed over eight to ten years, and management could only assert value-add in response. That matters because consumer capex is currently earning a negative return while the aerospace side's incremental returns are improving from 14% to 20% — so the segment growing fastest, from 5% to 17% of quarterly revenue, is also the one with the weakest evidence of durable economics. The huge growth spending (₹1,900cr and ₹2,856cr state MoUs, ₹500cr of consumer capex in FY27 alone) is what earns the growth marks and what creates the return-on-capital and self-inflicted over-investment problems, and it partly rests on government support — PLI approval whose first income only arrives in FY27, SEZ status and two state MoUs. Set against that, the aerospace base is genuinely defensible: technology disruption and fashion risk both scored low, and 83–85% of revenue is functional, contracted aerospace work.

Whether Mattel's volumes actually replace the Hasbro revenue in full, and whether any second consumer customer is added — this is the one high-rated risk (stakeholder concentration) and the single biggest swing factor.

Cash return on capital turning positive from this year's -5%, and the 259-day working-capital cycle shortening — the two checks scoring -2 that drag the whole rating down.

Consumer segment returns turning positive on the capex already spent; today it is ROIC-negative while aerospace incremental returns improve from 14% to 20% (capex-for-growth check, cyclicality risk).

Any sign of price increases rather than utilisation-driven margin gains, which would move the pricing power pattern off no fit and change the nature of the investment case.

Evidence on the Chinese margin-compression challenge in consumer enclosures (good enough goods risk), and whether Indian aerospace competitors such as the one cited winning a large door order start taking qualified work (new entrants risk).
