# Aequs (AEQUS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aequs is an Indian contract manufacturer that makes precision metal and plastic parts for other companies' products — it does not sell its own branded goods. Its biggest business is aerospace: forging, machining, surface treatment and assembly of aircraft components (aerostructures, actuation, engines, landing gear, interiors — 5,654 part numbers as of Q4 FY26) sold to global aircraft makers and their Tier-1 suppliers such as Airbus, Boeing, Collins, Safran, SAAB and Honeywell; a smaller consumer business applies the same machining, stamping and injection-moulding skills to make parts and finished products for consumer electronics brands, toy companies (Mattel; Hasbro has said it will stop placing orders) and cookware. It earns money by being paid per part under long-qualified, multi-year programmes, and it reports a USD $889 million aerospace order book at end-FY26; roughly 93–94% of sales are US-dollar-linked, including deemed exports. Manufacturing runs from three vertically integrated clusters in Karnataka, India (Belagavi, Hubballi, Koppal — 2.22 million sq ft, largely inside a special economic zone), plus units in Cholet, France and Paris, Texas, USA. FY26 consolidated revenue was INR 12,304 million (up 33%) with EBITDA of INR 1,545 million (13% margin) and a net loss of INR 1,133 million, the loss driven by depreciation on new consumer capacity.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Aerospace** — Makes critical aircraft parts — aerostructures, actuation, engine, landing gear and interior components — end-to-end from forging and machining through NADCAP-certified surface treatment and assembly, for global OEMs and Tier-1s; it is the mature, profitable core at INR 10,464 million of FY26 revenue (about 85% of the total, up 27%), 20% segment ROCE, a USD $889 million order book, and management guides to 25–30% growth with ~20% segment EBITDA margin in FY27.
- **Consumer** — Uses the same precision machining, stamping and plastic injection moulding to produce components and products for consumer electronics brands, toys and cookware; it is the smaller, fast-growing but currently loss-making arm at INR 1,840 million of FY26 revenue (15% of the total, up 84%, and 17% of revenue in Q4) with an EBITDA loss of INR 783 million because new plants run at only ~23% utilisation, with break-even targeted for Q4 FY27.
- **Consumer electronics (within Consumer)** — Precision components for a large global consumer electronics brand — programmes won earlier are now fully industrialised and shipping, and this is where most of the recent capital has gone (about INR 830 crore gross block in consumer, plus roughly INR 500 crore more planned in FY27), with MeitY approval received for PLI incentives that will first be claimed in FY27.
- **Toys (within Consumer)** — Moulds and assembles toys for global brands — Aequs entered this around 2016-17 citing an USD $80 billion global market and few Indian suppliers; Mattel shipments began in Q3 FY26 under a long-term agreement and are expected to absorb the capacity freed up after Hasbro said it would stop placing orders.
- **Cookware (within Consumer)** — Made through a partnership with Brazilian multinational Tramontina to serve the global cookware market; the calls mention it as part of the consumer portfolio without giving separate revenue figures.
- **Joint ventures** — Aerospace process JVs with Magellan Aerospace of Canada (surface treatment) and Aubert & Duval of France (forging) that deepen the integrated ecosystem and, management says, earn strong margins — including Aequs' proportionate share, FY26 revenue rises to INR 13,466 million and EBITDA to INR 1,830 million versus INR 12,304 million and INR 1,545 million reported.
- **Defence / UAVs** — A newer venture with Accel India and Vagus Defense to design and manufacture unmanned aerial vehicles primarily for Indian defence needs; described as an early-stage priority area rather than a revenue contributor in these calls.
- **New capacity build-out** — Two state MoUs signed in FY26 — INR 1,900 crore over 10 years with Tamil Nadu for a 250-acre aero engine and landing gear ecosystem at Hosur, and INR 2,856 crore over five years with Karnataka to expand Belagavi aerospace and Hubballi consumer capacity — plus an advanced materials R&D centre at IIT Dharwad, forming the foundation for guided FY27 consolidated growth of 45–50%.

### The story in depth

Aequs makes parts for other companies — aircraft components for Airbus, Boeing, Collins and Safran, and toys and consumer-electronics parts for branded customers — and about 85% of revenue comes from the aerospace side, with 93-94% of revenue linked to the US dollar and earned from India. The clearest strengths are in growth and in end markets: the quality framework scores structural end-market growth at its highest mark, pointing to a $447bn aerospace and $126bn consumer market by 2030, a permanent China+1 shift of supply chains toward India, and government PLI/ECMS support. Sales have compounded at roughly 15% a year, the order book has risen from $814m in December 2025 to $889m in March 2026 with deliveries running to 2031, and the aerospace parts portfolio grew 26% to 5,654 approved items. This is what earns the top-scoring multibagger pattern, Cost to Replicate: fifteen-plus years of NADCAP-certified surface treatment, forging joint ventures with Aubert & Duval and Magellan, part-by-part customer qualification, and what the record calls the only single-SEZ fully vertically integrated ecosystem in India. Alongside it sit partial fits for Toll Roads (once a part is qualified, customers do not casually switch) and Market Share Gainers, and a likely fit for Innovation Dominance based on process capability rather than products.

The awkward part is that the very thing creating the moat is also what drags the returns and drives the biggest risks. Building an integrated cluster means owning a great deal of plant, and the framework marks that down: every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.46 of sales, fixed asset turnover has fallen to 1.18x from 1.84x, about 259 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and the headline cash return on capital was negative 5% this year. The capex behind the moat is enormous and still unproven — ₹1,900cr at Hosur, ₹2,856cr in Karnataka, ₹500cr for consumer in FY27 — with the consumer business earning a negative return on capital at 23% utilisation. That same front-loading of spend into an up-cycle is exactly what the Cyclicality risk flags, and it is why the government-support that helps the story (SEZ status, PLI eligibility from FY27, two state MoUs) also shows up as a Government Dependency watch item. The second shared trait is being a build-to-print manufacturer of other people's designs and brands. That is why Toll Roads, Low Price Plus and Market Share Gainers only partially fit, why Pricing Power, Brand Strength, Recurring Revenue and Forward Integrators do not fit at all — material costs are passed straight back to customers, there is 'no differential pricing' out of India, and the brand equity belongs to the customer list — and why the record's single HIGH RISK is Stakeholder Concentration: Hasbro has already told Aequs it will stop placing orders, and the consumer-electronics build-out rests on one large brand whose demand underwrites the new capacity.

Read together, the picture is a genuinely defensible aerospace franchise being used to fund a much less protected consumer business. Aerospace is where the barriers, the qualification cycles and the segment margins above 20% sit, and management delivered 20% segment return on capital against 14% guided — the one concrete promise kept that supports the partial Corporate Culture fit alongside founder involvement since 2006-07 and prompt disclosure of both the Hasbro loss and the CFO's exit. Consumer is where the largest capex is going and where four of the watch-level risks cluster: Fashion Risk (volumes ride on someone else's product popularity), Good Enough Goods (an analyst put the Chinese precedent of 8-10 year margin compression directly to management), New Entrants (Chinese incumbents in electronics, and in aerospace a competitor winning a large door order that overlapped Aequs' claimed capability), and the concentration on a single anchor brand. The scoring reflects that split — growth scores 0.83 and customer benefits 0.75, while return on capital scores minus 1.0 and both capital allocation and competitive advantage score zero — producing a combined 53 out of 100, or 'Decent', with resilience judged sound. The investment question is not whether the moat is real; the record says the aerospace one is. It is whether the money now being poured into asset-heavy capacity earns an acceptable return before the up-cycle it was built for turns.

## Section 2 — Does it look like a long-term winner? (11 multibagger patterns)

**Overall:** the company passes the foundation every multibagger shares (steady cash + high returns on capital + growth), 1 of 1 testable checks passing, and fits 2 of the 11 patterns: Cost to Replicate, Innovation Dominance.

### Cost to Replicate — STRONG FIT
*"Could a newcomer copy this?"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** The aerospace side gives a concrete, articulable replication barrier: 15+ years of NADCAP-certified surface treatment, forging JVs (Aubert & Duval, Magellan), part-by-part OEM qualification and the only single-SEZ fully vertically integrated ecosystem in India — a well-funded copycat still faces years of certification before shipping a part; the consumer contract-manufacturing arm carries no comparable barrier.

> "That's a definite MOAT for us as a company. We have invested over the last 15 years to get there." — *management, on an earnings call*

### Innovation Dominance — LIKELY FIT
*"Out-inventing the competition"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits and every one of the 2 computable checks supports the pattern.

**What the calls show:** Investment is in process capability rather than product innovation — an advanced materials R&D ecosystem at IIT Dharwad, an Airbus innovation award for the Belagavi ecosystem, and 433 new parts added in Q4 — but no R&D spend is disclosed or growing as a line item, and there is no proprietary product pipeline or innovation-led pricing.

> "we established an advanced materials R&D ecosystem at IIT Dharwad campus" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Keeps ₹60 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs — room to fund brand, R&D and price leadership.
- ✅ High retained margin (60% of sales) alongside 15% yearly growth — the profitable-innovation flywheel: margins fund the spending that drives the growth.

### Toll Roads — PARTIAL
*"Small cost, can't skip it"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (0 of 2 checks supporting).

**What the calls show:** Qualification and certification (NADCAP, FAIs, SKU-level approvals) create genuine 'don't switch once converted' stickiness — 5,654 aerospace SKUs each tied to a qualification cycle and multi-year contract — but Aequs is a build-to-print contract manufacturer whose parts are a material, negotiated cost input, not a sub-1% magic ingredient, and it holds no standards-setting position.

> "Each of these SKUs represent a rigorous qualification cycle, a secured long-term contract, and a deep-rooted relationship with our customers acting as a high-entry barrier to others." — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ Operating margin 7%, swings of ±3.4 points, median return on capital 2% — good but not the near-monopoly profile of a toll road.
- ❌ Operating margin of 7% — too thin for a protected franchise.

### Low Price Plus — PARTIAL
*"Cheapest — and still the best"*

**What the calls show:** The thesis is India cost arbitrage plus SEZ efficiency and shared infrastructure across integrated clusters, with the customer proposition explicitly 'value-oriented'; but there is no itemised cost-savings culture, and asset turns are weak (fixed asset turnover fell to 1.18x, consumer utilisation 23%) rather than the high-turn economics the pattern requires.

*"Sells once, earns forever"*
