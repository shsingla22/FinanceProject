# Aptus Value Hou. (APTUS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aptus Value Housing Finance India Limited is an Indian lending company that gives small home loans and small business loans to low- and middle-income, mostly self-employed customers in Tier 2, 3 and 4 towns — people the calls describe as 'underserved and unserved' and who have little access to regular mortgage credit. It makes money on the spread between what it charges borrowers (high rates — management cites about 21% on small business loans, with home loan rates cut 50-75 bps on new loans in FY26) and what it pays to borrow from banks, the National Housing Bank, NCD investors (including IFC, insurers and mutual funds) and securitisation, with cost of funds down to about 8.1% and spreads around 9% by March 2026. It lends through its own physical branch network — 211 branches in mid-2022 growing to 339 by March 2026 — backed increasingly by digital/referral sourcing and 'connector' partners, and the loan book (AUM) grew from about Rs. 5,519 crores in June 2022 to Rs. 13,107 crores in March 2026, earning roughly 8% return on assets and over 20% return on equity. It started in and is still concentrated in the southern states (Tamil Nadu, where it has operated 15-16 years, plus Karnataka, Telangana and others) and is now expanding into Maharashtra and Odisha.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Housing Loans (home loans)** — Loans to buy or build a house for low- and middle-income households in small towns; management calls this the core affordable-housing business and, alongside small business loans, one of the two things it is 'growing disbursements and loan book' in — it is the segment where it cut lending rates by 50-75 bps on new loans in FY26 to attract better-quality borrowers.
- **Quasi home loans** — Loans that sit next to the plain home loan (referred to on calls as 'quasi home loans' and, by one analyst, alongside LAP) at higher yields than home loans; the company says it did not cut rates here and is raising average ticket size by about Rs. 1 lakh a year like its other products.
- **Small Business Loans / SME** — Loans to small self-employed businesses at a yield of around 21%, which management says is at or below what rivals in that segment charge; it is described as the second growth engine beside housing and as a reason Aptus's returns look higher than peers', though management flagged a slight uptick in SME NPAs in Q3 FY26.
- **Small-ticket loans below Rs. 7 lakhs (discontinued)** — A sub-segment of lending the company deliberately stopped sanctioning during FY26 to move upmarket to better-quality customers — it was about 17.5% of lending in April 2024 and was still roughly 10-11% of the loan book in December 2025, running down over time, and dropping it temporarily slowed disbursement growth in the first half of FY26.
- **NBFC arm (group entity)** — The calls refer to consolidated results and to 'the NPA of NBFC' rising slightly in FY26, indicating lending is done through more than one group entity, but the calls give no further detail on its size or products.
- **Branch network and sourcing channels** — The delivery engine rather than a product: 339 branches as of March 2026 with about 60 more planned in FY27, supported by digital channels (customer referral app, construction ecosystem app, social media) that contributed roughly 18% of business in Q1 FY25, plus a newer 'connector' partner channel described as still nascent.
- **Borrowing and liquidity function** — How the lending is funded — roughly 58% from banks, 15% NCDs, 19% securitisation including direct assignment, and about 9% from NHB as of March 2026, with Rs. 2,057 crores of liquidity including Rs. 1,505 crores of undrawn bank sanctions; ratings were upgraded to AA- by CARE and ICRA back in 2022, which management says helped lower funding costs.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Aptus is a lender to self-employed households in small Indian towns, and the records show the strongest evidence sits with its people and its returns rather than with its industry. Management scores highest of any quality area (1.17), on a record of fully organic growth with no acquisitions, low borrowings against a net worth of over INR 3,000 crores in FY23, a conservative 24-month write-off policy with a retained management overlay, and openly self-critical disclosure — quantifying the April 2024 technology-migration hit to disbursements, election-driven overdues creeping up, and the exact INR 283 crore of disbursement given up by exiting loans under Rs.7 lakh. That discipline shows up in the numbers: return on assets of 8.8% in FY23 and still 8.2% in FY26, spreads of 9%, and return on equity climbing from about 14% to 21.2% over five years. The delivery engine behind it is a branch network grown from 211 in June 2022 to 339 by March 2026, staffed by the company's own sales officers and its own field collection team rather than outside agents, reaching towns larger lenders will not originate in — and run at operating costs of just 2.7-2.8% of assets.

That single trait — owning the whole chain from sourcing to collection in underserved towns — is what drives most of the pattern scores, but it also explains why almost none of them come out clean. It supports the Forward Integrators reading (no dependence on outside sales agents, direct assignment of loans kept under about 10% of assets), the Low Price Plus reading (line-by-line benchmarking of employee, branch and transaction cost, loan-to-values of 35-40%, credit cost held at the guided 0.5%), the Cost to Replicate reading (a 15-year underwriting and collections footprint, with only about 2% of customers refinancing away on Rs.8-9 lakh loans), and the Market Share Gainers reading (assets compounding roughly 21-30% a year from Rs.5,519 crore to Rs.13,107 crore without loosening standards). Yet each stops at PARTIAL for a stated reason: management itself admits new entrants simply hire away its staff and open branches nearby, it never claims market share, and it is not the cheapest lender. Only Corporate Culture is a STRONG FIT, resting on the same founder-led stability and repeated delivery against guidance. Two patterns — Recurring Revenue and Toll Roads — do not apply at all, because interest is the customer's main expense rather than a small unavoidable fee, and there are no renewal or service-type income streams. The result is a multibagger fit score of only 15, the weakest of the three inputs, alongside the lowest quality area, Industry Structure, at 0.17.

The risk review is the most reassuring of the three at 84, with four channels rated LOW — technology, stakeholder concentration, shifting customer preferences and fashion — because the book is granular retail (137,000-plus live customers at roughly Rs.9 lakh average, no single customer named across 16 calls), demand rests on functional necessity with 80% of funded customers living in the financed house, and the only technology discussed is Aptus's own incremental spending. But the four WATCH items grow directly out of the same strengths described above. The high yields that produce the 8% return on assets are exactly what invites the Good Enough Goods risk: banks and large housing finance companies are a genuinely cheaper alternative, margins have already fallen from 14.28% to about 12.8%, and Aptus itself cut new home-loan rates by 50-75 basis points in FY26 — which is also why Pricing Power drops from its decade-long record of holding rates through a 140 basis point tightening cycle to only PARTIAL. The tier-3/4 concentration that makes the network hard to copy is the same concentration behind the New Entrants risk, with Tamil Nadu growth stuck at 10-15% against 20%-plus elsewhere. Funding and rules remain policy-linked (National Housing Bank refinance was 22-28% of borrowings, since reduced to 9%), and cyclicality is unresolved because the five-year record is short even though the loans are secured, small-ticket and repaid monthly. Combining 69 for business quality, 15 for pattern fit and 84 for risk safety gives 57 out of 100 — a Decent, three-star verdict: a well-run, high-return lender whose long-term resilience is recorded as UNKNOWN and whose economics are more defensible than they are unassailable.
