# United Breweries Ltd. (UBL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: CONSUMER GOODS*

United Breweries Limited (UBL) is an Indian beer maker: it brews and sells beer under brands such as Kingfisher (including Kingfisher Ultra and Ultra Max), Heineken and Heineken Silver, and Amstel Grande, with Heineken holding majority control of the company. It makes money by selling cases of beer to state-controlled liquor channels — government corporations in some states and private distributors and retailers in others — with the final consumer prices and excise duties set state by state, so revenue is essentially volume times the price it can get approved in each market. It operates only across India, split by management into North, West, East and South regions, with big markets including Karnataka, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Odisha, West Bengal and Assam. Profit comes from the gap between that revenue and its brewing costs — barley/raw materials, glass bottles and packaging — with gross margin running in the low-to-mid 40s percent and operating (EBIT) margin in the single digits, and it is a seasonal business where the hot summer quarter is the peak and heavy monsoon or duty hikes can knock volumes down.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Beer volume / core (non-premium) portfolio** — The bulk of the business — mainstream Kingfisher-led beer sold across all four regions, and the part most exposed to state duty increases and affordability problems, which management repeatedly calls its number-one concern.
- **Premium segment** — Higher-priced brands like Kingfisher Ultra, Ultra Max, Heineken, Heineken Silver and Amstel Grande, which management says consistently grow faster than the total portfolio (up 14% in Q3 FY24, 24% in Q4 FY25, 32% for FY25 and 17% in Q2 FY26) and are the core of its 'premiumization' strategy to lift price mix and margins.
- **Own breweries (supply chain / manufacturing)** — Company-owned plants across states — five in Karnataka and Telangana alone — that management is actively reshaping: a new greenfield brewery being built in Uttar Pradesh, the closure of the Mangalore brewery, and three breweries flooded in the Q2 FY26 monsoon.
- **Contract brewing / partner network** — Third-party breweries UBL buys production from to serve demand it cannot brew itself; management says leaning on these 'contracted buying parties' while its own capacity expansion is incomplete puts short-term pressure on gross margin.
- **Returnable bottles** — Not a separate business but a named profit driver — beer is sold in glass bottles that are meant to come back and be reused, and when returns fall short the company must inject costly new bottles, which management blamed directly for the softer gross margin in Q3 FY24.
- **Route-to-market / distribution and trade investment** — How the beer physically reaches roughly tens of thousands of outlets — distributor markets like Maharashtra (where it found 13 districts with zero coverage and is pushing next-day delivery from 62% to ~95%) versus government-run channels like Telangana (where it had overdue payments) — plus visi-coolers placed in stores, up from about 15,000 to 37,000.
- **Brand investment** — Ongoing marketing and brand-building spend, described as a deliberate choice rather than a segment — up over 20% year-on-year in Q2 FY26 and a main reason operating profit fell even as management insisted on continuing it.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

United Breweries earns its highest marks for reach and brand. The quality framework scores its distribution advantage and its convenience benefits at the top of the scale: the largest brewery network in India, the widest route-to-market, chilled availability scaled from 15,000 to 37,000 coolers, and a Maharashtra distribution redesign that lifted next-day delivery from 62% to 95% and reached 13 districts that previously had no coverage at all. That same machinery is what makes the two best-scoring patterns work. Brand Strength is a strong fit — Kingfisher is a heritage name, brand investment rose more than 20% year-on-year in Q2FY26 even in a loss-making quarter, and tracked brand-power scores on Ultra and Kingfisher are at all-time highs. Market Share Gainers is a likely fit, with premium volumes up 14% in Q3FY24, 32% in FY25 and still 17% in a declining Q2FY26, against a premium share described as mid-single-digit and growing toward a stated 35% ambition. The framework also credits a structurally under-penetrated market (85-90m beer drinkers against 200m liquor consumers) and a weakened craft competitor explicitly identified as a source of share in Andhra Pradesh and premium. Management candour scores well too, and shows up again inside the patterns and risks: peak-season execution graded '7 out of 10', zero distribution in 13 Maharashtra districts admitted, flooded breweries and a disappointing quarter owned rather than deflected.

The central tension is that the single feature protecting the business is also the one capping it. State licensing, brewery capital and state-set maximum retail prices keep new entrants out — margins have moved only ±3.2 points over the years — and the Cost to Replicate pattern rests on exactly that licensing regime plus the brewery network and exclusive access to Heineken's portfolio. But the same rulebook means prices are granted by state excise committees rather than set by the company, and management says plainly it cannot pass cost inflation on to consumers. That is why Pricing Power and Toll Roads are both scored as no fit, and why Government Dependency is the one high risk in the review: price increases need state-by-state approval, Karnataka duty and label changes wiped out a month of sales, and Telangana forced a supply suspension. Affordability is management's self-declared number one concern, which is the Good Enough Goods risk in another form — when economy prices rose in Karnataka the category fell double digits, and the response has been to push cheaper local brands (London Pilsner in Orissa, Kalyani Black Label in West Bengal) rather than hold the line on price. The Low Price Plus pattern is only a partial fit for the same reason: bottle-return improvement, the Mangalore brewery closure, network optimisation and trade-spend streamlining read as reactive defence of margin, not a durable low-cost model. Being under-indexed in premium at mid-single-digit share, against premium and craft entrants that as unlisted companies can outspend on advertising without quarterly scrutiny, keeps the New Entrants risk elevated even while UBL is taking share from a weakened craft player.

Where the story weakens most is cash and capital. The business keeps ₹80 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs, but only converts ₹7 of operating cash per ₹100 of capital employed, and roughly 187 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. That long cycle is not just a working-capital number — it connects directly to the Stakeholder Concentration risk, since in corporation states the government is effectively the sole buyer and payer, and Telangana left UBL chasing overdues with management saying 'we are still not out of the wood there'. It is also the mirror image of the Recurring Revenue pattern scoring no fit: this is one-off case sales into state-controlled channels with customers who pay late, not customers who fund the business up front. Capital allocation scores flat overall; capex is genuinely growth-oriented and rising — from around ₹250cr guided in FY23 to a UP greenfield brewery plus coolers by FY26 — but the returns on that new spending are not yet proven, and it is being committed into the same price-controlled markets that cap what those assets can earn. Netting it out, the combined rating is 45 out of 100 — 64 on business quality, 23 on multibagger fit, 38 on risk safety — a Mixed, two-star verdict with resilience judged strained. The reading is a genuinely advantaged distributor and brand owner operating inside a regulatory system that both shelters it from competition and denies it the pricing and cash returns that would turn those advantages into compounding value.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration, New Entrants, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.3× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ⚠️ Only about 31% of recent operating profit became cash (was 98%) — profits are getting stuck in paper, an early stress signal.
- ✅ Returns on capital moderated from about 16% to 13% — lower, but still well clear of the deep slide this check looks for.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Across all thirteen quarters pricing, volumes and even receivables are set by state excise policy: price increases require state-by-state approval and a government pricing committee, Karnataka duty/label changes wiped out a month of sales, Telangana suspensions and overdue government payments hit the largest profit market, and management's fourth strategic pillar is lobbying governments via the Brewers Association. Volume is also constrained by state-run depots and corporation-market warehousing.

> "in India, we cannot translate cost price increase into consumer pricing" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Wide multi-state footprint so no single excise regime dominates; active policy engagement (Maharashtra, Assam, Meghalaya duty wins) shows some ability to shape outcomes*

### Stakeholder Concentration — ELEVATED
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** In corporation states the government is effectively the sole buyer and payer — Telangana left UBL chasing overdues ("we are still not out of the wood there") and forced a commercial decision to suspend supply — while route-to-market changes in Tamil Nadu/Andhra (~10% of volume) and consolidation among key glass-bottle suppliers created a supply glitch. Offsetting this, revenue is spread across many states and a broadening distributor/retail base.

> "the bottle industry went through a lot of changes, especially with some of the key big suppliers with mergers" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Geographic and channel broadening — Maharashtra distributor redesign taking coverage to 20,000+ outlets and 13 previously uncovered districts*

- ⚠️ Customers now take about 112 days to pay, up from 86 — lengthening collection terms are how weak bargaining power against concentrated customers shows up in the numbers.

### New Entrants — ELEVATED
*"A new player changes the rules"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** UBL is under-indexed in premium (mid-single-digit share) against premium and craft entrants, and an analyst explicitly framed the disadvantage of being listed versus unlisted competitors who can outspend on advertising without quarterly scrutiny; the response has been heavy brand and innovation investment. The main craft entrant is now in "existential crisis" and UBL is taking share from it.

> "Your competitors in premium are unlisted, and that gives them an advantage of propping up ad spend without looking at the quarterly results too much" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: UBL building its own premium/new-format portfolio (Heineken Silver, Amstel Grande, Ultra Max) and gaining share as the craft entrant weakens*

- ⚠️ Operating margin drifted down from about 14% to 9% — steady profit erosion, the mark of rising competitive pressure.

## What to watch

- Cash returns: whether the 7% cash return on capital and the 187-day cash cycle improve, particularly as state corporations settle overdues (Return on capital, Cost of working capital, Stakeholder Concentration).
