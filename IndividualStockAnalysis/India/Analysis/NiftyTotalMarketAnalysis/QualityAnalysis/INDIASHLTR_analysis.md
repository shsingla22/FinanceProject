# India Shelter Fin. (INDIASHLTR) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

India Shelter Finance is a housing finance company that lends money to people in smaller Indian towns to buy, build or improve a home, or to borrow against property they already own; every loan is backed by property as security. Its typical customer is a low- or middle-income, largely self-employed family taking a first home loan of about Rs. 10 lakhs, with a woman as a borrower in almost all cases, and the company lends conservatively at roughly half the property's value. It makes money on the gap between what it charges borrowers (portfolio yield around 14.8%-14.9%) and what it pays to borrow from banks, the National Housing Bank and other lenders (cost of funds around 8.2%-8.8%), holding that spread above 6%, plus some fee income; profits come to about Rs. 500 crores a year on assets of Rs. 11,044 crores as of March 2026. It operates through its own branch network in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities (about 90% of the loan book), which grew from 215 branches across 15 states in early 2024 to over 300 branches by FY26, with nearly all loans sourced by its own staff rather than through agents.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Home loans (HL)** — Loans to low- and middle-income customers building or buying their first home in Tier-2/Tier-3 towns; management describes this as about 50% of the loan book and the core focus, supported by government schemes like PMAY 2.0 under which over 2,000 of its customers had received subsidy by December 2025.
- **Loan against property (LAP)** — Loans given against a customer's existing property, described as about 42% of the book, and management repeatedly says its repayment behaviour and bad-loan levels run essentially in line with home loans (a gap of only 1-2 basis points).
- **In-house sourcing and branch network** — The company finds, underwrites, values, and collects on its own loans through its branches rather than buying business from outsiders — 97%-98% of sourcing is in-house — and it adds 40-45 branches a year, which management calls the main engine of both growth and its improving cost ratios (opex to assets down to about 4%).
- **Digital sourcing channel** — A newly introduced online lead channel, described in the Q3FY26 call as contributing about 4%-5% of disbursements today with a plan to take it to 10%; small but flagged as the next step in its technology journey.
- **Borrowing / funding operations** — Not a customer-facing product but central to earnings: it raises long-term money from more than 30 lenders, including refinance from the National Housing Bank (about 15%-16% of borrowings, and roughly 60 basis points cheaper than bank funding), with average borrowing tenure over eight years and positive asset-liability matching.

### The story in depth

India Shelter is an affordable-housing lender whose whole edge sits in how it reaches and services its borrowers rather than in what it sells. It sources 98% of its business in-house through a 300-plus branch network in Tier-2/3 towns built over fifteen years, and it keeps credit, legal, technical and collections work inside the company, with 92% of collections done digitally. That single design choice is what earns the top marks in the quality framework for distribution advantage and customer closeness, and it is the same trait behind the 'Forward Integrators' and 'Cost to Replicate' pattern fits. It also explains why two of the eight risk channels come back mild: because there are no dealer, agent or builder tie-ups and the average loan is roughly Rs.10 lakh spread across 15 states, there is no single customer or middleman the company depends on. Management scores well for the same reasons — three straight years of branch guidance made and met at 41-42 additions, purely organic growth, leverage of only 2.8x, and unusual candor in explaining that its own decision to let accounts roll so it could use SARFAESI added 25-30bps to Stage-3 rather than the 10-15bps it might otherwise have been, while calling FY26 'a muted year' outright. That record is the reason the 'Corporate Culture' pattern is the one clear strong fit in the entire screen.

The weakness is that none of this converts into the ability to charge more. The product is a plain secured mortgage: the portfolio yield has sat flat at about 14.8-14.9% for three years, disbursement yields have drifted down from 15.0% to 14.6%, and management runs to a target spread of about 6%, passing rate cuts on to customers when funding gets cheaper instead of keeping the difference. That is why four of the eleven multibagger patterns — pricing power, brand, toll roads and recurring revenue — are flat 'no fit', and why the intangibles check scores negative. The very same fact drives two of the risk warnings: a 14.8-14.9% yield sitting well above what banks charge is a premium that has to be defended every year, so roughly 4-5% of the book transfers out to cheaper lenders annually, and analysts keep pressing on banks poaching customers as rates fall. The defence is holding for now — balance-transfer-out actually fell from about 5.5% to 4.3-4.5% even through a 125bps easing cycle, and the Rs.5-25 lakh, 80%-plus self-employed, 51-52% loan-to-value niche is one cheaper lenders are not set up to underwrite or collect in — but it is defence, not a toll gate. Returns are also structurally capital-hungry: this is a business where the balance sheet is the product, and a return on assets of roughly 5.5-5.8% comes from the interest spread rather than from turning capital over quickly. Together these pull the multibagger fit score down to 15 out of 100, the weakest of the three inputs.

The cheap NHB refinance that helps the cost story is itself a policy dependency, and this cuts both ways in the records. Cost discipline is real — operating costs have fallen from 4.4% to 4.0% of assets and cost-to-income from 41% to 36%, helped by NHB money that comes about 60bps below bank funding — but NHB is 15-16% of borrowings, PMAY 2.0 is cited as a demand driver, RBI rate moves set the spread, and priority-sector rules shape the housing-loan versus loan-against-property mix. The same government support that makes the end market a genuinely under-penetrated, secular opportunity, which is the framework's strongest growth mark, is also what puts the government-dependency channel on watch. The offsetting fact is that the underlying demand is self-construction and self-purchase by families in tier 2-4 towns, not subsidy-created: PMAY subsidy reached only around 2,000 customers. On the whole the risk review is the most reassuring of the three reads, at 84 out of 100, with four channels rated low and four merely on watch and none flagged severe; technology disruption in particular is a non-issue, since the incumbent is the one building the digital sourcing channel. What holds the overall verdict to 56 out of 100, or Decent, is the current cycle rather than the structure: assets under management grew from Rs.5,609 crore to Rs.11,044 crore at roughly 30% a year, well ahead of system credit growth, but FY26 disbursement growth slowed to 11%, there were two muted quarters, Stage-3 rose, and guidance was cut — even as the Rs.30,000 crore by 2030 goal, the branch cadence and the 60:40 product mix were all left unchanged. Resilience through a full downturn is recorded as unknown.
