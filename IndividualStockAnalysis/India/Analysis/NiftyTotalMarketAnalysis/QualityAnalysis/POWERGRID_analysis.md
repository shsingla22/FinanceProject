# Power Grid Corporation of India Ltd. (POWERGRID) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

POWERGRID is India's largest electricity transmission utility — it builds, owns and operates the high-voltage lines and substations that carry bulk power from where it is generated (coal, nuclear, and increasingly large renewable hubs like Khavda, Bhadla and Rajasthan) to state utilities and load centres across the country. It does not sell electricity itself; it charges regulated transmission fees to the utilities and distribution companies that use its grid, so revenue is essentially annuity-like rent on the assets it has built, earned as long as the lines stay available. Availability is the key operating metric — management repeatedly cites ~99.81–99.84% uptime, above the 99.75% threshold at which it earns extra incentive payments. Its network is pan-India (about 1.84 lakh circuit km of lines, 291 substations and 624 GVA of transformation capacity as of March 2026), it is majority government-owned (51.34%), and it has begun a small international push through a PPP partnership with Africa50.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Transmission (regulated / RTM assets)** — The core business — building and running high-voltage transmission lines and substations whose tariffs are set by the regulator, contributing roughly ₹32,368 crore of transmission charges in the first nine months of FY25 out of ₹34,869 crore total income, i.e. the overwhelming majority of revenue.
- **TBCB projects (Tariff-Based Competitive Bidding)** — Projects won in open auctions against private bidders, each housed in its own subsidiary/SPV — a growing share of new work (management cited ~50% of annual tariff and 57% of projects won up to Q3 FY25), and the reason the group has 80+ SPVs, which it is now merging into fewer entities for a leaner structure.
- **Telecom (PowerTel)** — Sells bandwidth and connectivity over optical fibre strung on its transmission towers, plus fibre-to-home broadband and international long-distance services; small but the fastest-growing piece, at ₹722 crore over nine months of FY25, up 22% year on year.
- **Consultancy** — Advises and executes transmission and sub-transmission work for other utilities in India and abroad, a modest fee business of roughly ₹427 crore over nine months of FY25.
- **Data centres** — An emerging venture using its own substation land — approval received for a data centre at Manesar and land procured in Chennai for a disaster-recovery data centre — still early stage with no meaningful revenue disclosed.
- **Smart metering, solar and battery storage** — Newer diversification bets: smart-metering tenders (e.g. Gujarat), an 85 MW solar plant at Nagda now commissioned, and its first battery energy storage (BESS) project won via bidding — all small relative to transmission.
- **Asset monetisation** — Periodically transfers completed transmission assets into an investment vehicle to recycle capital, though management noted delays because higher interest rates depressed valuations and the structure had to be reworked so assets return to POWERGRID at the end of the concession.

### The story in depth

Power Grid's core strengths all rest on one thing: it owns the physical backbone of India's power system and nobody can build a second one. It holds roughly 84-85% of the country's inter-regional transfer capacity across 1.84 lakh circuit km and 291 substations, which the quality work scores as a full-strength monopoly with, in its words, infinite switching costs — a utility that wants power moved has no other route. The service record backs the position up: availability held at 99.81-99.84% against a 99.75% incentive threshold, and each line trips about once every four years. The revenue that follows is contracted for 35-40 years and paid on availability rather than volume, which is why the risk review rates cyclicality only a WATCH and puts fashion risk, technology risk and shifting customer preferences all at LOW — a conductor is needed whatever generates the power. The one pattern rated a STRONG FIT is Cost to Replicate, and it is the honest description of the whole business: the world's largest 765 kV network, 11 HVDC systems, 35+ years of execution depth, and rights-of-way that even the incumbent struggles to obtain. Demand ahead looks genuinely large — 786 GW of renewables by 2035-36 plus data centres, EVs, green hydrogen and storage, against government plans quantifying ₹9-15 lakh crore of investment to 2035.

The trouble is that the very features producing those strengths also produce the biggest risks, and in several places it is literally the same fact scored twice. Owning irreplaceable physical assets means being extremely asset-heavy: ₹3,20,334 crore of gross fixed assets generated ₹47,684 crore of FY26 income, about ₹0.16 of sales per ₹1 of assets, scored -2. That same weight is why Recurring Revenue only rates PARTIAL — the income is annuity-like and near-perpetual, but the model is the opposite of capital-light. Being a national monopoly in an essential service means the price is not the company's to set: tariffs come from the regulator or from reverse auctions, so pricing power is the one pattern marked NO FIT, pricing/mix/volume scores -2, and FY26 income was near-flat at ₹47,684 crore against ₹47,459 crore despite capex scaling roughly 4.5x to about ₹40,000 crore. The shift from cost-plus to competitive bidding is where the moat stops working: in that arena the deliverable is effectively a commodity chosen on lowest tariff, so New Entrants is HIGH RISK, Good Enough Goods is ELEVATED, and the company won only 9 of the FY26 awards with 7-8 bidders on smaller projects. Its Low Price Plus advantage — AAA rating, 7.54% cost of borrowing, scale — is real but only half-wins, and its bid discipline has a price: it walked away from the ₹25,000 crore Fatehpur-Bhadla project rather than bid below its limit. That is why Market Share Gainers reads PARTIAL: dominant overall, but drifting down where competition is allowed.

Layered on top is that almost every side of the business points back to the state — 51.34% government ownership, CERC-set tariffs that cut about ₹140 crore of O&M in a single quarter under the FY25-29 regulations, an order book from government NEP planning, and customers who are the state distribution companies. That makes Government Dependency the other HIGH RISK, and the diversification meant to offset it (telecom, consultancy, data centres, smart metering, the Africa50 PPP in Kenya) is still a small fraction of the whole. Customer risk is ELEVATED but well managed in practice: pooled pan-India billing and the PRAPTI portal's automatic recovery beyond 75 days delivered 101.5% realisation with dues down to ₹4,380 crore, even though the underlying discoms are structurally weak. Management comes out well — capex guidance was raised from ₹28,000 to ₹35,000 crore and about ₹40,000 crore delivered, capitalisation committed at ₹25,000 crore and ₹28,206 crore delivered, execution failures named openly, and a training pipeline of some 1,400-1,500 technicians a year — but delivery is being applied to a business whose returns the company does not control. The scorecard shows exactly that split: full marks on competitive advantage and customer benefits, but zero on return on capital and only a third on growth and industry structure, giving 64 on business quality, 28 on multibagger fit, 36 on risk safety, and 46 out of 100 overall — Mixed, two stars, with resilience judged SOUND. In plain terms: a fortress that is very hard to attack and very hard to make more profitable.

### Recurring Revenue (Product Upfront) — PARTIAL
*"Sells once, earns forever"*

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** This is the archetype the channel warns about: a fixed-infrastructure utility, 51.34% government-owned, whose tariffs are set by CERC (the FY25-29 regulations cut O&M by ~₹140cr in one quarter alone), whose entire order book comes from government-planned NEP/CEA schemes and PSU tender routes, and whose project economics depend on policy items like the Rajasthan land policy, revised RoW compensation norms and the ₹8,000cr central grant on Ladakh.
