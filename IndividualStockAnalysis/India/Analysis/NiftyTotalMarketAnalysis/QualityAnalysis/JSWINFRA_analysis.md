# JSW Infrast (JSWINFRA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

JSW Infrastructure is an Indian ports company that gets paid to load, unload, store and move cargo — mainly coal, iron ore, containers and liquid products — through the seaport berths and terminals it owns or operates under long-term government concessions. Roughly half its cargo comes from its own parent group's factories (JSW Steel plants at Dolvi, Vasind, Tarapur and elsewhere) and the other half from outside "third-party" customers, with the third-party share rising from 36% of volumes in FY24 to a record 52% by Q1 FY26. It makes money on a per-tonne cargo-handling basis plus vessel-related charges (about 17-18% of revenue is dollar-denominated), and it runs at high margins — around 52% EBITDA — handling 58.2 million tonnes in the first half of FY26 for Rs. 2,686 crores of revenue, Rs. 1,387 crores of EBITDA and Rs. 758 crores of net profit. Its assets sit along both Indian coasts (Jaigarh, Dharamtar, Paradip, Mangalore, Ennore, PNP, Goa/South West Port, Tuticorin, JNPA, Kolkata) plus liquid-storage and operations contracts in the UAE, and it is expanding capacity from 177 million tonnes a year today to a targeted 400 million tonnes by FY30.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Port business (ports and terminals)** — The core operation — cargo-handling berths and terminals at Jaigarh, Dharamtar, Paradip (separate coal and iron ore terminals), Mangalore, Ennore, PNP, South West Port, Tuticorin and JNPA — which brought in Rs. 1,086 crores of the Rs. 1,314 crores total revenue in Q1 FY26 at a 51.7% operating EBITDA margin, making it the overwhelming bulk of the company.
- **Group (captive) cargo vs third-party cargo** — Management splits every tonne into cargo from its own JSW group companies and cargo from outside customers; third-party volumes have grown fastest (48% year-on-year in Q2 FY25) and reached 52-54% of the mix, and management treats this shift as the key measure of the business becoming less dependent on the parent.
- **Liquid storage and terminals** — Tank storage and liquid-cargo handling — a 465,000 cubic metre liquid storage facility at Fujairah in the UAE bought for $187 million (described as a major world bunkering hub), a 2 million tonne LPG project at Jaigarh, and a new liquid terminal at JNPA that was nearing commercial start-up in late 2025.
- **Overseas operations and maintenance contracts (UAE)** — Paid contracts to run other owners' port facilities at Fujairah and Dibba in the UAE; Fujairah was on track to beat its minimum guaranteed cargo volumes, which entitles JSW to a share of the extra profit.
- **Logistics business (Navkar Corporation and multimodal assets)** — An inland container and rail-linked logistics arm built around the acquired Navkar Corporation — 81,000 TEUs of EXIM container volume and 275,000 tonnes of domestic cargo in Q1 FY26 on Rs. 138 crores of revenue — plus NCR Rail Infrastructure (won through insolvency proceedings) and a new Rs. 380 crore multimodal logistics park and inland container depot at Kudathini, Ballari; still small today but targeted to reach a Rs. 8,000 crore top line by FY30.
- **Greenfield port development** — Three brand-new ports being built at once — Keni in Karnataka, Murbe in Maharashtra and Jatadhar in Odisha — together about 93 million tonnes a year of capacity in their first phase; management says no other Indian port company is developing three greenfield ports simultaneously.
- **Brownfield expansion of existing ports** — A Rs. 2,359 crore programme of new berths, civil and mechanical work and a railway siding at Jaigarh and Dharamtar, expected to add roughly 27 million tonnes a year of handling capacity by March 2027.
- **Winning privatised government terminals (PPP / landlord-port model)** — Bidding for terminals that central-government-owned "major ports" are handing to private operators — described as the company's single biggest growth opportunity since major ports hold over half of India's capacity — with a recent win being a 30-year concession to redevelop and run the 6.3 million tonne container terminal at Netaji Subhash Dock, Kolkata.
- **Iron ore slurry pipeline** — A 302 kilometre pipeline to pump iron ore in slurry form instead of trucking or railing it, with 218 km welded and 195 km lowered as of October 2025 and completion targeted for March 2027.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

JSW Infrastructure is a port and logistics business whose whole story turns on one fact: ports are extremely hard and expensive to build. That shows up on the good side of the ledger as very steady profits — ₹49 of every ₹100 of sales is operating profit, with margins moving only about ±3.6 points a year — and as strong protection from competition. The 'Cost to Replicate' pattern is the strongest read in the whole file: coastal sites are finite, held under 30-year concessions, and require multi-year environmental approvals, public hearings (Keni, Murbe) and heavy dredging spend before a rival could even start. But the same heaviness is the biggest single drag on the numbers: every ₹1 of assets throws off only ₹0.26 of sales, and the return-on-capital area scores zero. Management is spending roughly ₹1,100 crore of quarterly port revenue against multi-thousand-crore capex commitments — ₹2,359 crore at Jaigarh/Dharamtar, three greenfield ports at once, and FY26 guidance of ₹4,000 crore for ports plus ₹1,500 crore for logistics. The moat and the capital drag are the same trait seen from two sides.

The growth case is unusually well evidenced and it comes from taking business off weaker owners. Capacity is going from 177 MTPA today toward 288 by FY28 and 400 by FY30, and third-party cargo has climbed from 36% in Q2 FY24 to a record 52% in Q1 FY26 — that is the 'Market Share Gainers' pattern, and it is fed directly by the 'share donators' the framework identifies: inefficient state-owned major ports being handed to private operators, and distressed assets bought out of insolvency such as Navkar and NCR Rail. The end market itself is expanding by policy, with India targeting 10,000 MT of port capacity by 2047 from 2,600 MT. The catch is that this is also the single largest risk. Government Dependency is rated HIGH RISK precisely because the growth engine runs on government decisions — privatisation of major-port terminals, concession and royalty terms agreed with port authorities and state governments, environmental clearances and public hearings. The mechanism that creates the opportunity is the mechanism that could withhold it. The partial offset noted is the same diversification that drives growth elsewhere: private ports at Jaigarh and Dharamtar, the Fujairah/Dibba operations in the UAE that are now beating their minimum volume commitments, and the pan-India logistics push.

The third thread is customer concentration, and here too the strength and the risk are one thing. Dharamtar is 100% captive to the Dolvi steel plant and the 302 km dedicated slurry pipeline with a take-or-pay contract hard-wires cargo into Jatadhar — that is why the 'partial monopoly and switching costs' check scores well and why volumes are described as sticky. But JSW Steel is the anchor customer across the portfolio: Jaigarh is roughly 85–90% captive, group cargo was still 54% of volumes in Q2 FY26, and both Stakeholder Concentration and Cyclicality are rated ELEVATED because volumes track JSW Steel's production and commodity price cycles. That was not theoretical — a 3.4 million tonne shortfall at the Paradip Iron Ore Terminal, when seaborne iron ore prices fell below economic levels, cut H1 FY26 growth from about 10% to 4%. Rising third-party share is the deliberate cure for exactly this. On the softer side, several patterns land only as PARTIAL: pricing power is real but narrow (a permanent rate increase at SWPL and Ennore worth ₹27 crore, per-tonne realisation up about 7%, port EBITDA margin to 53% from 52%), and the culture read is mixed despite a conservative balance sheet (net cash in FY25, net debt/EBITDA of 0.75x by September 2025), investment-grade upgrades from S&P and Fitch, an orderly CEO/CFO succession and candid disclosure of the Paradip miss. Add it together — 65 on business quality, 36 on multibagger fit, 56 on risk safety — and it scores 54 out of 100, a 'Decent' three-star business with SOUND resilience: a genuinely protected, genuinely growing asset whose returns on the capital it is pouring in remain unproven.

The rating is best read as a business doing the right things at the wrong ratio for now. The barriers, the market-share capture and the policy tailwind are all well documented across nine calls; what is missing is evidence that the enormous capex converts into returns, and independence from a single customer and a single policy-maker. Nothing in the record signals technological disruption, changing customer preferences or fashion risk — the threats that exist are concentration and capital intensity, not obsolescence.
