# Apollo Micro Systems (APOLLO) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Apollo Micro Systems is a Hyderabad-based Indian defence electronics company that has spent 40+ years designing and building the electronic "brains" that go inside Indian weapons — subsystems management calls Line Replaceable Units (LRUs) that sit onboard missiles, torpedoes, ships, submarines and ground launchers. Its main customer is India's government defence establishment: it develops systems for specific DRDO programmes, supports the testing and trial phase, and then earns most of its money manufacturing those systems in volume once a programme enters production, supplying DRDO and production agencies such as Bharat Dynamics. Management says it is present in essentially every indigenous Indian missile programme, contributing about 63% of subsystems across 80–90 DRDO programmes, and it is now moving up the chain from subsystem supplier to a "full-fledged weapon system manufacturer" that sells complete weapons and platforms under its own name. Business is almost entirely domestic India (revenue INR904 crores in FY26, up 61%, with an order book of INR1,432 crores at March 2026), with exports just beginning — a first export order, an INR113 crore UK order, and enquiries from the Middle East and Europe.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Subsystems and systems for missile / defence programmes (the core business)** — Designs and manufactures onboard electronic subsystems (LRUs) for DRDO missile and weapon programmes — from 4-km short-range missiles up to the 5,000-km Agni 5, plus QRSAM and Akash NG which are entering bulk production — and management describes this as the bulk of the company, contributing roughly 63% of subsystems across 80–90 programmes.
- **Naval and underwater weapons** — Builds underwater weapons and mines — heavyweight torpedo (limited series production done, bulk order expected), mini torpedo, limpet mines, MIGM and MOORED mines, the RGB-60 anti-submarine system, underwater communication and sensor suites — which management repeatedly flags as its most mature, closest-to-big-order area and a potential "game-changer."
- **Complete weapon systems and platforms (forward integration)** — The newer push to sell whole weapons rather than parts — loitering munitions (consortium with Redon Systems), a hard-kill anti-drone/swarm-drone rocket going to trials, and a full Grad rocket with an American partner — small today but the stated direction of the company under its "Vision 2036" goal of becoming a global OEM whose name is on the platform itself.
- **Aerospace subsystems** — Supplies critical subsystems for Indian combat aircraft programmes, including about five subsystems on LCA Mk2 and participation in AMCA; described as active participation rather than quantified as a large revenue line.
- **Explosives — IDL Explosives (via subsidiary Apollo Defence Industries Pvt Ltd)** — A 100%-acquired explosives business bought for INR107 crores, which has received an industrial licence for TNT and RDX; it is additional to the core business — guidance of 45–50% revenue growth explicitly excludes it — and a further acquisition through the same subsidiary was pending as of the May 2026 call.
- **R&D and manufacturing capacity** — Not a revenue line but central to how the company works: it spends about 7–8% of revenue on R&D (INR72 crores in FY26) developing things like fiber-optic-gyro inertial navigation and underwater autonomous vehicle sensor suites, and is scaling manufacturing through Unit 2 and the 350,000 sq ft Unit 3 (IPiDS) facility near Hyderabad airport, which management says is needed before it can take large foreign orders.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Apollo Micro Systems earns a middling overall grade of 60 out of 100 — "Decent", three stars — but that single number hides an unusually split picture. The business quality score of 58 and the risk-safety score of 50 sit well below the multibagger-pattern fit of 71, and the resilience read is "STRAINED". What the company is genuinely good at is technology and position: it scores top marks on technology advantage, on research and development (7-8% of revenue rising toward 9-10%, INR72 crore in FY26, producing named products such as the Mini Torpedo, fibre-optic-gyro navigation systems, underwater-vehicle sensor suites and RF seekers), and on the growth of its end market, where the government's indigenisation push is permanently moving defence electronics from imports and state-owned firms to qualified private suppliers. Operating margin is a healthy 24 rupees on every 100 rupees of sales and has been remarkably steady, moving only about two points a year. The competitive-advantage area scores a full 2.0 out of 2 — the highest of any area — while return on capital scores minus 1.0 and capital allocation is slightly negative. In short, this is a company whose competitive position is much stronger than its financial machinery.

The multibagger patterns confirm that strength, and they all trace back to a single trait: qualification. Three patterns are strong fits. "Toll Roads" fits because Apollo supplies small-value but mission-critical parts — line-replaceable units, seekers, fuzes, navigation systems — that are a tiny slice of a missile's cost yet cannot be swapped out once they have been approved for a programme. "Market Share Gainers" fits because that approved position has widened from a general claim of gaining share to a specific one of supplying 63% of subsystems across 80-90 programmes, backed by 61% revenue growth in FY26. "Forward Integrators" fits because management is visibly climbing from tier-2 parts supplier toward whole weapon systems, through the Unit 3 integration facility and the IDL Explosives purchase. That same qualification barrier is why the "Good Enough Goods" risk — being undercut by a cheaper generic rival — comes out LOW, and why "Cost to Replicate" registers as a partial fit through 41 years of programme approvals, production-partner status with DRDO and a lifetime manufacturing licence. But the very gatekeeper that creates the moat is also the largest risk: Government Dependency is rated HIGH precisely because every revenue driver — development programmes, purchase clearances, bulk-production orders, partner status, licences, even export permission — is a government decision. Stakeholder Concentration is ELEVATED for the mirror-image reason: being the trusted qualified supplier means orders arrive only when a handful of prime contractors choose to pass them down. The moat and the dependency are the same fact seen from two sides. Meanwhile the patterns that did not fit are equally telling — there is no recurring revenue, no evidence of pricing power (the margin rise from 19% to 24% is attributed entirely to the mix shifting from development work to repeat production, not to charging more), and the brand, while real, is a business-to-business reputation with no premium pricing behind it.

The weak half of the story is the balance sheet, and it connects straight back to the growth. Roughly 443 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and the working capital cycle, though improved from 626 days to 359, remains extreme. Every rupee of assets generates only 38 paise of sales. The consequence shows up starkly in the headline return on capital: for every 100 rupees of shareholders' funds and borrowings in the business, it generated minus 7 rupees of operating cash this year, even as the accounting return on capital employed was reported as rising from 10% to 18%. That gap between a rising paper return and negative cash generation is the working capital cycle. On top of this the company is spending heavily — INR150 crore on Unit 2 and Unit 3 phases funded partly by an INR110 crore term loan, plus additional land taking capacity to roughly eight times and eventually 12-13 times current levels — which is credited as genuine growth capex but has not yet earned its keep. The same build-out that supports the Forward Integrators and Market Share Gainers patterns is what makes Cyclicality a WATCH risk: revenue is lumpy, order-driven and programme-driven rather than a steady flow, and an analyst raised exactly this concern about capacity being built ahead of order timing. The IDL Explosives acquisition plays the same double role — it is core evidence for the Forward Integrators pattern, yet it scores negative on the acquisition check as a INR107 crore purchase of a weak seven-plant business needing a complete overhaul with outside consultants, one that dilutes group profit margins, with three more deals already queued. Growth has averaged about 24% a year but arrives unevenly. Management's credibility is a genuine offset: the 45-50% growth guidance was beaten at 61%, and the 15% profit target came in at 16%. The honest reading is a company with a real, defensible technical position in a structurally expanding market, currently consuming cash faster than it generates it in order to claim more of that market before deep-pocketed conglomerates — the New Entrants WATCH risk — arrive with technology transferred directly from DRDO.
