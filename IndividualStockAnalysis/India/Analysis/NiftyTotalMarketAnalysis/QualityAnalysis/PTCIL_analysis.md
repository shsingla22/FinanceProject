# PTC Industries (PTCIL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

PTC Industries (PTCIL) is a 60-year-old Indian manufacturer, based in Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh, that makes metal parts and the metal raw material they are made from, for applications where ordinary metallurgy will not do — aircraft and aero-engine parts, missiles, ships and submarines, artillery, industrial gas turbines, oil and gas, and even medical implants. It earns money by selling castings and components made from hard-to-work materials such as titanium, nickel and cobalt superalloys, high-strength steels and duplex alloys, and is now adding the sale of titanium and superalloy 'wrought' material itself (ingots, billets, bars, rods, slabs and plates). Management describes the company as technology-driven rather than product-driven: its stated rule ('parity dharma') is to enter only those metal-manufacturing technologies that India does not yet have, which it says keeps competition low and margins comfortable over the long run. It sells to Indian defence and space programmes (it already supplies titanium parts for BrahMos) and to foreign customers including Dassault, BAE Systems, Israel Aerospace and Honeywell, operating from its Advanced Manufacturing & Technology Centre in Lucknow plus a new 50-acre campus in the UP defence corridor about 5-6 km away.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Industrial castings, machining and assembly** — The original and currently operating business: air-melt, high-alloy, high-precision castings (using its replicast, rapidcast and investment casting technologies, plus heavy automation and robotics) together with machining and assembly, sold into oil and gas, LNG, petrochemicals, power generation, pulp and paper, food processing and marine — described as the segment the company has been running for the last 10-15 years.
- **Aerospace castings group** — Titanium castings, superalloy castings and 'controlled microstructure' castings (single-crystal, directionally solidified and equiaxed turbine blades) plus forge-cast and hot isostatic pressing — capabilities management says almost no other company or country has, that absorbed most of roughly Rs 350 crore of plant-and-machinery capex over the past seven to eight years, and that is now being scaled up in a new ~15,000 sq m building with furnaces targeted at over 300 tonnes a year of titanium castings and 300 tonnes a year of superalloy castings.
- **Aerospace materials group** — A newer, still-under-construction vertical of titanium alloy and superalloy melting mills (vacuum arc remelting, electron beam cold hearth melting, plasma cold melting for titanium; vacuum induction melting of about 600 tonnes a year for superalloys) to produce the raw wrought material — ingots, billets, bars, rods, slabs, plates — that India today imports at roughly 800-1,200 tonnes of titanium a year, a demand management expects to 'skyrocket' as Indian platforms enter production and as Western buyers look for an alternative to Russian and Chinese titanium.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

PTC Industries earns its high marks on what it can do that nobody else in India can. It is the only private titanium player in the country, and it holds capabilities — commercial-scale hot isostatic pressing, controlled-microstructure castings, electron-beam cold hearth remelting at scale — that essentially do not exist domestically, which makes it the default choice for work of that kind. That position was bought slowly: management spent seven to eight years and roughly Rs.350 crore building capability before the revenue arrived, deliberately accepting a suppressed return on equity and defending that choice openly when analysts pushed for quicker returns. Most of the programme was funded from money the business generated itself, with only about Rs.100 crore of borrowing, and leverage still fell sharply over the period (long-term debt to EBITDA from 3.0 to 1.64). The payoff shows up in price and mix rather than tonnage: revenue per kilogram rose about 80% from Rs.850 to roughly Rs.1,480 over six years, EBITDA per kilogram rose about 150% from Rs.150 to Rs.380, and margins widened from 18% to 29% on only modest volume growth. Sitting on top of all this is a certification wall — AS9100, NADCAP and customer-specific approvals from Safran, BAE, Dassault, Honeywell and IAI — where Safran approves a new casting company only about once every three or four years.

The multibagger screen reads the same facts back and lands on two firm fits and several tentative ones. Innovation Dominance is a strong fit because the whole case is research-led: a formal MRL1-to-MRL10 technology development process that produced rapid cast, forge cast, powder forge and single-crystal casting in-house, and turned them into actual orders from Safran, Dassault, BAE, IAI and Honeywell. Cost to Replicate is a strong fit for the mirror-image reason — the years, the self-funded spending, one of the world's five largest HIP presses, and multi-year qualification cycles per customer are exactly what a rival would have to repeat. Pricing power is only a likely fit, because the rising realisation is largely a shift in mix toward titanium and superalloys rather than proof that PTC can raise prices on the same part. Market share gain is likewise mostly ahead of the company rather than behind it: aerospace and titanium are just 15-20% of revenue today against an ambition of 70-80% within five years, with capacity going from 30-40 tonnes to 600 tonnes on the back of a fresh Rs.330 crore build-out on 50 acres. The company is also integrating in both directions — backward into its own metal melting, forward into sub-assemblies, including taking over BAE's UK welding and assembly line — and its cost discipline appears in what it pays for equipment (an EBCHR furnace for $2.5 million against an $80-90 million list price, a VAR unit for $0.35 million against $4-5 million) rather than in what it charges. Two patterns, Friendly Middleman and Brand Strength, are flagged only by the numbers with no supporting evidence from the calls, and recurring revenue is not there at all.

The risk review is where the same traits turn around and bite, which is why the overall rating is a middling 61 out of 100 ("Decent") and resilience is judged strained. The customer list that creates the certification moat — Safran, BAE, Dassault, HAL, BrahMos, DRDO labs, ISRO, MDL — is also a short list, so stakeholder concentration is rated high risk on the customer side, deepened by BAE physically moving its M777 assembly equipment into PTC's plant, and on the supply side by dependence on titanium sponge. The same names make the thesis heavily policy-driven — Atmanirbhar Bharat, defence budgets, the UP Defence Corridor, import negative lists and government grants — hence a high government dependency rating, with the deliberate build-out of foreign OEM business, targeted to match domestic defence revenue in three to four years, as the main offset. Because revenue tracks customers' capital programmes rather than day-to-day consumption, cyclicality is elevated. The heavy, specific process equipment that makes the business hard to copy is also the thing that could be stranded if aerospace parts start being made differently, which is why technological innovation sits on watch. New entrants are on watch too: an analyst pressed directly on Midhani, a cash-rich state producer already making about 300 tonnes of titanium that the government could expand, and management's answer was dismissive rather than substantive. Against this, demand is functional and specification-locked rather than fashion-driven, and the company deliberately targets technology-gap niches to avoid commoditisation. The weakest internal numbers line up with the same story: return on capital scores negative and capital allocation scores poorly, driven by roughly 970 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers — a long cycle that locks up capital in a business already spending heavily to grow.

### Forward Integrators — LIKELY FIT
*"Owns the shop, not just the product"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits and every one of the 2 computable checks supports the pattern.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** The investment thesis presented is almost entirely policy-driven: Atmanirbhar Bharat, defence budget allocations, the UP Defence Corridor, import negative lists, government R&D grants, and a customer list dominated by DRDO labs, BrahMos, HAL, VSSC/ISRO and MDL; even the medical opportunity is attributed to a government price ceiling forcing localisation. ### Stakeholder Concentration — HIGH RISK
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**What the calls show:** Results hinge on a handful of named relationships on both sides — Safran, BAE Systems, Dassault, HAL, BrahMos on the customer side (with BAE physically relocating its UK M777 assembly equipment into PTC), and on the input side management flags that titanium sponge comes almost entirely from Russia, CIS states, China, Japan and Saudi Arabia and calls the squeeze a future risk in plain terms.

## What to watch
