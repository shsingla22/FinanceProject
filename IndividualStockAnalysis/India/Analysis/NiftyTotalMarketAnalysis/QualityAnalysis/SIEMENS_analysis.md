# Siemens Ltd. (SIEMENS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

Siemens Limited is the India-listed arm of the global Siemens group (headquartered in Mumbai, with sales offices across Indian cities) that sells engineering hardware, software and turnkey projects for factories, buildings, power networks and railways. Its customers are Indian industrial companies (semiconductors, batteries, pharma, chemicals, food & beverage, steel, cement, automotive), data centre and commercial real estate developers, state power distribution companies (DISCOMs), and Indian Railways and metro authorities, plus the Siemens parent company which buys exported components from its Indian factories. It makes money by booking orders — product supplies, automation and electrification systems, and long-run projects with delivery and maintenance tails — and converting a large order backlog into revenue over time, so its fortunes track public infrastructure CapEx and private-sector CapEx cycles in India. Management describes itself as a 'One Tech company' that bundles software, automation and electrical hardware into a single solution, and after the demerger of its energy business into the separately listed Siemens Energy India Limited it now runs three segments: Digital Industries, Smart Infrastructure and Mobility.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Digital Industries** — Sells factory automation, motion control/variable frequency drives, process control (DCS) and manufacturing-execution software to industrial customers — the calls cite 20% energy savings for a metal producer and a paperless batch-record system for a pharma/chemicals maker — and management says its growth is driven by CapEx in electronics, semiconductors, batteries, e-vehicles, photovoltaics, chemicals, pharma and food & beverage.
- **Smart Infrastructure** — The electricals and buildings business — electrification and automation, electrical products, building management, fire safety and security, ring main units for DISCOMs, plus the e-mobility (EV charging) business boosted by the Mass-Tech acquisition — with demand described as growing strongly from data centres, commercial buildings, steel, cement and utilities under the RDSS scheme.
- **Mobility** — Supplies rail electrification and signalling (including TCAS across 3,000+ stations) where management calls itself a long-standing leader, plus rolling stock: the 9,000 HP locomotive contract for Indian Railways (1,200 locomotives over 11 years with 35 years of maintenance, first 40 delivered by March 2026 at over 90% localisation), metro electrification such as 58 km at Bangalore Metro, and a bogie/traction motor/gearbox factory at Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar that won an ₹18-billion export order from the parent for delivery over 2029–2039.
- **Energy (demerged)** — Until the demerger this was a major segment covering power transmission substations (AIS-to-GIS conversion, bulk power receiving stations) and generation equipment — order income rose 22% in FY2022 — but it has since been separated into the independently listed Siemens Energy India Limited and is no longer part of Siemens Limited.

### The story in depth

Siemens India comes out of the quality work as a genuinely strong business that is currently earning poorly. The framework gives full marks to the things that are hard to build: a technology stack spanning PLCs, control systems, digital twins, industrial edge, cybersecurity and industrial AI that is stitched into end-to-end offerings; a distribution and installed-base reach rivals cannot copy, covering most electricity distribution companies, half the metros and one in three PLCs; and end markets in multi-decade expansion — manufacturing rising from 15% to 25% of GDP, the energy transition, railway electrification and signalling, and data centres. Management scores well too: it quantifies bad news precisely (a 290 basis point EBITDA decline pinned to specific silver, copper and Euro moves), admits demand weakness and its inability to pass costs on, and has grown organically with only a tiny bolt-on, funding 25 localised factories, the Dahod locomotive plant and the Aurangabad bogie factory out of INR 21.6bn of operating cash rather than debt. The one glaring exception sits in the same column: for every ₹100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings in the business, it generated minus ₹4 of operating cash this year. That single figure is why the combined rating lands at 50 out of 100 — 'Decent', three stars — despite 68 out of 100 on business quality.

The multibagger screen scores only 25, and the reason is less that the traits are absent than that they are unproven in the records. Ten of eleven patterns come back PARTIAL or better but only one, Corporate Culture, is a STRONG FIT — earned by promises kept, from the December 2022 statement that Mobility was a strategic focus and the 9,000HP locomotive would be localised, through to the first contractual delivery of 40 locomotives at 90% localisation by March 2026. Everywhere else the underlying fact is present and the evidence stops short. The installed-base statistics imply toll-road economics — control content is a small slice of a plant's cost yet essential to running it — but management never frames it that way. Market share is claimed to be gained through a weak private capex cycle, with orders up 26.8% and revenue up 14.3% in H1 FY26 on a record INR 450bn backlog, but no share series against named competitors is given. Product and service revenue is a steady 69-70% of sales and the locomotive contract carries a 35-year maintenance tail after 11 years of delivery, yet attach rates, renewal rates and service revenue share are never quantified. Innovation is visible in Xcelerator scaling from 125 use cases to over 200 references, but R&D spend is never disclosed. The only outright NO FIT is Low Price Plus, and that is deliberate: management is pushing away from lowest-bidder tenders toward solution selling on flexibility, quality and speed.

The risk review shows the same traits cutting both ways, and rates overall resilience as STRAINED. The railway and grid work that anchors the Cost to Replicate pattern — the 11-year locomotive contract, the 35-year maintenance tail, the bogie plant with an INR 18bn export order running to 2039 — is also what makes Government Dependency the single HIGH RISK: Indian Railways, DISCOMs and the RDSS scheme, transmission tenders, PLI and customs duty decisions all sit with the state. The cyclical position is scored a strength because private capex was muted for three years and the first genuine upturn is now being reported, which is the same fact that makes Cyclicality ELEVATED — management frames demand almost entirely through customer and government capex cycles. Pricing power tells the clearest two-sided story: FY24 margin expansion was explicitly credited to better pricing and price enforcement, while the June 2026 call is the direct counter-example, with commodity and currency cost increases that could not be passed through — which is also what keeps Good Enough Goods at ELEVATED, since the tender model where the lowest party wins has not gone away. The German parent shows up twice, as supplier behind transfer-pricing-driven Digital Industries margins that collapsed to 2.6% on an 18% Euro move, and that is the core of Stakeholder Concentration. Against that, technological displacement, shifting preferences and fashion risk all read LOW, and new entrants show no signal at all — the pressures here are cyclical, political and cost-related, not competitive obsolescence. Industry Structure scores zero and Return on Capital negative; competitive advantage, at 1.67, is the strongest area by a wide margin.
