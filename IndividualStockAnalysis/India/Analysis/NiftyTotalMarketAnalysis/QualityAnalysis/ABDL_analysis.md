# Allied Blenders (ABDL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Allied Blenders and Distillers (ABD) is an Indian alcoholic-spirits company that makes and sells branded liquor — mainly whisky, plus brandy, rum, vodka and gin — under the "IMFL" (Indian-made foreign liquor) label, with 17 brands including its 35-year-old flagship Officer's Choice, ICONiQ White, Sterling Reserve and Kyron. Management describes it as India's third-largest spirits company by annual sales volume (31.7 million cases in FY24, 9 million cases in a single quarter by FY27), and says whisky is about 97% of its portfolio. Because liquor is a state subject in India, ABD must produce and sell within each state, so it runs 32 bottling units plus its own distillery and sells to whichever wholesaler the state allows (government-run or private), which then supplies roughly 79,000 retail outlets and on-premise venues — over 90% width of distribution; it also supplies the defence forces (CSD and paramilitary) and exports. Revenue comes from selling cases of liquor at a margin — FY26 consolidated income from operations was Rs 3,949 crore with a 14.4% EBITDA margin — and management is pushing two profit levers: shifting mix toward pricier "Prestige & Above" brands, and backward integration (its own PET bottle plant, malt distillery and ENA distillation) to cut input costs.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Prestige & Above (P&A)** — The premium end of the mainstream portfolio — brands like ICONiQ White, Officer's Choice Blue, Sterling Reserve B7 and Srishti — and the main growth engine: 47.2% of volume and 57.3% of value in FY26, up from about 25% of the mix back in FY18, with ICONiQ White alone at 10.7 million cases in FY26 and called the world's fastest-growing millionaire whisky brand.
- **Mass Premium and others** — The affordable, high-volume end led by Officer's Choice whisky — roughly flat to slightly declining in recent quarters, but management calls it the leader in India's Mass Premium whisky category, with gross margins over 40%, and describes it as the key cash-flow and profit generator that funds everything else.
- **ABD Maestro (Super-Premium to Luxury)** — A newer, deliberately "asset-light build, buy and partner" platform launched to enter the top-end market, with 10 brands across whisky, gin, vodka and rum (including Arthaus and Zoya gin) built in its first year FY26; still small in revenue terms, with the current focus on widening distribution to over 5,500 premium touch points, duty-free and six international markets.
- **International business / exports** — Selling its Indian brands abroad — expanded from 14 countries in mid-2024 to 39 countries by Q1 FY27, making it one of India's leading spirits exporters by volume, with Officer's Choice as India's No.1 exported whisky; management calls it asset-light, higher-margin than the domestic business and lighter on working capital per case, targeting 1 million cases in Africa by FY28.
- **Manufacturing and backward integration** — Its own production base — 32 bottling units, a distillery in Telangana (its largest market) and an R&D/blending centre in Aurangabad — now being expanded with a Rs 115 crore PET bottle plant (commissioned 2025, already EBITDA-accretive, 600 million bottles a year), a malt distillery and ENA distillation capacity; not a separate revenue line but management's stated route to roughly 300 basis points of EBITDA margin gain by FY28 and another 100 bps by FY29.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Allied Blenders earns a middling overall grade of 50 out of 100 — "Decent" — but the number hides an unusually clear internal logic: nearly everything the company does well and nearly everything that threatens it trace back to the same three facts. The first is a deliberate shift up the price ladder. Premium and above went from 25% of volume in FY18 to 37.3% in FY24 to 48.2% in Q1 FY27 and 59.3% of value, realisation per case rose 3.8%, and gross margin expanded 480 basis points to 48.2% in Q4 FY26. That one move is what earns top marks on pricing/mix/volume, intangible benefits (₹68 of every ₹100 of sales kept after direct inputs), market-share gains and structural end-market growth, and it is what makes the Market Share Gainers and Pricing Power patterns fit. The second fact is a distribution and manufacturing base that is genuinely hard to assemble — 79,000 outlets at over 90% width, ~500 feet-on-street, 32 bottling units, pan-India licences in a state-by-state regime, only four national players, plus new captive ENA and malt capacity. The third is that the whole business sells through a channel the government controls.

Those same three facts generate the risks. The move upmarket works because the mass-premium base underneath is eroding: the segment de-grew in H1 FY26, Officer's Choice needs active effort to "arrest decline", and three of the four millionaire brands have shrunk at high-teens volume rates over three years and now need blend refreshes, repackaging and celebrity campaigns. So the strength (successful premiumisation) and the risks (Shifting Consumer Preferences, Fashion Risk, and the failure of the Brand Strength pattern's durability test) are two readings of one event. Likewise, innovation is the strongest pattern in the whole screen — ICONiQ White reaching 10.7m cases by FY26, Zoya's flavour extensions at 30% of that brand's volume, 10 ABD Maestro brands in a single year, a 34-year malt over half pre-sold — but almost all growth now rests on that one trending brand, which is precisely why Stakeholder Concentration is rated HIGH RISK and why the Cost to Replicate pattern only half-fits: ABD's own ICONiQ proved how fast a new brand can go from launch to scale, which cuts both ways. The distribution moat is real, but a moat that the incumbent itself just vaulted is not a wall.

The state-control fact is the most double-edged of all. Licensing across a state-subject regime is part of what keeps the field to four national players, yet it also means excise committees, not the company, set prices — which is why Pricing Power is only a "likely" fit (the gains are mostly mix and input costs, not price rises) and why Toll Roads and Forward Integrators are outright no-fits, with no owned retail, no direct-to-consumer route, and only third-party duty-free and canteen channels. It also produces the report's two hardest numbers: about ₹400 crore of overdue receivables from state corporations and roughly 154 days of cash locked between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, the single worst check in the framework. Telangana alone was about 10 of 31.7 million cases and ~65% of PET consumption, and its receivable stretch from 60 to 150 days flattened FY24 volumes by itself. That is why return on capital scores zero and capital allocation scores low even though management is otherwise well-regarded: candid disclosure of the ₹24 crore supply-chain hit and the Telangana overdue, all-organic growth, leverage held inside stated guardrails (1.7x net debt/EBITDA against a <2x limit, 0.6x net debt/equity against 0.75x), two rating upgrades and borrowing costs down 140bps, and a heavy growth capex programme with stated returns — a ₹115 crore PET plant adding roughly ₹30 crore of annual gross margin, backward integration worth ~300bps by FY28. In short: capable operators running a genuinely improving portfolio, on a balance sheet whose cash conversion is hostage to a customer they cannot invoice-chase. Hence the "STRAINED" resilience label — the profit engine is working while the cash engine is not.

### Low Price Plus — PARTIAL
*"Cheapest — and still the best"*

### Stakeholder Concentration — HIGH RISK
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

## What to watch

- Telangana receivables: whether the ~₹400 crore overdue and the 60-to-150-day stretch clear — this single item drives both the HIGH RISK Government Dependency call and the -2 score on the 154-day working-capital cycle.
- ICONiQ White's concentration: whether a second brand reaches meaningful scale, or growth stays dependent on one name — the crux of the HIGH RISK Stakeholder Concentration verdict and the reason Cost to Replicate is only a partial fit.
- The three declining legacy millionaire brands: whether blend refreshes, repackaging and the celebrity campaigns actually arrest the high-teens decline — this is the specific durability test the Brand Strength pattern is currently failing, and the core of Fashion Risk.
- Source of margin gains: whether gross margin keeps expanding on genuine price increases rather than mix and input costs alone — the stated reason Pricing Power is rated LIKELY rather than STRONG, given price rises need state excise approval.
- Payoff on the backward-integration capex: whether the PET plant, malt and ENA distilleries and the ~300bps by FY28 actually show up in returns — capital allocation and return on capital are the two lowest-scoring quality areas despite the disciplined-stewardship marks.
- Local and state-created competition: whether Maharashtra Made Liquor-style entrants and "the rise in local brands in certain regions" spread beyond a couple of states — currently only a WATCH, but it presses directly on the mass-premium core flagged under Good Enough Goods.
