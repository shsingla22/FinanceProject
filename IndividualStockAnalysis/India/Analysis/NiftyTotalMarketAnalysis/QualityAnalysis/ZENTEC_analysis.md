# Zen Technologies (ZENTEC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Zen Technologies is a Hyderabad-based Indian defence equipment maker that sells military training simulators (such as tank and crew gunnery simulators) and anti-drone/counter-drone systems, plus related hardware like remote-controlled weapon stations, surveillance systems and, more recently, an unmanned ground vehicle and a cyber security suite. Its customers are armed forces — mainly the Indian Army and the Ministry of Defence, with growing sales to Navy and Air Force and to export markets in the Middle East, Africa, CIS countries and the Americas. It makes money in two ways: one-off sales of equipment (the bulk of revenue) and recurring annual maintenance contracts (AMC) on equipment already installed, working off an order book that it executes over roughly a 12-month cycle. Manufacturing and R&D are in Hyderabad, India, and the group is debt-free with about Rs.1,217 crores of cash as of June 2026 and a consolidated order book of Rs.1,239 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Simulators / training and simulation** — Computer-based training systems that let soldiers practise driving, gunnery and combat drills without live ammunition or field ranges — the company's original and self-described leadership business, boosted by a September 2021 Government of India push to shift conventional training to simulators, and still winning large orders such as a Rs.177.5 crore Ministry of Defence order in July 2026 for upgrading tank and crew gunnery simulators.
- **Anti-drone systems (counter-drone)** — Systems that detect, track and neutralise hostile drones using radar (about 20 km range, 15+ km detection), RF and GNSS jamming plus 'hard kill' gun options; management calls Zen among the top players and says the government placed roughly Rs.800 crores of anti-drone orders after Operation Sindoor, and expects this to become the mainstay of the business in two to three years.
- **AMC business** — Recurring annual maintenance revenue from servicing the installed base of equipment — Rs.35.90 crores of Rs.161.44 crores standalone revenue in FY23, and Rs.318.4 crores of the Rs.1,239 crore order book at June 2026, so a steady but smaller slice that management expects to grow as more equipment is delivered.
- **Exports** — Sales outside India, focused on the Middle East, Africa and CIS countries with a planned push into North and South America (Argentina, El Salvador mentioned); exports were Rs.55.5 crores of the Rs.125.54 crores of FY23 standalone equipment sales, roughly a 45/55 split with domestic.
- **AI Turing (associate company) — remote controlled weapon stations and surveillance** — An acquired/associate business making remote-controlled weapon stations (RCWS) and surveillance systems that management says are 'sought-after' by armies and address an estimated USD 1 billion market in India alone, with a much larger market abroad.
- **ARI and ANAWAVE (subsidiaries)** — Group companies that have developed newer products including a six-layer cyber security suite for command centres, ships and aircraft, and a dedicated anti-drone simulator for training operators to counter single-drone and swarm attacks — described as major new products but with no revenue size given.
- **Vrishabh (unmanned ground vehicle)** — An unmanned ground vehicle for combat, logistics and casualty evacuation — 150 kg payload, 50 kmph, 100 km range, over 85% indigenous content — launched at an event in Prayagraj in May 2026 with commercial launch planned in FY2027, so it contributes nothing to revenue yet.

### The story in depth

Zen Technologies is, at heart, a research-and-development business that owns what it sells. Three decades of self-funded, independently developed intellectual property — it turned down DRDO co-development money and works on four-to-five year horizons — show up in the numbers as a very high gross margin: about 80% in FY21-22 and roughly 73% since, with 72.9% in Q1 FY2027 described as broadly consistent, because 65-80% of a contract's value is software and IP rather than bought-in parts. Those margins pay for R&D that management calls a structural commitment and keeps raising even in a bad year, and that R&D keeps turning into named products — anti-drone systems, Barbarik/RCWS, the Vrishabh unmanned ground vehicle, 30mm ammunition, HyperStrike. This is why the quality framework scores technology advantage, R&D and end-market growth at the top of its scale and gives full marks to competitive advantage and customer benefits, and why the multibagger screen's single strongest verdict is Innovation Dominance. Management candour is the other standout: a five-factor bridge for the FY26 margin miss, disclosure of a fire loss and of provision reversals that had flattered an earlier quarter, and open admission of missed guidance and failed R&D projects.

The interesting part is that the same traits that create the strengths also create most of the risks. The technology edge is real, but in counter-drone it decays fast: management says its own 2021 product was two generations behind by 2024 and that 400MHz-6GHz jamming coverage is already outdated in 2026, which is why the technological innovation risk sits on watch even though the innovation pattern is the strongest fit. The cost-effectiveness pitch that supports the Low Price Plus pattern — a $10,000 interceptor against $30,000 Shahed drones, a close-in weapon system at a much lower price point — is the same pitch that feeds the Good Enough Goods risk, because sales run through government tenders where price is a decisive criterion. The battle-proven reputation after Operation Sindoor, worth roughly Rs.800 crore of anti-drone orders, is genuine credibility, but it is credibility with one buyer class, so Brand Strength only partially fits while stakeholder concentration is rated elevated and government dependency high. Selling straight to the Ministry of Defence through tenders is what rules out the Friendly Middleman and Toll Roads patterns entirely, and it is also what makes revenue hostage to when the government publishes tenders. Market Share Gainers is a likely fit on management's claims of being number one in India and expanding into Navy, Air Force and North America/EU, but no rising share figure is ever given and FY2026 revenue fell.

The weak side of the record is what happens between the order and the cash. Consolidated FY2026 revenue fell 29.4% to Rs.687.7 crore from Rs.973.6 crore, Q4 was down 45.2% year on year, and about 425 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. That combination is why growth persistence and the cost of working capital both score at the bottom of the quality framework, why the growth and return-on-capital areas score lowest of all, and why cyclicality is rated high risk — the swings come from lumpy defence capital procurement, not from customers' day-to-day operations. Capital allocation is only middling for related reasons: the capex story is growth-oriented (US manufacturing, supply-chain ramp, HyperStrike mass-production capacity) but never quantified, and the build-out happened into a 29% revenue fall, while a Rs.1,000 crore QIP earmarked for acquisitions sits alongside bolt-ons that have so far been small and complementary. Against that, the balance sheet is debt-free with Rs.1,217 crore of cash, R&D spending continued through the trough, and an AMC book of Rs.318.4 crore within a Rs.1,239 crore order book gives a slowly compounding annuity-like layer as equipment leaves warranty at 8-11% of contract value. Resilience is judged sound. The combined score of 52 out of 100 — 63 on business quality, 49 on multibagger fit, 34 on risk safety — lands at Decent: an excellent engineering business whose customer, cash cycle and order timing are all outside its control.

New entrants risk: another credible domestic challenger landing an order Zen expected — management was already unaware of Paras' competing interceptor drone — would undercut both the Market Share Gainers pattern and the Cost to Replicate barrier.

- ✅ High retained margin (76% of sales) alongside 28% yearly growth — the profitable-innovation flywheel: margins fund the spending that drives the growth.

**What the calls show:** Revenue is tied to lumpy defence capital procurement rather than customer operations, and the swings are severe: consolidated FY2026 revenue fell 29.4% to Rs.687.7 Cr from Rs.973.6 Cr, Q4 FY2026 was down 45.2% year-on-year, and EBITDA margin fell from 40.9% (Q1 FY26) to 27.3% (Q1 FY27) purely on negative operating leverage — after FY2023-FY2025 when management guided to a '50% average CAGR over the next three years' at the peak of the order cycle.

> "the revenue trajectory in FY2026 has been impacted by the timing of order inflows with the bulk of the orders received over the past several months scheduled for execution from FY2027 onwards" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Growing annuity-like AMC book (Rs.318.4 Cr of the Rs.1,239 Cr order book) and continued countercyclical R&D spend through the down year*

## What to watch

- Cyclicality risk: whether revenue recovers from the FY2026 fall to Rs.687.7 crore, or whether a second down year turns lumpy growth into a genuine decline against the persistence-of-growth check.
