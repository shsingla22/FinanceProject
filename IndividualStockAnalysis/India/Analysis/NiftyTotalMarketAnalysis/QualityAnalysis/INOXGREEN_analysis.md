# Inox Green Energy Se (INOXGREEN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Inox Green Energy Services is an Indian company that looks after wind farms (and now solar plants) after they are built — it does the day-to-day operations and maintenance, servicing turbines and keeping them running, with machine availability of about 96.5% as its headline quality measure. Its customers are the owners of these power projects: mostly buyers of turbines made by its parent Inox Wind (which owns about 56% of it and signs O&M contracts of 20-25 years that pass to Inox Green), plus third-party asset owners it wins through tenders and by buying up other service providers. It earns steady, annuity-like service fees tied to the size of the portfolio it maintains, so revenue grows as more megawatts are added — the portfolio went from about 90 MW at the start to roughly 3,050 MW in early 2023, 3.5 GW in late 2024, and over 13 GWp by 2026 — and management describes the business as asset-light with high margins (Q3 FY26: income of Rs.112 crore, EBITDA of Rs.53 crore, PAT of Rs.25 crore). It operates across wind and solar sites all over India and is part of the Inox GFL Group, whose other companies (Inox Wind, Inox Clean Energy) keep feeding it new projects to service.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Wind O&M (organic / captive portfolio from Inox Wind)** — Long-term maintenance contracts on turbines supplied by parent Inox Wind, which flow automatically to Inox Green for 20-25 years once commissioned — this is the core of the business and the main engine of portfolio growth, with the parent targeting several gigawatts of execution that will add to it.
- **Third-party / inorganic O&M** — Servicing wind assets that Inox Wind did not build, won either through tenders or by acquiring existing service providers — starting with a 51% stake in I-Fox Windtechnik (230 MW, bought at about a 4x multiple and said to have roughly doubled in size and profit within 12 months) and later investments to take over 6.5 GW of operating wind O&M assets from two large companies.
- **Solar O&M** — A newer addition of about 3.3 GWp of solar maintenance work, picked up from KEC International and group company Inox Clean Energy, taking the company beyond pure wind.
- **Value-added and refurbishment services** — Extra paid work on top of routine maintenance — refurbishment of equipment and in-house/localised manufacture of critical spares like control systems and gearboxes, which management says both adds revenue and cuts costs and supply-chain risk.
- **Power evacuation / substation (common infrastructure) business** — The transmission substations and shared infrastructure at wind sites, historically capital-heavy and sitting on the balance sheet; it contributes only about Rs.3-4 crore of quarterly revenue but carried roughly Rs.16-17 crore of quarterly depreciation, and is being demerged into group company Inox Renewable Solutions (approved by NCLT by mid-2026) to leave a cleaner, asset-light balance sheet.
- **Own wind power assets (SPVs) — being exited** — Legacy special-purpose vehicles that owned wind capacity, such as the 50 MW Nani Virani project, which the company said in 2023 it was selling down to become net-debt-free and move to an asset-light model.

### The story in depth

Inox Green sells one thing: the promise that a wind turbine keeps running. Machine availability is the headline number every quarter and it directly determines what the customer earns, which is why the Assurance benefits check scores at the top of the range (availability held at 96.4-96.5%). Around that sits a genuinely strong competitive position. The company runs long maintenance contracts attached to turbines its parent sells — roughly Rs 8-10 lakh per megawatt per year on ten-year terms with a 5% annual price step — and the serviced portfolio has grown from 90MW a decade ago to 3.05GW, then to 13.3GWp by February 2026. That is why two patterns come out as LIKELY FIT: Recurring Revenue and Market Share Gainers. Two more strengths reinforce it. Inox Green controls the shared evacuation substations at its sites, which ties customers in for 20-25 years and is the single concrete barrier behind the PARTIAL Cost to Replicate and Toll Roads readings, and it has a pan-India footprint of 84 substations and dozens of sites with its own technician pipeline — reach that matters because the service has to be delivered on the ground. The end market keeps expanding, and every turbine installed creates a multi-decade maintenance obligation, so the pool of future work grows structurally. Much of the growth thesis is explicitly about picking up what failed rivals leave behind: bust turbine makers and sub-scale service providers whose contracts are available for capture.

The uncomfortable part is that the same features producing the good patterns also produce the risks. The contracts that make revenue annuity-like arrive automatically from the parent's commissionings — 'married to us for the next 20 years or 25 years' — and by June 2026 management said two-thirds of annual execution for the next stretch comes from that channel. So the Recurring Revenue pattern and the ELEVATED Stakeholder Concentration risk are the same fact seen from two sides, and the ELEVATED Cyclicality verdict follows the same logic: the existing book is steady, but portfolio growth is entirely a derivative of how much new capacity the parent installs. The demand base underneath all of it is created by policy — central bidding programmes, the 450 GW target, FDRE awards — which is why Government Dependency is the one HIGH RISK on the list; the structural end-market growth the quality framework rewards is the same government-driven expansion the risk review flags. The substation lock-in is real, but it does not fully protect the service itself: turbine maintenance is judged good-enough-goods work, increasingly won through competitive tenders from sophisticated buyers who can switch to cheaper independent providers, which is exactly why Pricing Power and Low Price Plus come out only PARTIAL — the 5% escalation is a contractual step, not evidence of the ability to raise prices at will. The company's answer to the independent-provider threat has been to become one, buying I-FOX and building multi-brand servicing, and that is why New Entrants scores LOW. Brand Strength, Innovation Dominance and Corporate Culture also land at PARTIAL — group provenance and marquee customers rather than a consumer brand, incremental named innovation rather than a research lead, kept promises rather than a long record. Friendly Middleman shows no fit at all, and Forward Integrators is the opposite of what the group is doing, since it is moving backward into cranes, transformers and power electronics and away from turnkey work.

Where the story breaks down is in the numbers the business actually produces. Return on capital is the weakest area in the whole framework: for every Rs 100 of shareholders' funds and borrowings, the business generated Rs 4 of operating cash this year. About 216 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and getting paid by customers, which is a long time to fund. Sales have gone backwards by about 3% a year on average and the year-to-year path is lumpy, so the portfolio growth described in the calls has not yet shown up as consistent revenue growth. At the group's reported level, Rs 8 of every Rs 100 of sales is operating profit and that figure has swung by roughly 18 points a year, pointing to important costs management does not control — a very different picture from the roughly 50% margins described for the contracts themselves. Capital allocation and management both score slightly negative. The I-FOX deal is a genuine bright spot, bought at 4x with profitability doubling in a year, but the far larger 6.5 GW purchase of two companies is transformational rather than a simple bolt-on and was still incomplete across both February and June 2026 — and that same deal is what the growth and diversification case leans on. Add it up and the picture is a strong competitive position and a structurally attractive end market sitting on top of weak returns, a heavy working capital load, and dependence on a parent and a policy regime. The combined score is 39 out of 100 — 45% weight on business quality at 54, 30% on multibagger fit at just 16, and 25% on risk safety at 40 — giving a Mixed, two-star rating with resilience assessed as sound. It is a good business that has not yet been a good use of capital.

## Section 2 — Does it look like a long-term winner? (11 multibagger patterns)

### Recurring Revenue (Product Upfront) — LIKELY FIT
*"Sells once, earns forever"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern, but the numbers push back (1 of 3 checks supporting) — held one step below a full confirmation.

**What the calls show:** Core model is multi-year O&M annuities attached to each turbine sold by parent Inox Wind — 10-year average contracts with 5% annual escalation, ~Rs 8-10 lakh/MW/annum, ~50% EBITDA margins, portfolio compounding from 2.8GW (Dec 2022) to 13.3GW (FY26); management repeatedly frames it as 'multi-decadal' annuity revenue. Weaker on the customer-funding leg: first two years are free O&M (cash only from year three) and group working capital ran 200-210 days, so it is not a prepaid/negative-working-capital model.

> "the current average length of typical O&M service contract is 10 years... we have an escalation of 5%" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ About 216 days of cash is tied up in the trade cycle — customers are not funding the business.
- ✅ Capital spending is about 1% of sales — growth needs little capital.
- ❌ Operating cash was positive in 6 of 9 years — not fully consistent.

### Market Share Gainers — LIKELY FIT
*"Winning customers from rivals"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern, but the numbers push back (0 of 1 check supporting) — held one step below a full confirmation.

**What the calls show:** Persistent, explicit portfolio share gain across the whole period: 90MW to 3.05GW over a decade, 3.5GW in Nov 2024 against a 6GW FY26 target, and 13.3GWp by Feb 2026 including 6.5GW of acquired third-party OEM assets, with management claiming India's largest renewable O&M position. The caveat is that much of the step-up was bought via acquisitions and captive group volumes rather than won competitively, and the FY26 acquisitions were still not consolidated as of Jun 2026.

> "Inox Green's portfolio stands at 13.3 gigawatt... This also includes the investments which we have made to acquire 6.5 gigawatt of operational wind O&M assets of two major companies" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ Sales compounding at -3% a year — below the 12% pace the share-gainer pattern looks for.

> "to ensure longevity of the business and to ensure we have solid control over the one, two, three years and then typically people stick with you" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ Operating margin of 8% — too thin for a protected franchise.

> "We have indigenized several critical spare parts and equipments, including control system, gearbox... resulting in significant cost savings" — *management, on an earnings call*

*"Raises prices, keeps the customers"*

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** The entire demand base is policy-created and repeatedly framed that way across all four calls: the Feb 2023 call opened on the government replacing reverse auctions with 8 GW of annual central bids to 2030 and the 450 GW target; Nov 2024 cited 12 GW of FDRE awards and PSU tenders (NLC order via I-FOX); Jun 2026 credited 'strong push from the government' on domestic manufacturing — and the company is now seeing payment delays specifically in PSU contracts, exactly the low-margin/slow-pay government-contract pattern.

> "On the receivable front, there has been some challenges, especially in our PSU contracts, where we have seen payment delays." — *management, on an earnings call*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 2 numeric fingerprints reinforce it.

**What the calls show:** The core O&M business is a flow/operations product — 10-year contracts with 5% annual escalation, ~50% EBITDA margins and 96.4-96.5% machine availability sustained across FY23-FY26 — so revenue is annuity-like; but portfolio growth is entirely a derivative of wind capex additions, and the Feb 2026 call showed customer site-readiness delays deferring turbine offtake and forcing the group to abandon megawatt guidance.

> "we have witnessed delays at the customer sites resulting in postponement of off-take of wind turbines, which is beyond our control. This is something which most of the industry participants are facing currently" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/operations-linked demand: 10-year O&M contracts with 5% escalation on an installed base, plus stated group strategy to 'insulate from market cycles'*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 2 of the last 8 years with swings of ±29 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from 4% to 53% (swinging ±17.9 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
