# Aegis Vopak Term (AEGISVOPAK) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aegis Vopak Terminals (AVTL) rents out storage space at Indian ports: it owns and operates big tank farms and cryogenic tanks that hold LPG (cooking/industrial gas), petroleum products, chemicals and other liquids on behalf of other companies, and management calls it India's largest independent (third-party) tank storage terminal operator for LPG and liquids. Its customers are oil marketing companies, chemical, fertilizer and industrial firms — for example an exclusive terminalling agreement with HPCL at Haldia running to 2038, and a 15-year take-or-pay contract with Hindustan Zinc for ammonia at Pipavav. It makes money by charging fees for storing product and for the volume (throughput) that moves through its tanks, jetties, pipelines, rail gantries and truck bays, so earnings track capacity and utilisation rather than the price of the goods stored. It operates only in India, across ports at Kandla, Pipavav, JNPA/JNPT, Mangalore, Kochi and Haldia, with a proposed new site at Vadhvan, and is a joint venture between Aegis Logistics and Royal Vopak of the Netherlands. In FY26 it earned revenue of INR 923.1 crores, operating EBITDA of INR 686.5 crores and net profit of INR 341.9 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Gas Terminaling** — Cryogenic and pressurised LPG storage terminals at Pipavav, Kandla, Mangalore and Haldia — the slightly larger of the two divisions at INR 482.6 crores of FY26 revenue (up 8.6%), with gas throughput rising from 3.3 million tons in FY25 to 3.9 million tons in FY26, and LPG static capacity grown 4.5x since the JV was formed in November 2021.
- **Liquid Terminaling** — Tank storage for petroleum products, chemicals and other liquids across all six ports — INR 440.5 crores of FY26 revenue, up 27.8% and described by management as the fastest-growing segment, running on about 1.7 million cubic metres of capacity (3.75x growth since the JV was formed) with most sites at high utilisation.
- **Ammonia terminaling (new product line)** — India's first independent ammonia terminal, 36,000 metric tons at Pipavav, under a 15-year take-or-pay deal with Hindustan Zinc's upcoming DAP fertiliser plant — not yet earning revenue but positioned as the entry into the green hydrogen/energy-transition chain, with ITOCHU of Japan taking an initial 10% stake in the ammonia subsidiary (rising to 25%) and a non-binding MoU with L&T for ammonia terminals at Kandla.
- **Hindustan Aegis LPG (HALPG) — East Coast LPG** — A 75% stake acquired during FY26 in an LPG terminal with an attached bottling plant at Haldia, adding about 25,000 metric tons of LPG capacity and marking the company's entry into the East Coast market, anchored by an exclusive terminalling agreement with HPCL through 2038.
- **LPG bottling and evacuation infrastructure** — Supporting facilities that make the terminals usable — a 35,000 metric tons per annum LPG bottling plant being built at JNPA, LPG and liquid rail gantries at Pipavav and Mangalore, 16 truck loading bays, VLGC-capable jetties and pipeline hook-ups (KGPL, JLPL) that management says are the main trigger for higher gas volumes ahead.
- **Project GATI (Gateway Access to India) expansion pipeline** — The company-wide build-out programme — roughly USD 1.2 billion of cumulative capex by next year and a planned USD 5 billion by 2030, including INR 1,675 crores at JNPA, the 94,148 cubic metre CRL4/TRL4 terminal at Kandla, added capacity at Kochi and Mangalore, and a non-binding MoU worth about INR 20,000 crores for the proposed Vadhvan port.

### The story in depth

Aegis Vopak Terminals scores 67 out of 100 (Strong, four stars), and the story behind that number is unusually consistent: the company owns hard-to-copy physical positions and rents them out. Its best-scoring areas are customer benefits (1.25) and industry structure (1.0), and both come from the same source — around 25 storage units across seven ports, with pipelines, rail gantries, 16 truck bays, VLGC-capable jetties and on-site bottling plants that take the customer's biggest cost, transport, out of the picture. That same physical footprint is what makes three of the eleven multibagger patterns a strong fit at once. It reads as a Toll Road because terminalling is a small but essential link in a customer's supply chain, priced at roughly ₹1,200 a ton for gas and ₹2,500-3,000 per CBM for liquids at about 74% EBITDA margins, with only three terminals connected to the KGP pipeline. It reads as Cost to Replicate because port land is finite and leased, cryogenic tanks take 18-24 months to build and another 15-18 months to connect to rail and pipeline, and pipeline hook-ups need regulatory approval. And it reads as a Market Share Gainer because liquid capacity is up 3.75x and LPG 4.5x since the November 2021 joint venture, six ports are going to seven with a stated target of twelve by 2030, helped by three bolt-on purchases from sub-scale operators buckling under stricter safety and environmental law — what management calls a flight to quality.

The uncomfortable part is that the very thing creating the moat also creates the weakest scores and the biggest risk. Building barriers out of concrete and steel means the balance sheet is enormous relative to sales: every ₹1 of assets currently produces about ₹0.11 of revenue, with FY26 revenue of ₹923 crore sitting against roughly ₹10,000 crore of cumulative capex. That single trait explains the framework's worst area, return on capital at -0.67, and it is the flip side of the Cost to Replicate pattern rather than a separate problem — a competitor would have to spend the same way to arrive. It also feeds the cyclicality concern: capex is planned to rise from about $1.2 billion to $5 billion by 2030, which is a long spending commitment against a business where gas throughput was hit by roughly half in a single month. And because all of that steel sits on leased government port land and depends on state decisions the company does not control — regulatory approvals, pipeline commissioning timelines, lease renewals — government dependency is the one risk rated high. In short, the asset base is simultaneously the moat, the reason returns look poor today, and the reason the company is exposed to landlords and regulators.

The remaining patterns and risks pair up the same way. Brand strength and forward integration are both likely rather than certain: the reputation is real business-to-business credibility from Royal Vopak's safety and environmental standards, explicitly cited as winning work from multinationals, but there is no consumer franchise or premium price attached to it — which is exactly why the good-enough-goods risk stays on the watch list, since sophisticated buyers such as oil marketing companies and traders can still move on price. That shows up in the numbers: pricing power is only partial because gas realisation actually fell from around ₹1,500 in FY25 to roughly ₹1,175-1,200, and the liquid improvement from ₹2,250 toward ₹2,500 is attributed to product mix and new terminals rather than to raising prices. New entrants are a live question too — an analyst asked directly about growing interest in chemical and gas storage, and management named competitors sitting on the same Kandla pipelines. There is also a deliberate trade-off worth naming: recurring revenue is only a partial fit because management prefers spot, open-source terminalling over locking in long contracts, and that same preference is precisely what keeps customer concentration at a watch rather than a high risk. On management, the framework is lukewarm at 0.6 and culture is only a partial fit, but the record so far supports them — IPO money fully repaid ₹2,016 crore of bank debt, the Pipavav and Mangalore cryogenic terminals were commissioned on schedule, the ammonia terminal came in ahead of time, and gearing is conservative at 0.6x. Overall resilience is judged sound, with technology change, shifting preferences and fashion risk all rated low.

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Every element of the business is fixed infrastructure sitting on government port land held on lease, and the value of that infrastructure depends on state decisions the company does not control — PNGRB approvals, GAIL/IHB pipeline commissioning timelines (KGPL, JLPL, Mangalore-Hassan-Cherlapalli), port authorities enabling VLGC berthing, land allotments and tenders, plus demand drivers like the national green hydrogen mission and PSU counterparties (HPCL, IOC).

## What to watch

- Gas realisation: it has already slipped from about ₹1,500 to ₹1,175-1,200 a ton. Further drift would turn the partial Pricing Power fit into confirmation of the Good Enough Goods risk — that this is a tariff service buyers switch on price.
- Asset turn as capex scales: ₹0.11 of sales per ₹1 of assets is what drags return on capital to -0.67. If revenue does not rise faster than the spend on the way from $1.2 billion to $5 billion by 2030, the Toll Road economics never reach the shareholder.
- Government and regulatory timelines: lease renewals on port land, pipeline hook-up approvals and the commissioning of the pipeline grids are outside the company's control and sit behind the single High Risk rating (Government Dependency).
- Competitors on the same infrastructure: management itself named rivals connected to the Kandla pipelines. Any new hook-up approval granted to them would erode the Cost to Replicate barrier and validate the New Entrants concern.
- Contract mix versus spot: 15-year take-or-pay deals and the exclusive agreement to 2038 are described as the exception, not the norm. A shift either way changes both the Recurring Revenue pattern and the Stakeholder Concentration risk at the same time.
