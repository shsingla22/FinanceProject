# Bombay Burmah Trading Corporation Ltd. (BBTC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: CONSUMER GOODS*

The Bombay Burmah Trading Corporation (BBTC) is a very old Indian company (its 158th annual meeting was held in 2023) that runs a handful of small, unrelated operating businesses and also sits on large shareholdings in other companies. Its own operations are tea plantations in South India and Tanzania, a coffee plantation business (sold off during FY23), a factory business making auto electrical components, and a division selling dental products such as alloys to dental colleges and clinics. Revenue from these operations was about Rs.335 crores in FY2022-23 (Rs.374 crores the prior year), while on a consolidated basis sales were Rs.16,303 crores, up 14% — the difference coming almost entirely from its controlling stake in the biscuit maker Britannia, which is consolidated into its accounts and also pays it dividends (Rs.70 per share mentioned in FY23). It also holds stakes in other Wadia group companies, and its FY23 consolidated result was a loss of Rs.533 crores because it had to write off Rs.1,866 crores of exposure to associate airline Go Airlines (GoAir), which went into insolvency in May 2023.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Tea (plantations)** — Grows and sells tea from estates in South India (Mudis is named) and in Tanzania; production was 37.38 lakh kilos in FY23 versus 41.72 lakh kilos the year before, with output swinging on weather, and it is one of the two oldest core operating businesses.
- **Coffee (plantations)** — Grew and processed coffee (639 metric tonnes in FY23 plus 135 tonnes of bought coffee, sold at about Rs.280 a kilo), but management said returns were very low relative to the value of the land, and the business was divested for Rs.291 crores to pay down the company's secured debt.
- **Auto electric components (Electro Mags)** — Makes and sells electrical components to the Indian and export auto-parts market, with turnover of Rs.151.7 crores in FY23 against Rs.133 crores in FY22 — the largest of the company's own operating divisions and the one management points to for growth.
- **Healthcare / dental products** — Sells dental products, including alloys, to dental colleges and clinics; turnover rose to Rs.29.17 crores in FY23 from Rs.19.41 crores as COVID restrictions lifted, and better pricing made it notably more profitable — small in size but the fastest-growing division.
- **Investments in group companies (Britannia, GoAir, National Peroxide)** — Holds shares in other companies — most importantly Britannia, whose consolidation and dividends dwarf everything else on the group's accounts; associate GoAir caused a Rs.1,866 crore consolidated impairment in FY23 after filing for insolvency, and National Peroxide became an associate that year.
- **Land and property** — Owns land, including plantation estates, that it occasionally sells to raise cash — an equity land sale contributed Rs.80.77 crores of FY22 revenue — and shareholders repeatedly asked about the size and value of this land bank.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Bombay Burmah earns a "Decent" 54 out of 100, and the arithmetic behind that grade tells the story: 53 for business quality, only 39 for how well it fits a multibagger profile, but 72 for risk safety. The day-to-day engine genuinely works. Sales have compounded at roughly 8% a year with very consistent year-to-year growth, ₹18 of every ₹100 of sales drops through as operating profit with margins moving only about ±2.6 points a year, and the business turns ₹100 of capital into ₹28 of operating cash. Best of all, customers effectively fund the operations: cash comes back eight days before suppliers need paying, a rare structural strength. On the scorecard this shows up as the only positive areas — Growth at 0.8 and Return on Capital at 1.0.

The problem is that none of this comes from a defensible position, and the same trait that produces the cash also produces the risk. Every product line — bulk tea, coffee, auto components, dental consumables — is discussed by management purely in per-kilo or per-unit price terms, which is exactly why the Good Enough Goods risk is flagged and why Pricing Power, Low Price Plus, Market Share Gainers and Cost to Replicate all come back as NO FIT. Prices move with commodity and input markets, not with the company's own strength: tea fetched ₹5 more per kilo but volumes fell, coffee realisation swung from ₹186 to ₹280, and dental price increases were simply alloy costs passed on. That same commodity exposure drives the ELEVATED Cyclicality risk, since results are explained through weather-driven crop swings — tea production fell from 41.72 to 37.38 lakh kg on excessive rain. The one bright spot, the auto-electric components division growing from ₹133 crore to ₹151.7 crore on new-product output, is simultaneously the basis for the PARTIAL Innovation Dominance read and the reason the New Entrants risk is on WATCH — management itself says the growth is attracting domestic and foreign competitors, with no discussion of what that does to pricing. Likewise, the 157-year heritage and Wadia/Britannia association only earn a PARTIAL on Brand Strength, because that equity sits in the listed investee rather than in BBTC's own operations — the same reasoning that produced a -2 on the Halo Effect check alongside a ₹533 crore consolidated loss. Competitive Advantage, Customer Benefits and Industry Structure all score a flat 0.0.

What pulls the whole picture down is capital allocation, and the three analyses converge on one event. Group subsidiaries funded an airline outside the core; that associate's dependence on a single engine maker grounded half its fleet, forcing a ₹1,865.65 crore impairment that flipped ₹808 crore of consolidated profit into a ₹533 crore loss, brought a qualified audit opinion, and leaves a further ₹210 crore still to come. That single decision explains the -2 on Mergers and Acquisitions, the -2 on Disciplined Stewards, the ELEVATED Stakeholder Concentration risk, and the NO FIT on Corporate Culture — where blame was placed entirely on the engine maker with no reflection on the decision to commit the capital. It also shapes how cash is being raised: shareholders asked for a capex roadmap at both AGMs and never got an answer, while the actual funding story is selling assets (₹80.77 crore of land, ₹291 crore of coffee estates) and issuing ₹300 crore of debentures two years running to repay borrowings — hence the negative mark on capex for growth. The coffee exit cuts both ways: it removes a low-return, weather-exposed business, but management's own reason for leaving — lacking the scale to compete — is the clearest evidence that these operations would not be hard for a funded newcomer to replicate. Management scores -1.17 and Capital Allocation 0.0. Resilience is still judged SOUND and the exposure has been fully provided for, which is why risk safety scores 72 and the overall verdict lands at Decent rather than lower.

The honest read: a cash-generative, steadily growing collection of commodity businesses with no pricing power, wrapped in a holding structure whose stewardship record is the single biggest reason not to pay up for it.
