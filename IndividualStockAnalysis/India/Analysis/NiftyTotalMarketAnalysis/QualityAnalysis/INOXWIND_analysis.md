# Inox Wind Ltd. (INOXWIND) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

Inox Wind makes large wind turbines in India and sells them to companies that build wind power projects — independent power producers, commercial and industrial buyers, state-owned firms like NTPC, and named customers such as Aditya Birla, Amplus/Gentari, Jakson Green, First Energy and Leap Green, plus its own group company Inox Clean Energy. It earns money in two ways on each project: selling the turbine equipment itself, and, where the customer wants it, building the whole wind farm end-to-end (foundations, erection with cranes, evacuation infrastructure and commissioning) — the order book is currently split about 50:50 between these two, with management wanting equipment-only supply to rise toward 75%. After the farm is running, its 56%-owned listed subsidiary Inox Green earns recurring fees for operating and maintaining wind and solar assets. It operates only in India, with factories in Himachal Pradesh and Gujarat (including a nacelle and hub plant at Kalyangarh) and a new blade and tower plant coming up in the south; consolidated revenue was guided at over Rs. 5,000 crore for FY26 at 20-22% EBITDA margin, up from roughly Rs. 1,500 crore-scale annual revenue in FY24.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Wind turbine manufacturing and equipment supply** — Designs and builds wind turbine generators — the 3.3 MW machine launched in FY24 and a new 4.4/4.45 MW model due to launch commercially in calendar 2026 — and sells them to project developers; this is the core of a 3.1-3.2 GW order book that gives more than 24 months of execution visibility.
- **Turnkey / EPC project execution** — Builds complete wind farms for customers, covering foundations, erection, cranes and commissioning (for example a 50 MW farm handed over to NTPC in Gujarat), running across more than 25 sites for over 15 customers with each contract carrying a different scope; turnkey is about half the order book and management is deliberately shrinking it because it ties up more working capital.
- **Inox Green Energy Services (O&M subsidiary, 56% owned)** — Separately listed subsidiary that services and maintains wind and solar plants for a fee, growing from a 3.2 GW portfolio in 2023 to 13.3 GWp (about 10 GW wind, 3.3 GWp solar) by early 2026 including a 6.5 GW acquisition still awaiting approvals — small in revenue (about Rs. 110-130 crore a quarter) but high margin, cash-generative and described as on track to be India's largest renewable O&M company.
- **Inox Renewable Solutions (IRSL) — evacuation infrastructure / substations** — The substation and power-evacuation business being demerged out of Inox Green and merged into IRSL (approved by NCLT Ahmedabad, listing pending), which becomes the group's EPC arm and removes roughly Rs. 1,000 crore of gross block and Rs. 50-55 crore of annual depreciation from Inox Green.
- **Backward integration into components** — In-house manufacture of cranes and transformers, with a stated push into power electronics, which management credits for the step-up in margins to the 20-22% EBITDA range.
- **Group linkage with Inox Clean Energy (INOXGFL Group)** — Not owned by Inox Wind but the group's power-producing and solar-module arm, planning 3 GW+ of renewable capacity a year (20-30% of it wind) toward 14 GW by FY29 — expected to supply upward of 1 GW a year of repeat orders and eventually two-thirds of Inox Wind's annual execution alongside CESC.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

The case for Inox Wind rests almost entirely on timing and end-market direction, and on those two counts the record is genuinely strong. The company was picked up close to the bottom of its cycle — years of losses, a sector that had effectively shut down in FY23, inventory that sat 'stuck on our balance sheet for years' — and the upturn since has been real: a record 6 GW year nationally, with 8-10 GW of annual additions expected, supported by state renewable policies, renewable generation obligations for coal plants, ALMM for wind, a GST cut on wind components from 12% to 5%, and a shift toward round-the-clock and hybrid power that management argues raises wind content per tender. Against that backdrop, execution has scaled from roughly 100 MW a year historically to 143 MW in the first half of FY24 to about 350 MW in the first half of FY26, with roughly 1.2 GW guided for FY26, revenue above Rs 5,000 crore and FY27 guided up 75%. Capital spending has been for growth rather than upkeep — a new nacelle and hub plant at Kalyanghar, a new blade and tower facility in South India, and backward integration into cranes, transformers and power electronics — and margin guidance has been raised four times, from 18-19% to 20-22%, helped by a roughly Rs 6 lakh per MW royalty on the 3 MW platform rolling off. The FY24 promise to become net-debt-free was kept and the company still reports net cash. Growth and customer-benefit scores are the only two positive areas in the framework.

The trouble is that none of this has yet turned into money coming back. For every ₹100 of shareholders' funds and borrowings in the business, this year produced negative ₹8 of operating cash — a -8% cash return on capital, the single worst-scoring check. Every ₹1 of assets generates only ₹0.36 of sales, and about 443 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. These are not separate complaints: the same asset base that funds growth and forms the only articulable barrier — roughly 1,800 MW of pre-built common evacuation infrastructure, a land bank, multiple plants, in-house cranes and transformers — is exactly what makes the model asset- and working-capital-heavy and keeps cash returns negative. The 'Cost to Replicate' pattern and the two worst quality checks are the same fact seen from opposite ends. The same pairing runs through the rest of the screen: not one of the eleven multibagger patterns scores a full fit — seven are partial and four do not apply — because in each case the strength and the flaw sit together. There is a genuine product-then-annuity engine in Inox Green, whose operations-and-maintenance book has grown from 3.2 GW in 2023 to over 13 GWp by FY26 at roughly 50% EBITDA margin and 96.5% machine availability, but it was built from turbines the group itself sold. There is a real product cadence — 3.3 MW type-certified in FY24, a 4.4 MW machine due for launch in CY2026 — but no research spending is ever disclosed, and the company's own 2 MW platform went obsolete on its books because, as management put it, 'we have not upgraded that technology.' Share is clearly being taken, but no share figures against named competitors are ever given. Pricing power, low-cost positioning, toll-road economics and channel influence do not apply at all: orders are won in competitive tenders, margin gains are credited to the royalty expiry, integration and mix — never to raising prices — and profit margins have swung by ±24.7 points, which is what a business without pricing protection looks like.

The risk review points at the same traits a third time. Cyclicality is high risk because revenue is equipment sold into other people's capital spending — the very cycle position that scores the framework's best mark is also the exposure, cushioned only partly by the recurring operations-and-maintenance stream. Government dependency is high risk because demand is created almost entirely by the policy list that makes the end market attractive in the first place; the tailwind and the dependency are one item. Stakeholder concentration is high risk because of the June 2026 pivot: two-thirds of annual execution for the next four to five years is expected to come from Inox Clean and CESC, and Inox Clean sits at the promoter level. That single arrangement pulls down the stewardship check, undercuts the corporate-culture pattern, and hollows out the market-share and forward-integration patterns at the same time — turnkey work is being cut back from what was the entire book, partly because vertically integrated conglomerate buyers are building their own execution capability, which is also why new entrants are on watch alongside Adani's arrival with a 5.1 MW turbine, larger than anything Inox has launched or announced. Stewardship is further strained by perpetual group restructuring and by four managing directors or chief executives and three chief financial officers between FY21 and FY26. The combined rating reflects this arithmetic honestly: business quality scores 45 out of 100, multibagger fit scores zero because nothing is a full pattern match, and risk safety scores 8 — a weighted 22 out of 100, a Weak one-star grade with resilience judged stressed. This is a company well positioned in a growing market that has not yet demonstrated it can convert that position into cash returns, and whose newest commercial arrangement concentrates its future in two hands, one of them related to the promoters.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration, Fashion Risk. The balance sheet and cash engine shows multiple financial stress signals — 2 of the 3 stress checks are flagged — multiple warning lights at once.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.2× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ⚠️ Only about -43% of recent operating profit became cash (was -9%) — profits are getting stuck in paper, an early stress signal.
- ⚠️ Returns on capital slid from about 23% to 9% — the business is earning less and less on the money invested in it.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 3 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Revenue is wind turbine equipment sold into customers' capacity-expansion capex, and the record spans a full boom-bust: several years of losses and inventory 'stuck on our balance sheet for years when the sector shut down', ~100MW/yr operations for five years, then 5x scale-up, EBITDA swinging from -Rs18.7cr (Q2FY23) to +Rs333cr (Q4FY26), with guidance repeatedly re-based (megawatt guidance abandoned in Feb 2026, working capital guidance moving 120→200-210 days). Management itself frames the goal as insulation 'from market cycles'.

> "for the past 5 years, we have really been cleaning up inventory which was stuck on our balance sheet for years when the sector shut down" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Inox Green O&M subsidiary is an operations-linked, recurring flow business (13+ GWp portfolio, ~50% EBITDA margins) that cushions the equipment cycle; group IPP framework agreements aim for ~1GW of recurring annual orders*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 5 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from -48% to 21% (swinging ±24.7 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 53% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 17% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Demand is created almost entirely by policy: state RE policies (Gujarat, Rajasthan), renewable generation obligations, uniform green tariff guidelines, ALMM for wind, GST cut from 12% to 5%, CERC connectivity/GNA amendments, PSU tenders and SECI-style auctions — and the fragility showed when ~40GW of PPA-less projects faced cancellation/rebidding and when PSU contracts delayed payments. Ordering depends on PPA signing and financial closure, i.e. on government counterparties.

> "On the receivable front, there has been some challenges, especially in our PSU contracts, where we have seen payment delays" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Growing C&I and private IPP mix plus the captive group IPP reduces reliance on central/state tender awards, and the recurring O&M annuity is less policy-sensitive*
