# Bharti Airtel Ltd. (BHARTIARTL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: TELECOM*

Bharti Airtel is an Indian telecom operator that sells mobile phone service, home broadband and TV, and communications services to businesses, in India (including through its listed subsidiary Bharti Hexacom) and across Africa. It makes money mainly from monthly/prepaid subscription fees from hundreds of millions of retail customers — management tracks average revenue per user (ARPU), which rose from Rs.183 in mid-2022 to Rs.259 by late 2025 — plus contracts with enterprises and government bodies, and fees from newer digital businesses like payments, lending, data centres and cloud. It runs the underlying network itself, spending heavily on spectrum licences, mobile towers/5G sites and fibre, which makes it capital-intensive but high-margin (FY2026 EBITDAaL margin ~51%). FY2026 consolidated revenue was about Rs.2,11,000 crores, split roughly India mobile 52%, Africa 29%, India non-mobile 13% and Indus (passive tower infrastructure) 6%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Mobility (India mobile)** — Prepaid and postpaid mobile voice and data, now including 5G (181 million 5G customers and ~74% population coverage by Q3 FY2026) plus international roaming; the single largest business at about 52% of group revenue, with growth coming from feature-phone-to-smartphone and prepaid-to-postpaid upgrades and periodic tariff increases.
- **Homes / broadband** — Fixed home internet delivered both by fibre-to-the-home and fixed wireless access (FWA), available in 1,300+ cities; a consistent growth engine — 13 million connected homes and a record 1.2 million net adds in the December 2025 quarter, with FWA alone over 3 million customers.
- **Digital TV (DTH) and IPTV** — Satellite pay-TV plus a fast-growing IPTV service bundled with broadband under the 'Airtel Black' converged plans; the DTH industry has been shrinking under regulation and free content, so management is now prioritising profitable growth here while IPTV carries the growth.
- **Airtel Business (B2B)** — Connectivity and IT services sold to companies and government — leased lines, SD-WAN and secure networking (e.g. a multi-year deal as the Central Board of Direct Taxes' connectivity partner), plus CPaaS messaging, IoT connections, cyber security and cloud; described by management as 'a jewel in the portfolio' and India's #1 B2B player, though revenue growth has been lumpy and the global/wholesale piece was muted through 2024.
- **Airtel Payments Bank** — A digital bank/fintech for everyday payments and deposits, called the only profitable fintech player in India; grew from 44.4 million monthly transacting users and a Rs.1,100 crore revenue run-rate in 2022 to about 120 million users, a ~Rs.3,400 crore run-rate and Rs.4,600 crore of deposits by Q4 FY2026.
- **Airtel Finance / Airtel Money (lending)** — Consumer lending built on Airtel's customer data and app, run for three years as a loan service provider (partnering with lenders) at a monthly disbursement run-rate over Rs.550 crores; subsidiary Airtel Money Limited received RBI approval as a non-deposit-taking NBFC and is moving to commercial launch — one of three named 'new growth bets'.
- **Nxtra (data centres)** — The group's data-centre arm, targeting 1 gigawatt of capacity over the next few years and raising $1 billion from outside investors alongside Airtel; also the flagship for the company's renewable-energy and ESG claims.
- **Airtel Cloud** — A 'telco-grade sovereign cloud' offering launched as the third new growth bet — early stage, with 24 customer deals signed by end-FY2026 and more in the pipeline.
- **Africa operations (Airtel Africa)** — Mobile, data and Airtel Money services across African markets, about 29% of group revenue and growing faster than India in constant currency; the board approved a share swap to buy an additional 16.3% stake, with the growth case resting on low tele-density (~45%), ~52% smartphone penetration and a young population.
- **Indus Towers / passive infrastructure** — The shared mobile-tower (passive infrastructure) business, reported separately and roughly 6% of group revenue; management usually quotes India numbers 'excluding passive infra' so the underlying service business is comparable.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Bharti Airtel comes out of all three reviews as a Strong business, rated 68 out of 100 (four stars), built from a business-quality score of 74, a multibagger-pattern fit of 63 and a risk-safety score of 64, with overall resilience judged SOUND. The strength is concentrated in the people running it and what customers get: management scores highest of all the quality areas, on the back of a leadership team that admits its own misses without being asked (a late fixed-wireless launch, an unsatisfactory Rs.3 ARPU move, insufficient diversity), that cut borrowings from 2.52x to 1.02x of earnings while prepaying Rs.24,250 crore of expensive spectrum dues, that funded growth out of its own cash, and that handed the CEO job to an internal candidate. The business itself turns Rs.100 of capital into Rs.35 of operating cash a year, sells a service with almost no direct input cost, and is taking share in every segment it competes in. The weaker quality areas are the industry it sits in, its return on capital and its capital allocation record — which is where the risk review picks up the thread.

The patterns that fit best are the ones the quality checks already pointed at, and they mostly come from the same two traits. The first is a premium brand that customers will pay more for: average revenue per user went from Rs.183 in 2022 to Rs.211 in 2024 to Rs.259 by the December 2025 quarter while the company was still adding 4.4 million paying customers in that same quarter — raising price and growing the base at once is the real test of pricing power, and the Airtel name has been stretched into banking, lending, cloud, data centres and bundled home plans without breaking. The second is that its competitors are weak: a distressed third mobile operator, a state-owned incumbent and a structurally shrinking DTH and cable industry are handing over share quarter after quarter, which is why the market-share pattern reads as a likely fit rather than a claim. Five further patterns — recurring revenue, low-cost operation, innovation, forward integration and cost to replicate — score only partial, and in most cases the substance is visible but the numbers are not: management never gives renewal or attach rates, never quantifies research spending, and never discloses store counts, so the case cannot be closed. Two patterns, the friendly-middleman and toll-road models, simply do not apply. That disclosure gap, more than any weakness in the business, is why the pattern limb scores lowest of the three.

The risks are largely the same facts read from the other side. The single high risk is dependence on government, and it is inseparable from what makes the company hard to copy: the spectrum pool, fibre and licences that no rival could rebuild cheaply are the same assets the state prices and controls, evidenced by a Rs.43,040 crore auction commitment in FY23, a further Rs.6,850 crore in FY25, the Rs.24,250 crore of dues prepaid, and a DTH business management itself says is held back by regulation. The Rs.24,250 crore prepayment is therefore both the best proof of disciplined stewardship and a reminder of who sets the bill. Pricing power carries a matching risk: connectivity is a largely undifferentiated pipe that a cheaper rival can supply well enough, management's whole answer is a premium experience defence, and it keeps conceding the industry needs Rs.300 of average revenue per user — above today's Rs.259 — which leaves pricing partly hostage to whether the whole industry raises tariffs. Share gains in DTH are being taken from a market that is itself shrinking as viewing moves to streaming, and each new network generation forces the installed base to be moved again. Against that, three risks are genuinely low: revenue is a monthly subscription flow rather than a cyclical order book, with 16 straight quarters of sequential growth from Rs.32,805 crore to Rs.55,400 crore, India margins up from 51% to 52%, and Rs.41,500 crore of operating free cash flow in FY26; it is spread across hundreds of millions of retail customers and a broad enterprise book (Africa 29%, India mobile 52%, India non-mobile 13%, Indus 6%); and demand is functional and repeat rather than fashion-driven.

## What to watch

- Industry-wide tariff repair: if average revenue per user stalls near Rs.259 and never approaches the Rs.300 management says the industry needs, both the Pricing Power pattern and the Good Enough Goods risk get worse together.
- The next spectrum or licence cycle: any large new state-set payment would test whether net debt of 1.02x of earnings and the Disciplined Stewards check survive the Government Dependency risk.
- DTH and linear TV: whether IPTV adds and the 29 bundled streaming platforms keep outrunning the structural decline flagged under Shifting Consumer Preferences, or the segment starts subtracting.
- Africa, now 29% of revenue: the Geographical Expansion check rests on the India playbook transplanting successfully, so a stumble there removes the main offset to the Government Dependency risk.
- Disclosure: renewal and churn data, research spending or store economics would move Recurring Revenue, Innovation Dominance and Forward Integrators off partial — their absence, not weak evidence of the business, is what caps the pattern score at 63.
