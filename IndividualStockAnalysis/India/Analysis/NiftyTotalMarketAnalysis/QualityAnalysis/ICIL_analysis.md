# Indo Count Inds (ICIL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Indo Count Industries (ICIL) is an Indian textile manufacturer that makes home textiles — mainly cotton bed linen and bed sheets, plus fashion, utility and institutional bedding, and more recently curtains, towels, bath rugs and blankets. It sells overwhelmingly business-to-business to large global retailers (big box chains and omni-channel/e-commerce platforms) in over 50 countries, with the United States as its biggest market and roughly 30% of core revenue now from non-US markets; a small domestic India business sells under its own brands. It earns money by converting cotton into finished made-ups at its plants in Maharashtra and Gujarat (India) and, since FY26, at three pillow/quilt factories in the United States, and by selling under owned and licensed consumer brands. Volumes are measured in metres of fabric — 94 million metres in FY26 — and revenue was roughly flat in FY26 (management guides to about INR 5,500 crore in FY27 at around 13% EBITDA margin), with US import tariffs the main swing factor.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Core business (home textile / bed linen exports)** — The original and still dominant business — manufacturing and exporting bed sheets and bed linen from India to global retailers; it has won India's TEXPROCIL gold trophy for the highest cotton made-ups exports six years running, and management expects it to bring in about INR 4,000 crore of the INR 5,500 crore targeted for FY27, though it declined by a low-teens percentage in FY26 because of US tariffs.
- **Fashion, utility and institutional bedding** — Higher-value bedding categories the company pushed into from around FY19 onwards to move beyond plain sheets and lift margins; management describes them as central to growth and to winning a bigger share of each retailer's spend.
- **New business — US utility bedding manufacturing** — Three factories in the United States making pillows and quilts, started only about 16-17 months before Feb 2026, with capacity raised from 13 million to 31 million pillows and 1.5 million quilts a year after a new greenfield plant in Kernersville, North Carolina; existing units run at about 65% utilisation and management expects roughly USD 175 million of revenue from this over the next few years.
- **New business — brands (owned and licensed)** — Selling under consumer brand names rather than as an anonymous supplier: the 180-year-old US brand Wamsutta, bought outright for INR 85 crore in 2024 and relaunched, plus six licensed brands including Fieldcrest, Waverly, GAIAM, Jasper Conran and Tommy Hilfiger; together with US utility bedding this 'new business' was 20% of Q3 FY26 sales, INR 792 crore in FY26, and is targeted to roughly double in FY27.
- **Domestic India / B2C and D2C business** — Own-brand sales inside India under Boutique Living (also called Boutique Clothing) and Layers through about 2,000 retail touch points and digital marketplaces; small at roughly 2.25% of FY26 revenue but an area management wants to build.
- **Acquired GHCL home textile unit** — The home textile business bought from GHCL Limited in 2022, which management said made Indo Count the largest global bed linen company and which it now runs as a single combined manufacturing operation rather than reporting separately.
- **Spinning division** — A yarn spinning unit at Gokul Shirgaon, Kolhapur listed among the company's facilities, feeding into the home textile operation; the calls give no separate size for it.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Indo Count comes out of all three reviews as a genuinely large, well-run manufacturer that has not yet turned its size into durable pricing power. The quality framework gives it credit for growth-driven spending rather than mere upkeep — 25,000 spindles and Rs.270 crore in FY23, a build-out to 153 million metres of capacity, a new North Carolina pillow plant taking capacity from 13 million to 31 million pillows, and a further Rs.250 crore outlay — alongside share gains (six consecutive TEXPROCIL gold trophies, a rising Indian share of US bed-linen imports, no customer lost through the tariff shock) and a 15% cash return on capital. Management scores well for consistency: the value-added and branded shift has been argued since 2022 and the goal of doubling revenue by 2028 was reaffirmed through the crisis. But the same framework marks down the cost of working capital hard, with roughly 215 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and marks down pricing, because growth has come from volume and capacity rather than price. Area scores tell the same story: growth is the strongest suit at +0.67, while industry structure (-0.33), capital allocation (-0.2) and competitive advantage (0.0) are the weak ones. The combined rating is Mixed, 38 out of 100, built from 54 on business quality, 25 on multibagger fit and 26 on risk safety, with resilience judged STRAINED.

The multibagger screen and the risk review keep pointing at the same handful of facts from opposite directions, and that is the heart of the story. Five of the eleven patterns are only partial fits and six do not fit at all. Scale and low-cost supply — Indian cotton, free-trade agreements, the China+1 sourcing shift — give a partial fit on Low Price Plus and count as a structural tailwind in the quality checks, yet that same cost-and-policy foundation is exactly why Cost to Replicate fails (capacity is buyable, brands are licensed, and the advantage rests on a policy variable) and why Government Dependency is flagged HIGH RISK: export incentives booked as income, repeated reliance on trade deals, and then a 50% US tariff that hit the numbers directly. Similarly, the market-share win that earns a positive check is also the reason Market Share Gainers is only partial and Pricing Power fails outright — share in FY26 was defended by absorbing the tariff, with margins falling from 13.8% to 11%, volumes from 106.4 to 94.1 million metres, and profit after tax halved. Because the buyer is a big-box retailer that controls the shelf and negotiated tariff-sharing case by case, the screen also rules out Friendly Middleman and Toll Roads, and the risk review calls the private-label core Good Enough Goods, its second HIGH RISK. Roughly 70% of core revenue coming from the US through a narrow set of retailers keeps Stakeholder Concentration on WATCH, and the same order-to-order export model, with working capital as a large cash absorber, means Recurring Revenue does not apply at all.

What the company is doing about this is visible but unfinished, and that is why the verdict lands in the middle rather than at either end. The deliberate move upmarket — Jasper Conran, the Wamsutta acquisition, licences across six brands including Fieldcrest, Waverly, GAIAM and Tommy Hilfiger, domestic Boutique Living and Layers across around 2,000 touch points, plus D2C and e-commerce — gives partial credit on both Brand Strength and Forward Integrators and is named as the silver lining against the Good Enough Goods risk and the shift in consumer preferences. But the screen notes the heritage is bought or rented rather than built, and no own-store count, franchise royalty or D2C revenue figure is disclosed. Likewise, geographic diversification to about 30% non-US revenue, presence in 50+ countries and three US plants is the stated answer to both Government Dependency and customer concentration, but it is not yet large enough to change where the power sits. On the positive side of the cycle, the framework's strongest single mark is that the company sits at an identifiable trough with a visible upturn: FY26 margins bottomed at 11% under the tariff, the US trade deal and EU FTA have removed the overhang, and guidance points to 30%+ recovery — the same cyclicality that the risk review rates ELEVATED, given the FY23 de-stocking episode and the FY26 volume fall. Corporate culture is a partial fit, helped by prompt disclosure of bad news (including a quantified Rs.9.2 crore labour-code hit), Rs.200 crore of debt reduction, long-tenured finance leadership and an ESG score rising from 45 to 78, though prepared remarks lean heavily on awards. Innovation Dominance does not fit at all — there is no R&D spend, pipeline language or named innovation-led product across sixteen calls — so the upside case rests on capacity, brands, policy and the cycle turning, not on anything the company alone controls.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Good Enough Goods, Cyclicality, Technological Innovation, New Entrants. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.6× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 75% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ⚠️ Returns on capital slid from about 41% to 13% — the business is earning less and less on the money invested in it.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Corporate fortunes are visibly set by policy across the whole period: RoSCTL export incentives booked as income (Rs.50 crores of prior-period benefit in Q1FY22), repeated reliance on FTAs (Australia, UAE, UK, EU, Japan) and China+1, and then a 50% US tariff plus a Russian-oil penalty that drove a low-teen decline in core revenue, a 100bps sequential EBITDA hit and PAT falling from INR250cr to INR127cr in FY26 — with the FY27 recovery guidance explicitly resting on the US trade deal and EU FTA being implemented.

> "Despite operating in a challenging 50% tariff environment, we delivered a stable performance" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Geographic diversification underway — non-US now ~30% of core revenue, presence in 50+ countries, US-based manufacturing (three utility bedding plants) that sits inside the tariff wall, plus a small domestic branded business.*

### Good Enough Goods — HIGH RISK
*"Cheaper rivals that are good enough"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 2 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** The core is largely private-label bed linen sold to retailers who control the shelf and the label, with little end-consumer brand awareness — exactly the intermediary-controlled structure that lets cheaper suppliers win; pricing power is demonstrably weak, with tariff and penalty costs absorbed rather than passed on, consumers down-trading, and EBITDA margin falling from ~19.5% (Q1FY23) to 9.5% (Q3FY26) against a still-unreached 15-16% ambition. The entire multi-year strategy — value-added mix, brand acquisitions, licensing — reads as a defence against commoditisation.

> "a part of the tariff impact and the Russia oil -related penalty impact was absorbed to protect customer relationships and preserve market share" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Deliberate escape upward — owned brand Wamsutta, six licensed brands, value-added and premium mix, US-based manufacturing for proximity/responsiveness, and scale leadership (largest global bed-linen player, sixth consecutive TEXPROCIL gold trophy) that a subscale challenger cannot easily match.*

- ✅ The share of each sale kept after direct input costs held near 56% (was 47%) — no gross-level price erosion.
