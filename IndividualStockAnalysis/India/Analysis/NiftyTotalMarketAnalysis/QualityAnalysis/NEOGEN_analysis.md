# Neogen Chemicals (NEOGEN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Neogen Chemicals is an Indian specialty chemicals maker that has spent about three decades building chemical-synthesis know-how, and it manufactures bromine-based and lithium-based compounds that other manufacturers use as ingredients in their own products. Its customers are mainly pharmaceutical and agrochemical companies, plus flavours-and-fragrance makers and other specialty users, and more recently lithium-ion battery cell manufacturers in India and abroad. It makes money by selling these chemicals in bulk from its own plants (Dahej and a new greenfield site at Pakhajan in Gujarat), passing swings in raw-material, freight and packaging costs through to customers so that its absolute profit is protected even when reported revenue moves with lithium and bromine prices. Sales are mostly domestic with a meaningful export share (roughly 74:26 domestic-to-export in one quarter cited, versus 56:44 in an earlier one), and consolidated quarterly revenue has grown from about Rs. 148 crore in Q1 FY23 to about Rs. 250 crore in Q1 FY27, with management guiding to Rs. 950–1,050 crore standalone for FY27.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Organic Chemicals** — The larger of the two reported segments — bromine-based and organo-lithium specialty compounds and intermediates sold mainly to pharma (including API makers), agrochemicals and flavours & fragrances; it brought in Rs. 194 crore of the Rs. 250 crore consolidated revenue in Q1 FY27, so roughly three-quarters of the business.
- **Inorganic Chemicals** — Lithium-based salts and related inorganic products, where reported revenue swings a lot with lithium prices (down 24% in one quarter purely on price, then up 145% and 158% year-on-year in the FY26–FY27 quarters); it contributed Rs. 57 crore in Q1 FY27, roughly a fifth to a quarter of sales.
- **CSM and Advanced Intermediates** — Custom synthesis and contract manufacturing of higher-value, multi-stage molecules made to specific customers' requirements, historically tied to pharma and agro but being extended into non-pharma, non-agro uses such as flavours and fragrances; management treats it as a value-added growth push rather than a separately sized reporting segment, and says its progress has been held back by weak agrochemical demand.
- **Neogen Ionics / battery chemicals** — The subsidiary building the company's lithium-ion battery materials business — electrolytes and lithium electrolyte salts and additives for battery cell makers — using Japanese technology partnerships (Mitsubishi's MUIS technology and an equity joint venture with Morita, which is putting in USD 20 million); it is still small in revenue (Rs. 36 crore in FY26 and Rs. 19 crore in Q1 FY27) but absorbs very large capital spending (Dahej Phase 1 budgeted at Rs. 428 crore and Pakhajan Phase 2 at Rs. 1,367 crore) and management calls it the most important strategic pillar for future growth.
- **BuLi Chemicals** — An acquired organo-lithium (butyl lithium) operation that is consolidated into the group and is described as adding incremental revenue on top of the base business; Q1 FY27 was called the highest-ever quarterly revenue for the organo-lithium portfolio.

### The story in depth

Neogen Chemicals earns its keep on trust rather than price. The quality framework's strongest marks go to customer assurance (score 2) — buyers who cannot even test the impurity profile of what they are receiving pay Neogen for batch-to-batch consistency and security of supply, and have told management they will pay formula prices above the alternative. Steady operating margins of about ₹16 on every ₹100 of sales, moving only about ±1.6 points a year, point to costs under control and few new competitors crowding in. That same trust is what carries the two 'partial' pattern fits: Toll Roads, where the lithium salt is a small part of the customer's total cost but reliability matters enormously and qualification audits run for years, and Brand Strength, where 25-30 years of consistent batches means the real competition is foreign incumbents rather than local copycats. The company is also gaining ground — claimed 60-75% of India's specialty lithium carbonate imports, chemistry-and-services mix rising from roughly 10% to 15-18% — and sits in front of an end-market in clear secular expansion, with Indian battery demand projected to move from 33 GWh to 92 GWh by 2027 and past 200 GWh by 2032.

The catch is that almost every strength here has a matching cost, and usually it is the same fact viewed from the other side. The barriers that make the business hard to replicate — licensed Japanese technology from Mitsubishi and Morita, non-FEOC status ahead of the 2027 US tax-credit deadline, completed customer site audits — are largely a capital and compliance wall rather than a cheaper or better product, and building that wall is exactly what wrecks the returns. Roughly ₹3,000 crore of capital spending is being deployed against FY26 revenue of ₹862 crore; every ₹1 of assets currently produces only ₹0.30 of sales, about 260 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and the headline return on capital is negative — for every ₹100 of capital in the business this year, operating cash came out at minus ₹10. The spending is genuinely for growth rather than upkeep, with Pakhajan Phase 2 at ₹1,367 crore claiming 18-20% returns, but the record includes repeated cost revisions and delayed commissioning. Likewise, the formula pricing that protects absolute profit when lithium prices swing is precisely why the screen finds no pricing power at all: management says commodity price moves do not change the economics, revenue rose 75% in FY23 on lithium pass-through and inorganic revenue fell 24% in one quarter purely on lower lithium, which is what makes cyclicality a high risk rather than a footnote.

Pulling it together, this is a credible franchise attached to a bet that has not yet paid, which is why the combined score lands at 35 out of 100 — Mixed — with financial resilience marked as stressed. Three of the eight risk channels come back high. Cyclicality, as above, is the mirror image of the missing pricing power. Government dependency is the mirror image of the regulatory moat: the entire battery thesis rests on India's production-linked incentive schemes, ministry demand projections, and US rules that create the January 2027 shift — the same rules Chinese rivals cannot cross. Customer concentration is the mirror image of the hard-won qualifications: four international salt customers and four domestic cell makers, with the ₹200 crore salt guidance leaning largely on a single long-term formula contract. Below those sit three watch-level risks, all pointing the same direction — electrolyte salts are specification-driven goods where cheaper Chinese material, priced 20-50% below Neogen's formula price, is functionally acceptable, so the premium rests on reliability rather than a better product; other Indian players have announced capacity; and the majority of future revenue, a potential ₹2,400-2,900 crore by FY29 against ₹862 crore total in FY26, rides on one battery chemistry while sodium-ion emerges. Offsetting all this: the legacy specialty business is policy-independent and still guided at ₹950-1,050 crore, about 25% of FY23 revenue came from wholly new customers, plants are said to be switchable between lithium- and sodium-ion electrolyte, the BuLi Chemicals bolt-on was turned around to full capacity and profitability, and the promoter family put ₹161 crore of its own money in at a premium in FY26 while openly owning its misses — it cut FY25 battery guidance from ₹75-100 crore to ₹50-75 crore and said plainly, 'last year we had gone wrong.' Honest management is a real asset; it is not yet a delivery record.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration. The balance sheet and cash engine shows multiple financial stress signals — 3 of the 3 stress checks are flagged — multiple warning lights at once.

- ⚠️ Borrowings are now 1.7× shareholders' funds (up from 1.0×) — a leveraged balance sheet leaves little room when any of the risks above bites.
- ⚠️ Only about -17% of recent operating profit became cash (was 38%) — profits are getting stuck in paper, an early stress signal.
- ⚠️ Returns on capital slid from about 25% to 8% — the business is earning less and less on the money invested in it.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across all four years management explains revenue and margins primarily through lithium and bromine price swings (75% revenue growth in FY23 on lithium price pass-through, inorganic revenue -24% in Q2FY25 purely on lower lithium, +145%/+158% in FY26-Q1FY27 as prices rebounded), while the growth engine is battery materials sold into customers' gigafactory capex ramp — funded by ~INR 1,800 crore of capex that has pushed net debt to INR 1,295 crore and forced a INR 600 crore QIP and promoter infusion. Pharma/agro destocking cycles and 'weak demand, oversupply' commentary compound it.

> "inorganic chemicals' revenues declined 24%, mainly due to lower lithium prices" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Lithium/bromine are contractual pass-throughs so absolute EBITDA is protected; core pharma, flavours-and-fragrance and CSM demand is consumption-linked, and the company invested counter-cyclically through the 2024-25 chemical downturn*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 0 of the last 11 years with swings of ±18 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 14% and 19% (±1.6 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 65% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 15% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** The entire battery thesis rests on policy: India's ACC PLI scheme and a proposed PLI for battery components, Ministry of Heavy Industries demand projections, and on the US side the IRA and non-FEOC/non-PAP tax-credit rules that create the 'January 2027 shift' underpinning the INR 200 crore salt order book. An analyst explicitly asked about Trump revoking the IRA, and management could only relay customer reassurance rather than point to demand that survives without the subsidy.

> "our battery material strategy is strongly backed by Government support and proactive policy actions... allocation of 10 gigawatt hour ACC PLI re-bidding tranche" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: The legacy specialty/CSM business (pharma, agro, flavours and fragrance, semiconductor) is policy-independent and still guided at INR 950-1,050 crore standalone; customers claim they need a non-China backup 'even if IRA is there or no'*

### Stakeholder Concentration — HIGH RISK
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

## What to watch

- Return on capital turning positive as the ₹3,000 crore build starts selling — the headline check is at minus 10% today, and the Capex-for-growth check already flags repeated cost revisions and delayed commissioning at Pakhajan Phase 2.

- Whether the four international salt customers and four domestic cell makers become many, and whether the ₹200 crore salt guidance stops leaning on one formula contract — the Stakeholder Concentration risk is the direct cost of the multi-year qualification barrier.
