# Ashoka Buildcon (ASHOKA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Ashoka Buildcon is an Indian construction contractor, based in Nashik, Maharashtra, that builds infrastructure for government bodies — mainly highways and roads, but also railway systems, power transmission and distribution networks, buildings and urban works like bridges and flyovers. Most of its money comes from fixed-price EPC (engineering, procurement and construction) contracts won from clients such as NHAI, state road and public works departments, the Mumbai municipal corporation (BMC), CIDCO and Indian Railways, where it is paid to build and books revenue as work progresses; its balance order book was about INR 15,312 crores at March 2026, with standalone income of INR 5,952 crores in FY26. Separately, it also owns and operates road assets itself — collecting tolls from motorists on the Jaora-Nayagaon toll road (about INR 77 crores a quarter) and receiving fixed semi-annual 'annuity' payments from NHAI on hybrid annuity (HAM) projects — and periodically sells these completed road companies to infrastructure funds (KKR, NIIF, Maple Infrastructure Trust) for lump-sum proceeds it uses to cut debt. Almost all work is in India, with a growing overseas EPC business in Saudi Arabia, Angola, Liberia and Guyana.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Road EPC (highways)** — Builds and widens national and state highways under plain construction contracts where the government pays the full cost — the single biggest business, at roughly half of quarterly revenue and, together with railways, about 66% of the order book.
- **HAM road projects (hybrid annuity)** — Builds NHAI highways through separate project companies (SPVs) in which Ashoka puts up part of the equity, then earns fixed annuity payments from NHAI once complete — about INR 1,619 crores of the order book at March 2026, and the assets it most often sells on to investors.
- **BOT toll roads** — Owns road concessions where it keeps the toll money collected from traffic; after selling most of its portfolio, this is now essentially the Jaora-Nayagaon toll road, bringing in roughly INR 77 crores of gross toll per quarter.
- **Power T&D EPC** — Builds electricity transmission and distribution networks, including overseas (Angola) — the second-largest line, at about 30% of the order book and roughly a fifth of revenue.
- **Railways EPC** — Executes railway works such as signalling and anti-collision systems — a smaller line at around INR 1,420 crores of order book and about 8-10% of revenue.
- **Building and urban infrastructure EPC** — Constructs buildings, bridges, flyovers and airport-linked structures (for example BMC's Mithi River works, a Navi Mumbai airport bridge, a Daman signature bridge) — the smallest named segment at roughly 3-4% of the order book, though large recent wins are lifting it.
- **International EPC** — A growing overseas arm doing roads and power distribution in Guyana, Liberia, Angola and a hotel package in Saudi Arabia's Diriyah project (Ashoka's share INR 900 crores) — described by management as a strategic expansion rather than a core earnings driver yet.
- **Asset monetization** — Not an operating business but a recurring source of cash: selling completed road SPVs and other holdings (10 HAM and BOT projects sold in FY26 alone, including INR 1,814 crores from five BOT SPVs) to cut consolidated debt, which fell from INR 4,910 crores to about INR 2,778 crores.
- **City gas distribution (exited)** — Formerly held a 51% stake in Unison Enviro, a piped-gas business, sold to Mahanagar Gas in FY24 for INR 286.67 crores to Ashoka — no longer part of the company.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Ashoka Buildcon comes out of all three reviews as the same kind of business: a competent road and infrastructure contractor with almost nothing that protects its profits. The 34-check quality framework finds only two things to like, and they are modest — sales have compounded at roughly 11% a year, which sits inside the steady 10–15% band the framework prizes, and the company converts ₹25 of every ₹100 of sales into operating profit. Both come with an immediate qualifier from the same records: the growth is lumpy year to year, margins have swung about ±4.1 points a year because key costs sit outside management's control, and the call commentary puts working operating margins at 8.5–10%, with the March 2026 quarter falling to 6.9%. Every other scoring area is neutral or negative — competitive advantage (−1.0), customer benefits (−1.0) and industry structure (−1.17) are the worst — and the combined rating lands at 32 out of 100, or Weak (one star), built from 38 for business quality, 10 for multibagger fit and 46 for risk safety.

The multibagger screen and the quality checks are really telling one story twice. Nine of the eleven patterns are a clear no fit, and almost all of them fail for the same single reason: every rupee of revenue is won by being the lowest bidder in an open tender, against a large field, to a customer that is effectively the government. That one fact is what produces the −2 scores on mini-monopolies, on the pricing/mix/volume check and on industry structure, and it is also what kills Pricing Power (fixed-price contracts absorb input inflation rather than pass it on, to the point that the company had to build 0.5%–1% escalation into its own May 2026 budgets), Brand Strength (repeat awards from the Mumbai civic body and an AA/A1+ rating reaffirmation, but no premium price attached), Low Price Plus (thin margins without any offsetting cost or asset-turn advantage) and, ironically, the Toll Roads pattern — the company literally builds toll roads but is a price-competitive tenderer, not an entrenched supplier. Market Share Gainers fails on the numbers: the order book went from ₹15,355 crore in June 2022 down to ₹11,697 crore in March 2024 and back to ₹15,312 crore in March 2026, with FY24 order inflow collapsing to ₹1,953 crore and FY26 revenue shrinking 17%. Only two patterns score even a partial fit — Friendly Middleman, where the evidence is mixed, and Corporate Culture, which is the genuine bright spot: a long-tenured promoter family running the calls throughout, bad news disclosed promptly (the ₹25–28 crore quarterly credit provisions, a ₹37 crore Saudi write-off taken conservatively), and a deleveraging promise that was eventually kept.

The risk review then shows the same traits reappearing as hazards, and in two places a single decision is simultaneously a strength and a weakness. Government dependency is rated high risk precisely because of the monopsony customer base that already sank the pattern screen — road, state, railway, power distribution and civic counterparties with no lock-in, whose payment behaviour forces those repeated ₹25–28 crore credit provisions and whose award pace alone sets order inflow. Cyclicality is high risk for the matching reason: revenue rides entirely on customers' capital spending, visible as +21% revenue in FY24 turning into a 17% decline in FY26 after a two-year award drought. Being the low-price supplier of a 'good enough' service is rated elevated, and it is the same L1 tendering that keeps promised double-digit margins perpetually out of reach. The offsetting move — selling concession assets (five road special-purpose vehicles to Maple for ₹1,814 crore, the Chennai ring road, and ten concession projects monetised in FY26) — cut consolidated debt from ₹4,910 crore to roughly ₹2,722 crore and is the reason resilience is judged sound and cyclicality has a real silver lining. But that identical programme is what makes Forward Integrators and Recurring Revenue no fits and drags the capex-for-growth check to −1: the only large capital the company deploys is being sold off rather than compounded, leaving fixed-asset spending of about ₹75–80 crore a year against a roughly ₹6,000 crore revenue base. Balance-sheet safety is being bought by giving up the only assets that generate income without winning a new tender. On the quieter side, technology risk is low, and new entrants, shifting customer preferences and fashion risk return no signal at all — a business this exposed to price competition is at least not exposed to obsolescence.
