# Steel Authority of India Ltd. (SAIL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: METALS*

Steel Authority of India Limited (SAIL) is a large, government-owned Indian steel maker that runs integrated steel plants — named on the calls as Bhilai, Bokaro, Durgapur and IISCO — turning imported coking coal and iron ore into crude steel and then into finished ("saleable") steel. It sells that steel mainly inside India, to construction and infrastructure users, to general industry, and to Indian Railways (which buys rails at a price fixed later by a cost-audit process, sometimes producing large back-dated arrears). It makes money on the gap between the price it gets per tonne (its "NSR", around INR52,000–57,000 a tonne in FY26–27) and its cost per tonne, which swings mostly with imported coking coal prices; volume, inventory liquidation and lower borrowing costs are the other big profit levers management points to. By FY25-26 it was selling close to 20 million tonnes a year for roughly INR110,000 crores of turnover, with sales overwhelmingly domestic and only a small, variable amount exported.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Flat products** — Sheet-type steel whose price tracks international markets closely — management repeatedly says flats take the bigger hit when global prices fall (e.g. in FY23 and again in 2024 under the surge of Chinese exports), making it the more price-volatile half of the book.
- **Long products** — Bars, rods and similar construction-type steel that management says holds prices better than flats because the domestic secondary sector sets the tone; in Q1 FY27 long prices were the swing factor month to month, moving a few thousand rupees a tonne.
- **Rails / Indian Railways business** — Rails supplied to the railways at an ad-hoc price that is later finalised by cost audit, so revisions arrive as lump-sum arrears — one such catch-up added about INR1,637 crores of prior-period benefit in Q2 FY25, and management stresses FY26 profits contained no such arrear.
- **Semis (semi-finished steel)** — Part-processed steel sold on rather than rolled into finished product — about 16% of production but only ~9% of sales in H1 FY25 because SAIL uses outside conversion services; a new 1 MTPA bar mill at Durgapur is being built specifically to shrink this lower-value slice.
- **Captive mines** — SAIL's own iron ore and other mineral mines that feed the plants, and from FY27 an area of active focus — surplus output is being sold in the market, adding roughly INR400 crores of extra sales and INR150 crores of profit in Q1 FY27 versus the year before.
- **Exports** — A small, opportunistic slice of volumes rather than a core business — weak exports dragged Q1 FY23 sales down, and management mostly discusses India's overall export/import balance rather than a meaningful SAIL export franchise.
- **Capacity expansion projects** — Not a revenue line but a major use of cash: brownfield expansion cleared at three plants (IISCO, Bokaro, Bhilai), with capex of about INR9,100 crores in FY26 rising to a guided INR15,000 crores in FY27 and over INR20,000 crores in FY28.

### The story in depth

Steel Authority of India is a large, government-owned integrated steel producer selling roughly 20 million tonnes a year into an Indian market that is genuinely growing — the one check that scores at the top of the framework is structural end-market growth, with domestic steel consumption up 13.5% in the first half of FY25 and over 8% in both FY26 and the first quarter of FY27, and India named the world's strongest source of demand. The business is also converting capital into cash reasonably well right now: every ₹100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings produced ₹21 of operating cash this year. Management has visibly tightened up too — borrowings peaked at ₹39,163 crore in August 2024 on working capital, then were cut by ₹8,150 crore in FY26 to a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.36, with inventory run down and the cost of borrowing falling from 7.3% to 6.2%. The combined rating still lands at 37 out of 100, a 'Mixed' two-star grade, built from 47 on business quality, 10 on multibagger fit and 52 on risk safety, with resilience judged 'sound'.

The reason the multibagger score is so low is that almost nothing about how SAIL earns its money is hard for someone else to copy. Of the eleven patterns tested, none is a clear fit: four are partial and seven are outright no-fit. The closest thing to a real edge is cost to replicate — captive iron-ore mines in Odisha, Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand holding around 32 million tonnes of sub-grade fines, plus self-sufficiency in met coke — but the review concludes these cushion the business rather than protect it, because they sit alongside a legacy high-cost structure carrying about ₹11,400 crore of employee cost on roughly 20 million tonnes. Everything downstream of that is commodity behaviour: selling prices are set by global indices, moving from about ₹66,000 a tonne in the first quarter of FY23 down to about ₹48,000 in October 2024 and back to ₹57,100 by the first quarter of FY27, which is why pricing power, brand strength and low-price-plus all score as no-fit. There is no disclosed R&D spend in any call and the one differentiated product raised, head-hardened rail, was still 'under trial' in August 2022 with nothing quantified since — so innovation dominance fails, and the research-and-development check scores negative for the same underlying reason. Market-share gains are not established either: sales fell 6.3% in the first half of FY25 while national consumption grew 13.5%, and although FY26 volumes recovered 11% to 19.9 million tonnes, roughly 1 million tonnes of that was third-party RINL and NSL steel being resold rather than SAIL's own.

The risks are largely the same facts viewed from the other side. Being state-owned is what makes government dependency the single high risk: rail prices are fixed by an ad hoc government price and then negotiated down through the CA Cost mechanism, with a ₹1,800 crore arrear swinging FY25 profit and provisional FY26 prices described as 'on the lower side' — and that same relationship with Indian Railways is what puts stakeholder concentration on watch. The price-taking that kills the pricing-power pattern is what makes cyclicality an elevated risk: coking coal went from $670 a tonne to $200, and EBITDA fell from ₹6,674 crore to ₹2,076 crore in a single year. The absence of brand or product differentiation shows up as the elevated 'good enough goods' risk, where cheaper secondary-sector TMT and imports cap what SAIL can charge and management itself tracks the primary-versus-secondary spread as the key driver of its own realisation. The heavy asset base that underpins the captive-mine advantage is also what drags the asset-turn check to negative — ₹1 of assets produces ₹0.82 of sales — and sits behind the 105-day cash cycle between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Two things do cut the other way: technological disruption is rated low, because the innovation happening is incremental and self-directed (coke rate, coal dust injection, energy intensity, a phased plan that has already cut CO2 by 20% with a further 12% targeted by FY31), and there is no signal at all on shifting consumer preferences or fashion risk. The business is essentially wholly domestic, with exports having collapsed from 3.64 lakh tonnes to 1.66 lakh tonnes in the first quarter of FY23 and no geographic expansion strategy, so the Indian demand cycle is the whole story.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** SAIL is a government-owned producer whose fortunes are repeatedly set by policy: rail prices are fixed by an ad hoc government price then negotiated via CA Cost (a Rs.1,800cr arrear swung FY25 profit, and provisional prices are 'on the lower side' in FY26), capex is 'projected to the ministry', pricing relief depends on an 11.5% safeguard duty and pending antidumping investigations, export duty hit volumes in FY23, and wages await a pay commission guideline from government.

> "Safeguard is still in place, which is 11.5% as of now... It is giving a relief to the -- much needed relief to the steel domestic market." — *management, on an earnings call*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but none of the 3 computed fingerprints confirms it in the numbers yet — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Every call frames results around commodity price swings: coking coal at $670/t collapsing to $200, NSR moving from Rs.66,000 (Q1FY23) to Rs.48,000 (Oct-24) and back to Rs.57,100 (Q1FY27), with EBITDA falling from Rs.6,674cr to Rs.2,076cr in a single year and borrowings swinging by thousands of crores; capex is now guided to triple from Rs.9,100cr to Rs.20,000-26,000cr precisely as margins peak, the classic expansion-at-the-top pattern.

> "The EBITDA stood at Rs. 2076 Crores as compared to Rs.6674 Crores in Q1 FY2022... Profitability has been adversely affected due to increase in the input cost especially coking coal." — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 3 of the last 11 years with swings of ±18 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between -7% and 21% (±7.7 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 8% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 17%).

## What to watch
