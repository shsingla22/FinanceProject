# ABB India Ltd. (ABB) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

ABB India makes and sells the electrical and automation equipment that powers and runs factories, buildings and infrastructure — everything management describes as "substation to socket" electrical gear (switchgear, compact substations, low- and medium-voltage power distribution, UPS), plus motors, generators and drives, process control/SCADA systems, and industrial robots. Its customers are industrial and infrastructure buyers across roughly 23 market segments — data centres, railways and metros, renewables, buildings and infrastructure, metals and mining, energy and petrochemicals, automotive, electronics, food and beverage — sold both directly (about 59% of business) and through 750-plus channel partners, integrators and OEMs (about 41%), with an added stream of after-sales service. It earns money by booking orders into a backlog and converting them into revenue: CY2025 orders were about ₹14,115 crore, revenue about ₹13,203 crore, profit before tax ₹2,230 crore (16.9% margin) and profit after tax ₹1,669 crore, with a closing backlog of ₹10,471 crore and return on capital employed of 21%. It is overwhelmingly an India business — manufacturing here for over 75 years from 5 factory locations (Nashik, Faridabad, Baroda and two Bengaluru campuses) housing 25 shop floors, served by 28 sales offices — with exports to about 30 countries making up roughly 10% of the portfolio.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Electrification** — Sells the full chain of electrical distribution products from substation to socket — low- and medium-voltage switchgear, compact substations, smart power products, UPS and installation products for cities, buildings, data centres and infrastructure; management calls it part of the core that makes up a major share of revenue, and it is the business most cited in data-centre and building/infrastructure wins.
- **Electrification Distribution Solutions** — A distribution-focused unit within Electrification that got its own president by 2025-26, supplying power-distribution platforms such as the new ARTU Formula low-voltage switchgear platform that partners use to build value-added solutions.
- **Motion** — ABB's energy-efficiency core — electric motors, generators, drives and mechanical power transmission products plus related services, used in HVAC and cooling, water and process plants, metals electric powertrains, rail propulsion and now locally built wind power converters; described alongside Electrification as a major part of the revenue stream.
- **Process Automation** — Process control systems and integrated electrification-plus-digital solutions for process and hybrid industries — SCADA, remote terminal units, leak detection, city gas distribution monitoring, petrochemical and energy projects — typically larger, longer-cycle project work.
- **Robotics & Discrete Automation** — Industrial robots and factory/machine automation, historically driven by automotive but increasingly sold into FMCG and food & beverage end-of-line packaging and palletising, and electronics and mobile-phone assembly.
- **Automation (combined)** — By the 2025-26 calls, Process Automation and Robotics & Discrete Automation are presented together under a single Automation head, so ABB India describes itself simply as an electrification, motion and automation company.
- **Divisions and market segments (how the parts are organised)** — The business areas are split into distinct divisions with their own business models — 20 in 2022, 19 in 2024, 18 in 2025 and 16 by early 2026 — all pointed at the same 23 market segments, a spread management says smooths out the cyclicality of any single end market.
- **Exports** — Shipments from Indian plants to about 30-plus countries; growing (up 45% in Q2 2022, 23% in Q1 2024) but still only around 10% of the portfolio because domestic growth is much stronger.
- **Services** — After-sales service and support attached to the installed base, delivered increasingly through channel partners close to the customer; grew 14% in the Q2 2022 quarter.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

ABB India comes out of all three reviews as a genuinely good business that is only a moderately good investment case, which is why the combined rating lands at 64 out of 100 — "Decent" — built from 67 on business quality, 48 on multibagger pattern fit and 78 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound. The strongest single thread running through the records is that this company sells certainty rather than cheapness. The quality framework's top mark goes to assurance benefits: management deliberately took on more expensive imported material to guarantee QCO-compliant supply reliability, and was rewarded with growth in base orders. The same trait shows up on the pattern screen as brand strength, a likely fit, where a 140-year-old group that has manufactured in India for 75 years converts heritage into a stated exemption from price comparison with cheaper rivals and preferred-supplier status with global hyperscalers — and it is also why the low-price-plus pattern is explicitly no fit. Behind it sits the second big strength, physical reach: 5 plants and 25 shop floors feeding 28 sales offices (up from 21 in 2022) and 750+ partners, splitting sales 41% through partners and 59% direct, with local installation and lifetime service. That reach earns full marks for both distribution advantage and convenience benefits, and it is the same asset that the cost-to-replicate pattern rests on.

The catch is that most of these same traits cut both ways. The premium for reliability is exactly what the good-enough-goods risk flags: management felt the need to defend its price premium against cheaper alternatives in the February 2026 call, the finance chief conceded that the 2023-24 price premium came from post-COVID scarcity and is now normalising, and roughly 1 of the 3.5 percentage-point rise in material cost was attributed to competition intensity — which is also why pricing power scores only a partial fit rather than a structural advantage, and why new entrants sits on watch. The end markets tell the same double story. Data centres, railways and metro propulsion, renewables, electronics and government-incentive-led private capex earn a full score for structural end-market growth, but they are all other people's capital-spending programmes, which is precisely the reason cyclicality is the one elevated risk in the whole review, and why persistence of growth is the framework's only negative check: sales have compounded at about 21% a year but arrive lumpily. The policy-anchored slice of that same demand — railway and metro orders inside Motion's Rs.4,200cr backlog, budget infrastructure allocations, incentive-led capex, and the QCO import rules that produced the reliability win in the first place — is what keeps government dependency on watch. The replication barrier, similarly, is described as blocking new entrants but not existing large rivals.

What holds the case together is the management and balance-sheet layer, which is the one place the records are unambiguous. Corporate culture is the single strong-fit pattern: the same leadership across all 17 calls, a balance sheet that stayed in net cash right through the cycle (about ₹2,800cr in 2022 rising to ₹6,042cr by early 2026), return on capital employed of 21% openly used as a self-measure, and bad news owned rather than buried — a missed profit promise admitted in 2022, the parent group's 5% India figure publicly reconciled against the 15% reported locally, and the recent margin shortfall broken down into its causes. Stewardship is purely organic, with no acquisitions, no borrowing, a short 21-day cash cycle, and a fresh $75mn manufacturing and research expansion plus new local lines for wind power converters and drives announced during the 2025 demand pause — spending into weakness rather than out of it, though the absolute amounts stay modest. This is also why the pattern score is the weakest of the three inputs at 48: six of the eleven patterns are only partial and three do not fit at all, mostly because the supporting claims are directional rather than measured — market share gains are asserted but never quantified, the service attach rate behind recurring revenue is not disclosed, and product launches, while regular, are incremental additions rather than category-changing ones. In short, a well-run, debt-free, service-heavy business in permanently growing markets, whose returns will still arrive unevenly and whose price premium is now being tested.

## What to watch

- Gross margin and the competition line: whether the roughly 1 percentage point of the 3.5-point material-cost rise blamed on competition intensity keeps widening, which would confirm the good-enough-goods risk and push the pricing power pattern from partial to broken.
- Order and backlog momentum through the demand pause: the elevated cyclicality risk and the negative persistence-of-growth check both hinge on whether the 16% order, 17% revenue and 21% backlog growth rates hold up rather than swinging.
- Policy-linked orders: any change to railway and metro procurement, budget infrastructure allocations, incentive-led capex or the QCO import rules would hit the government dependency watch item — and the same QCO rules are what produced the assurance-benefits win.
- Actual market share disclosure: management has never quantified its share, which is the sole reason the market share gainers pattern is only partial; hard numbers either way would move the verdict.
- Continued organic, debt-free investment: if the $75mn expansion is followed by acquisitions, borrowing or a shrinking net cash position from the current ₹6,042cr, the disciplined stewards check and the strong-fit corporate culture pattern both weaken.
