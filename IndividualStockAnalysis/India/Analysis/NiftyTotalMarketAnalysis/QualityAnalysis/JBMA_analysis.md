### The story in depth

JBM Auto scores 57 out of 100 and lands at "Decent" (3 stars). That number is a blend of three different readings of the same company: business quality at 69, fit with the multibagger patterns at only 13, and risk safety at 88. The quality side is genuinely respectable in places — the industry structure check is the single best score in the whole framework (2 out of 2), because profit margins have barely moved over the years, within about one percentage point, which is what you would expect when pricing is rational and few new competitors are crowding in. Costs look controlled too: ₹11 of every ₹100 of sales is operating profit, year after year, and only about 26 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, a short and healthy cycle. Sales have compounded at roughly 13% a year, right inside the 10–15% band the framework prizes most, though the growth arrives lumpily rather than evenly.

The trouble is that the same traits producing those strengths also produce the weaknesses. After direct input costs the company keeps only ₹32 of every ₹100 of sales — thin pricing power, with customers likely buying on price. That single fact explains why almost every pattern on the multibagger screen comes back NO FIT: Pricing Power, Brand Strength, Low Price Plus, Toll Roads, Friendly Middleman, Innovation Dominance and Forward Integrators all fail, and only Market Share Gainers registers as a quant signal, with Recurring Revenue a partial fit. A company that wins on price can take share — which is what the Market Share Gainers signal is picking up — but it does so by selling more volume through a moderately asset-heavy base, where every ₹1 of assets produces ₹0.83 of sales. Push that combination hard enough and the cash goes into the business rather than out of it, which is exactly what the worst check shows: for every ₹100 of capital employed, the business generated ₹-1 of operating cash this year, a -1% cash return and the only heavily negative score in the framework. Growth and industry stability score well; the return on capital area sits below zero; and the overall resilience reading is STRAINED. These are not separate observations — they are the same story seen from two ends.

The risk review is the part to read most carefully, because its high safety score of 88 rests largely on silence rather than on evidence of safety. Only one risk surfaced at all — Stakeholder Concentration — and it is marked a QUANT FLAG, meaning one numeric fingerprint is present but the conference calls say nothing either way, so it is a prompt to investigate rather than a verdict. Notably, every one of the eleven patterns and this one risk carries the same note: no conference-call judgement available. Two of the seven quality areas, Competitive Advantage and Management, come back blank for the same reason. So the low multibagger score of 13 and the clean risk score of 88 are both partly a reflection of unexamined ground, not proven results. A concentration of a few large customers would also sit naturally alongside the thin ₹32 gross retention — customers who buy on price and buy in size are the same customers — which means the pricing-power weakness and the concentration flag may well be one issue wearing two labels. The honest summary: a steadily profitable, share-gaining company in a rational industry that is currently not converting its growth into cash, with the questions that matter most left unanswered.

- Intangible benefits: only ₹32 of every ₹100 of sales is kept after direct input costs. If that figure rises, the NO FIT verdicts on Pricing Power and Brand Strength could change; if it falls, the price-taker position hardens.

- Resilience is STRAINED and both Competitive Advantage and Management score blank — management commentary or call transcripts covering capital discipline and customer mix would materially move the 69 quality score and the 88 risk-safety score in either direction.
