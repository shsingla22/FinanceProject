# KRBL (KRBL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

KRBL Limited is an Indian rice company — it buys paddy from farmers in Punjab, Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan and parts of Madhya Pradesh, mills and ages it, and sells the rice under its own consumer brands, chiefly basmati rice under the 'India Gate' brand (which management calls the world's No. 1 basmati rice brand) plus the Unity brand and a growing non-basmati range. It describes itself as the world's largest rice miller and basmati rice exporter, selling both inside India through general trade, modern trade and e-commerce (about 3.2 lakh retail outlets, reaching roughly 1.2 crore urban households) and overseas, mainly to the Middle East (Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, UAE), plus the EU, UK and the United States. It makes money on the spread between what it pays for paddy and what it gets for packed and bulk rice, so profits swing with crop quality, paddy prices, government export policy, and freight and geopolitical conditions on Middle East shipping routes. India is by far the bigger side of the business — around Rs. 4,000 crore of domestic revenue in FY24 against Rs. 1,345 crore of exports, out of total income of Rs. 5,482 crore.

**The parts of the business:**

- **India / Domestic business** — Sells branded packaged rice to Indian households through distributors, general trade, modern trade and e-commerce; it is the largest part of the company (roughly Rs. 4,000 crore in FY24, about Rs. 1,104 crore in Q3 FY26 excluding power) and management claims clear market leadership with ~37.8% share in general trade, 39.3% in modern trade and 41.2% in e-commerce.
- **Branded basmati (India Gate and other brands)** — The core premium product — aged basmati rice sold in consumer packs — described as the world's No. 1 basmati brand; it drives most domestic sales but was flat in Q3 FY26 as loose and regional players competed harder and raw material prices fell.
- **Unity brand** — A separate, lower-priced rice brand aimed at the mass market that grew more than 100% year-on-year to about Rs. 150 crore in a single quarter (Q1 FY23), so a fast-growing but much smaller piece than India Gate.
- **Branded non-basmati rice** — Ordinary (non-basmati) rice sold under KRBL's brands, treated as a completely separate commodity from basmati in price and market terms; it is the fastest-growing domestic line, up 35% over nine months of FY26 and 9% in Q3 FY26, though still smaller than basmati.
- **Export business (branded)** — Sells packaged branded rice overseas through country distributors, especially in the Middle East, with management repeatedly flagging distributor changes (for example rebuilding a lost 40-year Saudi relationship) as a swing factor; total exports were Rs. 1,345 crore in FY24 and Rs. 1,276 crore over nine months of FY26, up 21% year-on-year.
- **Bulk export segment** — Lower-margin, unbranded, large-volume rice shipments to overseas buyers; management deliberately pulls back here when payment risk in markets like Iran and Iraq rises, which it says cost roughly Rs. 600 crore of export revenue in FY24 and cut Q3 FY26 exports to Rs. 357 crore from Rs. 563 crore a year earlier.
- **Power** — A small energy-generation activity whose revenue management explicitly strips out when reporting domestic sales ('domestic revenue, excluding power'), indicating it is a minor side stream rather than a core business.
- **New products / portfolio expansion** — An effort to add products beyond core rice, with a stated target of about 10% of revenue from new products within two financial years (as of FY23), so currently a small contributor.
- **Procurement, milling and manufacturing base** — Buying paddy directly from farming belts and milling it — management stresses sourcing low-moisture, high-recovery paddy to protect margins, and mentions long-term consolidation of operations at Panipat for scale and cost efficiency.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

KRBL's story starts and ends with one asset: the India Gate brand and the shelf space it commands. The quality framework gives its highest marks to advertisement and promotion, convenience and distribution reach — 3.4 lakh retail outlets, 16 C&Fs, 8 super stockists, leading share in general trade, modern trade and e-commerce at the same time, built on 150-plus new distributors and 44,000 outlets added in a single quarter. That same evidence is what makes two of the eleven multibagger patterns a likely fit: Brand Strength (world's No.1 branded basmati, a 20-25% premium over loose rice, 60%-plus share of voice, a 4,200-GRP Bachchan festive campaign, 200bps of brand-equity gain) and Market Share Gainers (general trade from 32% in Q1 FY23 to 36.9% in Q4 FY24 to 37.8% in Q3 FY26, e-commerce at 40-41%). Customer Benefits scores a perfect 1.0 on the quality areas and Return on Capital a respectable 0.67, with ₹16 of operating cash generated per ₹100 of capital and ₹15 of operating profit per ₹100 of sales. This is the part of the business that works.

The trouble is that the very traits producing those patterns also generate the risks. The premium that defines Brand Strength is the same premium the Good Enough Goods risk says must be defended — and by FY26 cheaper loose and regional players were pressing on price hard enough that modern-trade share slipped from 41.7% to 38.7%, while KRBL itself launched Unity as a lower-priced fighter brand (the reason Low Price Plus reads only PARTIAL). Market share gains have the same double edge: the framework scores share gain only 1 because KRBL is already the dominant branded player, so the runway is short, and the New Entrants risk points at the same modern-trade drift. The paddy-ageing inventory model that makes the business hard to replicate is also what produces the framework's worst single score — minus 2 for working capital, with roughly 323 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, against ₹4,451 crore of inventory at March 2024 funded by rising bank debt. And the cheap inventory bought after a low-paddy-price season, scored as a favourable cyclical position, is the flip side of the Cyclicality risk: gross margin has swung from 32.5% to 25.7% to 30.2% and EBITDA from 19.6% to 14.1% as procurement costs moved, which is exactly why Pricing Power rates only PARTIAL.

The weakest links are exports and management credibility, and they are the same link. Geographical expansion scores minus 1: four years of promised export recovery has not arrived, the Saudi distributor lost in 2021 was still unreplaced by February 2026, and Iran remains payment-blocked. That single relationship failure is the Stakeholder Concentration risk (ELEVATED, an estimated ₹600 crore of lost revenue from Iran and Iraq alone), it sits inside the HIGH RISK Government Dependency channel (export bans that wiped out roughly ₹300 crore of non-basmati exports in FY24, EU pesticide rules, MSP-set paddy economics), and it is why Corporate Culture rates only PARTIAL and the Management quality area scores 0.17 — promises made and not kept. Industry Structure scores zero and Capital Allocation 0.25. Three patterns simply do not apply: no recurring revenue, no expert middleman, no toll-road position. Against all this sit genuine defences — a swing to net cash of minus ₹388 crore by December 2025, domestic revenue of ₹4,444 crore up 10% now at roughly 74% of the mix, branded non-basmati at ₹271 crore up 38%, and LOW ratings on technology, consumer-preference and fashion risk. Resilience is judged SOUND. The combined arithmetic — 45% × 60 business quality, 30% × 26 multibagger fit, 25% × 58 risk safety — lands at 49 out of 100: Mixed, two stars. A well-run brand with a strong domestic franchise, held back by a commodity input cycle, a capital-hungry balance sheet and an export book management has repeatedly failed to fix.
