# Supreme Petroch. (SPLPETRO) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Supreme Petrochem makes plastic raw materials — chiefly polystyrene, a commodity plastic sold as pellets to manufacturers who mould it into finished goods. Its customers are industrial buyers: appliance and consumer-durable OEMs (refrigerators, air conditioners), auto makers, packaging and insulation users, plus smaller non-OEM processors; it also trades bought-in material, which management said was roughly 22-23% of revenue in FY23. It earns money on the 'spread' — the gap between the price of its main raw material, styrene monomer (imported, priced in dollars, around $800-$1,650 a tonne across these calls), and the price of its finished polymer — so profits swing with that delta more than with volume. It is an Indian company, formed as a joint venture between Supreme Industries and Rajan Raheja Investments, headquartered in Mumbai with plants at Amdoshi/Nagothane (Raigarh, Maharashtra), Tamil Nadu and a new site at Panipat (Haryana); it sells mostly in India but is the largest exporter of polystyrene from India, and it runs debt-free, funding roughly Rs. 1,200 crores of expansion from its own cash. FY26 revenue was Rs. 5,338 crores with net profit of Rs. 327 crores on volumes of about 363,000 tonnes.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Polystyrene (PS) — GPPS and HIPS** — The core business: general-purpose and high-impact polystyrene pellets sold to durables, packaging and auto customers, with about 60% share of the Indian market, roughly 300,000 tonnes of capacity, and spreads over styrene monomer that management pegs at about $150-$200 a tonne in normal times.
- **Expandable Polystyrene (EPS)** — The foam-bead business entered in 2006, where the company is also market leader; capacity was raised to 110,000 tonnes and a Phase-II expansion commissioned in April 2026 took the Amdoshi/Nagothane line from 85,000 to 115,000 tonnes, against an Indian market management sizes at about 160,000 tonnes a year.
- **Mass ABS** — A newer, tougher engineering plastic made under licence from Versalis of Italy with a low carbon footprint — a 70,000-tonne plant was commissioned in September 2025, shut in December 2025 after a critical equipment failure, and was running at about 65% of design capacity by April 2026; management will not disclose its separate sales contribution.
- **Masterbatches and compounds (including special compounds)** — Value-added blends — colour and additive compounds, including compounds made from the company's own ABS — added in small modular lines as demand appears rather than in large capacity steps.
- **Extruded polystyrene (XPS) insulation foam boards** — Rigid insulation boards sold for green buildings and thermal insulation, with a second line and further XPS capacity among the projects under way.
- **Panipat (Haryana) project** — A new complex planned around 3D panels, sheeting and XPS with roughly Rs. 800 crores of investment, which management said carries revenue potential of about Rs. 2,000 crores at the capacities announced.
- **Trading** — Reselling bought-in polymer alongside its own output, running at about 22-23% of revenue in FY23 and expected by management to stay in a similar 20-25% range.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Supreme Petrochem comes out of the three reviews as a well-run company in a hard business, and the combined rating says so plainly: 48 out of 100, a Mixed (2-star) verdict built from 61 on business quality, 26 on multibagger fit and 52 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged STRAINED. The quality work is strongest exactly where an owner would want it — Management scores 1.2 and Capital Allocation 1.0, on the back of zero debt maintained across the whole period, roughly Rs 1,200 crore of expansion paid for entirely out of the company's own cash, no acquisitions, a rising dividend (Rs 7 in FY23 to Rs 10.5 in FY26), and management that disclosed the ABS equipment failure in the same quarter, refused to guess a restart date and corrected analysts rather than flatter them. The day-to-day machine is efficient too: every rupee of assets turns out Rs 1.55 of sales, and only about 21 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. What the same framework marks down is everything to do with what the company sells: Growth scores -0.5 because sales, while averaging about 30% a year, arrive in lumps; market share scores -1 because polystyrene share drifted from 60% in FY23 to about 55% by FY25 while imports hit multi-year highs; and pricing power scores -1 because only Rs 17 of every Rs 100 of sales survives direct input costs.

Those same two facts — a low-cost, debt-free operator selling a product it cannot price — explain almost the whole pattern screen, which returned just two likely fits, six partials and three outright misses. The two fits are the flattering side of the coin: 'Low Price Plus' fits because scale leadership, lean 30-40 day inventory, no debt and managed sourcing genuinely make this a low-cost producer, and 'Market Share Gainers' fits because it remains the clear leader in polystyrene and EPS and is adding ABS to displace imports that make up roughly half that market. But both are qualified in the same direction — there is no itemised cost obsession behind the cost edge, and leadership has been held rather than steadily gained. The three misses all trace back to one disclosed fact repeated across sixteen calls: prices are set at the landed cost of imports. That single mechanism is why 'Pricing Power' fails (FY26 revenue fell 11% purely because styrene monomer averaged 17% lower, and Q3 FY26 operating margin fell to about 5.5%), why 'Toll Roads' fails (the product is a big raw-material line for customers, who stop buying the moment styrene falls), and why 'Brand Strength' fails (no brand discussion anywhere, with cheap Thai and recycled material visibly taking wall-panel and photo-frame work). The middle band of partials — Corporate Culture, Innovation Dominance, Cost to Replicate — is where the quality strengths show up again but stop short: real barriers in bulk styrene handling and the guarded Versalis mass-ABS licence, real progress in lifting value-added grades from 25-27% to 37-40% of sales, yet the core process technology is licensed rather than invented and no research spend is disclosed.

The risk review then turns those same traits into the specific things that can go wrong. Cyclicality is the only HIGH RISK, and it is the direct consequence of the import-parity pricing that sank three patterns: management explains revenue and margin almost entirely through styrene prices and spreads, which is how FY23 EBITDA fell 27% and Q3 FY26 margin collapsed to 5.47%. The four WATCH items each attach to a strength or an ambition rather than sitting apart from them. The ABS project that underpins the 'Market Share Gainers' and 'Innovation Dominance' cases is the same project that creates supplier and licensor concentration — commissioned in September 2025, shut from December 2025 after a proprietary piece of equipment malfunctioned, which management could not even open without voiding the warranty; the quality framework independently flags this by giving growth capex only a 1, noting the plant broke down within months and incremental returns are unproven. The Panipat growth leg is frozen waiting on IOCL's styrene plant, which is government dependency in practice even though nothing about pricing is regulated — and the decision to defer that spend rather than build ahead is precisely the stewardship the framework rewarded, so the same discipline that protects the balance sheet is what postpones the growth. New entrants (China adding roughly 3 million tonnes of polystyrene and 10 million tonnes of styrene, a domestic rival expanding polystyrene capacity by about 150%, and 2025 seeing the highest polystyrene imports in ten years) explain the -1 on market share, and 'good enough' cheap substitutes explain the -1 on pricing power. Only technological disruption is rated LOW, because the company licensed the newer mass-ABS process rather than being outflanked by it. The honest reading is a financially conservative, candid, efficient operator whose earnings are set by a commodity spread it does not control, currently carrying an unfinished growth bet that has stumbled.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Fashion Risk. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 2 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.1× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ⚠️ Only about 48% of recent operating profit became cash (was 98%) — profits are getting stuck in paper, an early stress signal.
- ⬜ No return-on-capital history.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across all 16 calls management explains revenue and margins almost entirely through styrene monomer prices and PS-SM 'deltas': FY23 EBITDA fell 27% on 'reducing global deltas', Q3 FY26 operating margin collapsed to 5.47% on a ~20% SM price fall, then rebounded to 15.9% in Q4 FY26 on stable SM and better spreads — a swing of ~10 points in two quarters on flat volumes. Demand is further tied to OEM seasonality (consumer durables/AC/refrigerator build) and was hit by a mild monsoon season in FY26.

> "in a falling market, the demand automatically shrinks. So, these are the reasons for the PS margins to shrink also" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/operations-linked product (polymer feed into OEM and non-OEM processing, roughly 45:55), ~37-40% value-added grades earning a premium over published commodity spreads, debt-free balance sheet funding all capex from internal accruals — countercyclical capacity building through the downcycle*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±104 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 3% and 10% (±2.8 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 1% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 4%).

### Government Dependency — WATCH
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** The entire Panipat/Haryana growth leg is hostage to IOCL's (a PSU) styrene monomer plant: management has frozen the big PS/EPS spend there pending IOC's commissioning date, and admits this delay constrains medium-term volume growth. Pricing itself is set by import parity, not by regulators, so the dependency is on one state-owned upstream project rather than on subsidies or tariffs.

> "because the IOC's styrene monomer plant is delayed... we are not committing any expense for that at the moment... once we have more clarity on the IOC's SM plant commissioning date, then only we will put in the big monies" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: No regulated pricing, subsidy or tender dependence; ~20% cushion in existing capacity plus export markets to absorb domestic demand in the interim*

### Stakeholder Concentration — WATCH
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Supplier/licensor concentration is the live issue: the ABS plant, commissioned Sept 2025, was shut from Dec 2025 after 'malfunctioning of a critical production equipment' that is proprietary — management could not open it without voiding warranty and had to wait on Versalis and the European equipment supplier through Christmas and visa delays, restarting only at 65% of design capacity. Raw material is also imported from a handful of geographies (Korea, Singapore, Gulf, now China), exposed to a single chokepoint (Strait of Hormuz).

> "it is a proprietary critical equipment. So, we could not have opened it here also... we cannot touch these equipment because otherwise the warranty will go away for us" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Customer base is broad — 'we supply to practically all the OEMs in the country', no single named customer drives results, no advance order book concentration; alternate geographies were sourced during the Hormuz disruption*

- ✅ Customers pay in about 42 days (was 32) — no sign of weakening bargaining power in collection terms.

### New Entrants — WATCH
*"A new player changes the rules"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Repeated analyst questioning on structurally new supply: China adding ~3 mn tonnes of PS and ~10 mn tonnes of styrene, a domestic competitor expanding PS capacity by ~150%, other ABS players announcing expansions, and CY2025 seeing the highest PS imports in 10-15 years, largely from Thailand. Management's answer is consistently that demand growth absorbs it, but domestic PS utilisation of 80% against a market roughly the size of its own capacity shows the pressure is real.

> "there has been an increase in the calendar 2025, particularly this is in the month of March, April and May. These were the three months when all of a sudden bulk of it came" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: ~50% of Indian ABS demand still imported, leaving headroom for domestic substitution; freight costs and the company's established OEM relationships restrain import share*

- ✅ Operating margin held (about 4% then 8%) — no drift down under competition.
- ⬜ Insufficient sales history.

### Good Enough Goods — WATCH
*"Cheaper rivals that are good enough"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Commodity grades have essentially no pricing power — management states the base price is simply the landed import price plus costs — and cheaper 'good enough' substitutes are visibly taking applications: reprocessed/regranulated polystyrene imported from Thailand, the US, even Ghana and Slovenia is being diverted into fast-growing wall panel and photo frame uses where impact strength, finish and colour do not matter.

> "there you do not need the impact, you do not need the finish, you do not need any color issues. And these are mainly photo frames and wall panels" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Value-added grades at ~37-40% of mix with genuine differentiation (SMMA, XPS boards, compounds, masterbatches, whiter odour-free mass ABS needing less pigment) priced on a separate basis from landed-cost commodity grades; OEM customers are technically informed buyers, not an intermediary capturing the margin*

- ✅ The share of each sale kept after direct input costs held near 14% (was 10%) — no gross-level price erosion.
- ✅ Operating margin (10%) remains near its historical peak (10%) — no collapse in the premium.
- ✅ Operating margin held (about 4% then 8%) — no drift down under competition.

### Technological Innovation — LOW
*"Out-invented by the industry"*

Ongoing work is small-scale — new HIPS/GP grades, compounds, XPS, 3D panels.

> "With the mass ABS only being 10% of the global capacity today and 90% is still suspension. Demand is there for all ABS. So, it all gets absorbed." — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Steady small-scale, value-adding innovation cadence (value-added grades from ~25% to ~40% of mix, compounding, masterbatches) plus adoption of the newer process technology itself*
