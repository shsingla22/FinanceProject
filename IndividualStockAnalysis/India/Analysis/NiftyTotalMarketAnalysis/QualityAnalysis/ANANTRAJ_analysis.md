# Anant Raj (ANANTRAJ) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Anant Raj Limited is a family-run Indian property developer (in the construction business since 1969, now run by the fourth generation of the Sarin family) that buys land, builds on it, and sells or leases the finished space. Most of its money still comes from selling homes — group housing towers, independent floors, villas and plots — built mainly on its large land bank in Sector 63A, Gurugram, Haryana, plus lower-cost government-linked affordable housing in places like Neemrana and Tirupati; it also owns roughly 5 million square feet of completed commercial buildings that it leases out. Since about 2020 it has added a second business: converting its own ready land and buildings at Manesar, Panchkula and Rai (Sonipat) into data centres that rent out computing capacity and power to customers, and from October 2024 its own 'Ashok Cloud' service sold to businesses. Operations are concentrated in the Delhi-NCR region (Haryana, with a first Delhi project started in 2025); revenue rose from about ₹1,004 crore in FY23 to about ₹2,100 crore in FY25, and management says the company is now effectively net debt-free with surplus cash after a ₹1,100 crore QIP fundraise.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Real estate – residential (group housing, independent floors, villas and plots)** — Builds and sells homes, mostly on its Gurugram Sector 63A land where it holds around 200 acres (about 97 acres left to develop, good for roughly 4.5 million sq ft), with 2.6 million sq ft of launches planned in FY26 and projects like Ashok Estates, Estate Residences and The Estate One — this is the company's main cash earner and by far the largest share of revenue.
- **Birla Navya (JV with Birla Estates)** — A roughly 43-acre joint-venture housing project in Gurugram with Birla Estates (owned by Century Textiles), where phases have been launched and about 228 units of Phase I had been handed over by late 2024.
- **Affordable / low-cost housing** — Applies for land from state governments and builds low-cost housing — the completed Neemrana project was described as a very good cash earner and the formula is being repeated elsewhere, such as Tirupati, though it is a smaller line than the Gurugram residential business.
- **Commercial (leasing and new commercial development)** — Owns about 5 million sq ft of ready commercial buildings that it leases out with facilities, and has started two new projects — Ashok Towers in Sector 63A and a first 7 lakh sq ft Delhi development combining commercial, service apartments and a hotel, with Phase 1 due by FY28.
- **Data centre business** — Runs co-location data centres on land and buildings it already owned — 21 MW at Manesar plus 7 MW at Panchkula delivered (28 MW total, showcased in August 2025), 35 MW more under construction targeting 63 MW by December 2026, and a 20 MW first phase at Rai, Sonipat out of a planned 200 MW there; it contributed ₹58.4 crore of the ₹1,223 crore H1 FY26 revenue, so it is still small in the numbers but is management's main growth focus.
- **Cloud services – 'Ashok Cloud'** — A newer add-on launched in October 2024 (following a March 2024 agreement with partner TCIL) selling infrastructure-as-a-service to business customers on its own servers, described as a sovereign Indian cloud, with plans to extend into platform- and software-as-a-service; management said adding servers can lift revenue per megawatt several times over plain co-location.

### The story in depth

Anant Raj's best qualities sit with its owners and its end market. The family are fourth-generation owner-operators who accumulated land for decades, held it through a nine-year Gurgaon downturn, and spent four years building a data-centre business before it earned a rupee; they scored the top mark on both stewardship checks. They also did what they said: net debt fell from INR988cr in FY23 to INR290cr in FY24 to INR95cr by September 2024 and then to a net cash position, the cost of debt dropped from about 20% to below 10%, the first 28MW was funded internally, and FY25 guidance of INR1,800-1,900cr revenue was beaten at INR2,100cr. Almost all the money going out is growth spending rather than upkeep - IT load has gone 3MW to 6MW to 28MW, with 63MW guided by December 2026 and 117MW by FY28, at roughly INR26cr per MW against about INR75 lakh per MW per month of EBITDA, which pays back in about three years. The end market is expanding for structural reasons - data-localisation rules and a pending Data Protection Act - and the collection cycle is short and healthy at about 26 days. That is a genuinely strong management and growth story, and it is why the Management area scores 0.83 and Growth 0.5.

The trouble is that the single asset making all of this possible - the old, already-owned land - is also what caps the score, and it shows up on both sides of the ledger. Cheap legacy land is why the company can build a megawatt for INR25-26cr while, in management's words, others spend almost double, and why it can price colocation at roughly half the market and still make money. But that same land makes the business asset-heavy: every INR1 of assets produces only INR0.37 of sales, against a roughly 200-acre Gurgaon land bank plus land and buildings valued near INR3,000cr. The headline return check is the worst in the file - for every INR100 of capital employed the business produced minus INR7 of operating cash this year - and the Return on Capital area scores a flat -1.0. Growth has averaged about 16% a year but arrives in lumps rather than steadily. Nor is the expansion being paid for by the business alone: the record shows repeated share issuance - a INR500cr QIP in January 2024, a INR2,100cr fundraise plan announced November 2024, a INR1,100cr QIP completed in FY26 - and no dividend or buyback policy anywhere in the calls. On the multibagger screen this produces an unusually flat result: seven of eleven patterns are only PARTIAL and four are outright NO FIT, giving a multibagger fit of just 10 out of 100. Recurring revenue is real (INR90 lakh per MW per month, capacity tied up before commissioning, cloud sold as a subscription) but never quantified as renewals; pricing power is cyclical Gurgaon appreciation plus data-centre tariffs management concedes are inflation-linked; the heritage name stretches across Ashok Estates, Ashok Towers and Ashok Cloud without any marketing or distribution muscle behind it; and there is no disclosed R&D at all - the cloud business was handed over by a client with handholding, built on an Orange/OEM tie-up, with management admitting it lacks in-house expertise.

The risk review then turns the same facts into exposures, which is why the combined grade lands at Weak (28 out of 100) with resilience described as STRAINED. The cost advantage that drives the Low Price Plus pattern is exactly what the Cost to Replicate check rejects: management itself frames the moat as ready land and pre-certified buildings worth a three-to-four-year head start, which a funded rival can simply rebuild, and it concedes rentals will move when competition arrives - so New Entrants is ELEVATED and Good Enough Goods is ELEVATED, because pure colocation is a rack-and-power product bought on price and the company has chosen to compete as the cheap option. The policy tailwind that earns full marks on structural end-market growth is the same thing that makes Government Dependency a HIGH RISK: the entire data-centre demand case rests on localisation law, a pending Data Protection Act, a proposed tax benefit and sovereign-cloud positioning, while the property arm depends on state licences and RERA. The customer base compounds it - colocation clients were 75% government as of November 2025, and the same state-linked names (RailTel, TCIL, CAC) have been the route to customers since May 2023, with TCIL also the partner that started the cloud business, making Stakeholder Concentration ELEVATED. Meanwhile the core property business remains a classic HIGH RISK cycle that management describes in cycle terms itself, and the move up the technology stack from colocation to IaaS to promised PaaS and SaaS, aimed at competing with global hyperscalers on kit specified years ago, is ELEVATED. There are real offsets in each case - net cash and QIP funding rather than debt-fuelled expansion, 70% end-user rather than investor property sales, a JV with Birla, a staged 0.5MW cloud pilot, a client base management says is broadening with private and e-commerce names to a 50-50 split in cloud, and a niche sovereign-cloud certification. The honest reading is a well-run, honestly-communicated, self-disciplined family business that has monetised a dormant land bank into a fast-growing business - but one whose advantage is a head start rather than a durable barrier, whose customer is largely the state, and which is not yet turning capital into cash.

## What to watch

- Cash return on capital: the headline check is -7% this year while capex runs hard toward 63MW by December 2026 and 117MW by FY28 - the verdict changes if operating cash turns positive as the paid-for megawatts fill, or gets worse if it does not.
- Pricing when rivals arrive: management already concedes rentals are market-set and will move 'when the competition starts kicking in' - any drop below the roughly half-of-market pricing would hit both the Low Price Plus pattern and the New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks at once.
- Government share of the customer base: colocation is 75% government and cloud is 50-50 - the Stakeholder Concentration and Government Dependency risks ease only if the private and e-commerce additions management describes actually shift that mix, and worsen if RailTel, TCIL or CAC stay the sole channel.
- Equity issuance versus distribution: three raises in roughly two years (INR500cr, a INR2,100cr plan, INR1,100cr) with no dividend or buyback policy - further dilution without a matching step-up in cash generation would confirm the negative distribution check rather than the strong stewardship one.
- Execution on the technology move: PaaS and SaaS were promised on top of IaaS with admittedly thin in-house expertise and no disclosed R&D - delivery here would start to fill the Forward Integrators and Innovation Dominance gaps; slippage would validate the ELEVATED Technological Innovation risk.
- Policy follow-through: the demand case leans on the pending Data Protection Act and a proposed data-centre tax benefit - passage strengthens the structural growth check, delay or dilution weakens the whole data-centre thesis.
