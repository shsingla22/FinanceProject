# Anant Raj (ANANTRAJ) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Anant Raj Limited is a family-run Indian property developer, in the business since 1902 and listed on the NSE and BSE, that builds and sells homes and commercial buildings mainly in the Delhi NCR region — Gurugram (its 200-acre land bank in Sector 63A is the core asset), Haryana towns like Manesar and Panchkula, Neemrana, Sonipat, and more recently Delhi itself, plus an affordable-housing project in Tirupati. Most of its money comes from selling residential units — group housing flats, villas, plots and low-rise independent floors — to individual home buyers, with cash collected as construction progresses; it also builds and leases commercial space and had roughly 5 million sq ft of leased commercial property from its earlier years. Since about 2020 it has added a second business: converting its own ready land and buildings into data centres that rent computing space and power to corporate customers, and from October 2024 its own 'Ashok Cloud' service. Revenue was about INR 1,004 crore in FY23, INR 1,521 crore in FY24 and INR 2,100 crore in FY25 (PAT INR 425 crore); net debt was cut from INR 988 crore in FY23 to near zero, and the company describes itself as net cash positive after a INR 1,100 crore QIP in FY26.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Real estate — residential (group housing, villas, floors, plots)** — Builds and sells homes for individual buyers, concentrated in Sector 63A Gurugram, across formats like the Anant Raj Estate phases, Ashok Estates, Estate Residences and the upcoming luxury high-rise 'The Estate One' (5.1 acres, ~1.1 million sq ft); this is the company's 'legacy' business and still supplies the large majority of revenue — of H1 FY26 revenue of INR 1,223 crore, only INR 58 crore came from data centres, so essentially everything else is property.
- **Data centre business (co-location)** — Rents out secure, powered space for customers' computer servers at Manesar, Panchkula and Rai (Sonipat), scaled from 3 MW in FY23 to 28 MW delivered by mid-2025, with 63 MW targeted by December 2026 and a long-term planned capacity of about 300 MW across the three sites; revenue was INR 35.47 crore in Q2 FY26 and management calls it the company's main growth and fund-raising priority.
- **Cloud services — 'Ashok Cloud'** — Launched October 2024 as a India-based ('sovereign') cloud sold to business customers, starting with infrastructure-as-a-service and intended to extend to platform and software services; management says putting servers in alongside co-location can raise revenue per megawatt roughly five-fold (from about INR 90 lakh to INR 4–4.5 crore per MW), making Anant Raj a one-stop provider of co-location, servers and cloud.
- **Real estate — commercial and leasing** — Builds offices and other commercial buildings and leases them out with facilities — the historic core of the group (about 5 million sq ft built) — and is now developing two new projects: Ashok Towers in Sector 63A and a first 7 lakh sq ft mixed commercial, serviced-apartment and hotel development in Delhi, whose first phase is due by FY28.
- **Joint venture with Birla Estates ('Birla Navya')** — A 43-acre Gurugram residential project developed with Birla Estates (owned by Century Textiles), where three phases have been launched and handover of Phase I began with about 228 units delivered by late 2024.
- **Low-cost / affordable housing** — Applies for government land and builds low-cost housing on it — the completed Neemrana project became a good cash generator and management is repeating the formula elsewhere, with work started on an affordable housing project in Tirupati; it is a smaller, supplementary line versus the main Gurugram business.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Anant Raj is a fourth-generation family property business in Gurgaon that has spent the last few years converting decades-old land holdings into data centres, and the clearest thing in its favour is that it does what it says it will do. Corporate Culture is the only pattern in the screen rated a strong fit: management promised to be net-debt-free by December 2024 and got there, with net debt falling from ₹988 crore in FY23 to ₹290 crore to ₹95 crore and staying below ₹50 crore for five straight quarters, while profit came in at ₹425 crore against ₹370-390 crore guided and FY25 revenue at ₹2,100 crore against ₹1,800-1,900 crore guided. That record is what earns the top marks on the quality checks for disciplined stewardship and for being an independent, long-term owner that sat through a seven-to-nine year downturn. The spending itself also looks like the right kind: almost all of it is growth capex into data centres at about ₹26 crore per megawatt against roughly ₹75 lakh per megawatt per month of operating profit, a payback of about three years, taking delivered capacity from 3MW to 28MW with 63MW and 117MW planned. And it is aimed at a genuinely expanding market, with data-localisation law and the proposed Data Protection Act behind it and capacity described as completely tied up.

The trouble is that almost every strength here is the flip side of a weakness, often the very same fact read the other way. The owned, unencumbered land and buildings worth about ₹3,000 crore are what let the company build a megawatt for roughly half what management says rivals spend, and that is the whole basis of the Cost to Replicate and Low Price Plus patterns. But the same land bank is why every ₹1 of assets throws off only ₹0.37 of sales, and why the asset turn check scores badly. Being the low-cost builder also makes the company the cheap challenger pitching at about half market rates, which is precisely why the Good Enough Goods and New Entrants risks are both rated elevated: colocation is close to a commodity of space, power and cooling, management concedes rents are set by the market and will fall as competition arrives, and the incoming competitors are far better funded. The screen notes the head start is measured in years, not decades. The same doubling applies to policy. Data localisation is scored as a genuine structural tailwind on the quality side and as a high risk on the other, because the entire demand thesis rests on one government policy, the anchor partners RailTel and TCIL are both government undertakings, colocation is roughly 75% government and the cloud business about half. One decision changes both the opportunity and the exposure. Likewise the move up the value chain into Ashok Cloud, which earns the Forward Integrators pattern at four to five times the revenue per megawatt, is the same move that makes technology risk elevated, since management openly says it lacks expertise and leans on a client and on Orange, and puts far more capital per megawatt at stake than the ₹26 crore colocation conversion. Innovation Dominance is a clear no fit.

What is missing is the proof that any of this pays. The return on capital check is the weakest in the file: for every ₹100 of shareholders' funds and borrowings, the business produced minus ₹7 of operating cash this year, and the return-on-capital area scores at the bottom of the range. Growth has averaged about 16% a year but arrives in lumps rather than steadily. The debt-free record has been part-funded by shareholders rather than by the business, with a ₹500 crore share sale in FY24, ₹1,100 crore in FY26 and ₹100 crore of promoter warrants at ₹730, and no dividend or buyback policy discussed at all, so money has flowed in from owners rather than out to them. The competitive-advantage and industry-structure areas both score negative, and seven of the eleven patterns are only partial fits, with three not present. Against that, capital allocation, customer benefits, growth and management all score positively. The combined rating puts business quality at 51, pattern fit at 25 and risk safety at 18, weighted to 35 out of 100, a Mixed two-star verdict with resilience described as strained. In plain terms, this is a credible, honest operator building cheaply into a real market, on a balance sheet that can absorb the property cycle, but with no evidence yet of durable pricing advantage or cash returns, and a demand base concentrated in one policy and one set of government-linked customers.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Technological Innovation, New Entrants, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.1× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ⚠️ Only about -14% of recent operating profit became cash (was 9%) — profits are getting stuck in paper, an early stress signal.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 3% to 11% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 3 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** The core business is Gurgaon residential real estate, a classic price cycle that management itself describes in cycle terms — a seven-to-nine year recession that turned in 2021, ~60% price appreciation, and deliberately withholding inventory because prices keep rising; revenue tripled from ~INR1,004cr (FY23) to INR2,100cr (FY25) on that upswing, and the second leg (data centres) is a capex-heavy build tied to enterprise/hyperscaler capacity cycles.

> "Gurgaon had gone through a big recession for almost seven to nine years, and this only started changing in 2021... prices have gone up by almost 60%" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Net-zero-debt / net-cash balance sheet with cost of debt below 10%, land and buildings already owned and unencumbered, and a growing annuity-like data centre rental stream (~INR58cr in H1 FY26) that is operations-linked rather than one-time sales*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 4 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from 14% to 52% (swinging ±9.9 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 37% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 3% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** The entire data centre demand thesis is a government policy — data localisation — repeated across every call; the anchor channel partners RailTel and TCIL are both Government of India undertakings, colocation is ~75% government and cloud 50-50, the cloud is positioned as a 'sovereign cloud', and management is openly counting on a proposed DC tax benefit. The real estate side is equally licence- and approval-gated (RERA, LOIs, land licences, government land for affordable housing).

> "the Government of India primarily thinks that the Indian data should not be allowed to store outside the country... this law is here to stay, sir. So, demand here is only going to go up" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Private clients are being added on both colocation and cloud, and the real estate business — still the large majority of revenue — is sold to private end-users rather than to the state*

## What to watch
