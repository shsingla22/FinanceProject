# Great Eastern Shipping Co. Ltd. (GESHIP) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: SERVICES*

The Great Eastern Shipping Company (GESHIP) is a Mumbai-headquartered, BSE/NSE-listed company that owns ocean-going ships and hires them out to move other people's cargo — crude oil, refined petroleum products, LPG and dry bulk commodities like iron ore, coal and grain — across global trade routes such as the Middle East to Asia, US to Asia and Atlantic to Pacific. It makes money from the daily hire rate its ships earn, either on the open 'spot' market (rates quoted in dollars per day, which swing widely with oil and commodity trade flows) or on fixed-period 'time charters' that lock in a rate for months or years; management repeatedly says a large part of its capacity sits on the spot market, which is why it refuses to forecast earnings. Through its subsidiary Greatship (India) Limited it runs a second, separate business renting out offshore oilfield equipment — jack-up drilling rigs and support vessels — mostly on time charter to oil companies in India and the Middle East. Alongside operating the ships, management actively trades the assets themselves, buying and selling vessels to time the cycle, and tracks a per-share 'net asset value' (the fleet's market value plus cash) as its main scorecard; the business throws off heavy cash, has paid 16 consecutive quarterly dividends, and by FY26 held over $500 million of net cash.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Crude tankers** — Ships that carry unrefined crude oil (Suezmax-class is the size management quotes); a core earner whose rates swing hard with events — around $46,000/day in Q1 FY25 versus $5,500/day for the market in FY22 — and the segment behind the record March–April 2026 spike from the Strait of Hormuz disruption.
- **Product tankers** — Ships carrying refined fuels such as diesel and other middle distillates (MR-class); comparably important to crude tankers, and the biggest single beneficiary of the Red Sea rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope, which lengthened voyages and pushed rates to roughly $36,000/day in FY23 from $6,000/day the year before.
- **Dry bulk carriers** — Ships carrying iron ore, coal and grain (Capesize and Supramax sizes), driven mainly by Chinese import demand; a meaningful but more volatile leg — rates fell over 50% year-on-year in Q2 FY23 and were unusually strong in the seasonally weak Q4 FY26.
- **LPG carriers** — A small fleet — four ships as of FY23 — carrying liquefied petroleum gas, kept entirely on fixed time charters, so the company collects steady contracted hire and deliberately does not participate in the spot market's swings.
- **Offshore drilling rigs (Greatship / oilfield services)** — Jack-up rigs chartered out to oil companies in India and internationally, run through subsidiary Greatship (India) Limited; a predominantly time-charter business where a handful of rigs come up for repricing each year, and the main reason consolidated profit exceeds standalone (e.g. INR 813 crore consolidated versus INR 650 crore standalone in Q3 FY26 — so roughly a fifth of group profit).
- **Offshore support vessels (OSVs)** — Vessels servicing offshore oil platforms, also under Greatship and mostly on time charter with a large share of days locked in ahead (about 80% of FY27 days already fixed as of May 2026); management describes a global fleet of ~3,000 ships with 500–700 in long-term 'cold layup', supporting a tightening supply picture.
- **Ship buying and selling (asset play)** — Not a separate reporting segment but treated as a core profit driver — management times the cycle by selling older vessels and replacing them with newer ones ('switch transactions'), levering up when assets are cheap and staying net cash when they are dear, with net asset value per share compounding above 20% a year over five years.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Great Eastern Shipping comes out of these three reviews as a very well-run company inside a very ordinary business, and the combined score of 42 out of 100 — a Mixed, two-star rating built from 40 for business quality, 25 for multibagger fit and 64 for risk safety — is essentially that split expressed as a number. Almost everything positive traces back to the people running it: Management scores 1.8 out of a possible 2, with top marks for candour (refusing to give guidance, admitting outright when they don't know, and volunteering unprompted that the big cash pile is dragging returns down), for disciplined stewardship (borrowing up to $360m of net debt in FY17-19 to buy ships, then swinging $700m the other way to more than $500m of net cash by the peak), and for staying out of the limelight (calls run by the CFO, no forecasts, no promotional language). That same behaviour is what makes Corporate Culture the one pattern out of eleven rated a STRONG FIT — the same CFO on all fifteen calls, sixteen consecutive quarterly dividends, and net asset value compounding at 20-24% over five years.

The business underneath that management is where the marks fall away, and the pattern screen and the risk review are describing the same thing from two directions. Competitive advantage scores -1.67 and industry structure -1.17: there is no mini-monopoly (a pure commodity spot-freight business, by the company's own choice), no oligopoly (a fragmented global market of thousands of vessels where the company is a rate-taker with no negotiating power), no brand or taste-based pricing power, and no technology edge — the only technical work mentioned in four years of calls is fuel and emissions saving that every shipowner pursues. Those same absences are why Pricing Power, Brand Strength, Cost to Replicate and Toll Roads are all rated NO FIT, and why the Good Enough Goods risk sits on WATCH: freight is a price-taking service with no premium to defend, and older ships the company sells get redeployed by cheaper owners elsewhere. Ownership of the swing is deliberate — management explicitly prefers spot exposure to locked-in contracts, which is exactly why Recurring Revenue is a NO FIT, and it is also the direct cause of the HIGH RISK cyclicality verdict, with Suezmax spot rates moving from $5,500 a day in FY22 to $41,000 in FY23 and daily earnings swinging on events nobody can forecast.

Several traits do double duty as both the strength and the hazard, and it is worth naming them. The heavy asset base scores -2 on asset turn — every ₹1 of assets produces ₹0.28 of sales, with roughly ₹15,000 crore of fleet earning about 12-13% pre-tax on replacement value even at cyclical points — yet that same fleet is the instrument management uses to time purchases and sales, which is what the counter-cyclical record is built on. The large net cash position is simultaneously the silver lining on the cyclicality risk (they are not buying because asset prices are high) and a self-admitted drag on returns, and it shows up in the flat scores for capital allocation (0.0), growth (-0.5) and return on capital (-0.33), plus the NO FIT on Market Share Gainers, where capacity has been deliberately flat-to-shrinking with old ships sold and switch transactions replacing rather than adding tonnage. On the outside risks, three channels sit at WATCH rather than HIGH: government dependency (OPEC output, Russian oil embargoes, the Strait of Hormuz, Panama Canal rules, and Indian PSU tenders for the offshore arm), stakeholder concentration (the Greatship offshore arm repeatedly hostage to named counterparties, including Aramco cancellations and around 24-25 rigs called back in FY26), and good enough goods. Technological innovation is LOW, new entrants and shifting consumer preferences show no signal from the calls — though the barriers-to-entry check separately flags that capital floods into this industry whenever returns are good, with order books surging. Overall resilience is judged SOUND. The honest reading: you are buying capable, honest, patient operators, not a protected business, and the returns will follow the shipping cycle rather than escape it.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 3 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across all 15 calls the business is explicitly framed as a commodity freight cycle: Suezmax spot rates swung from $5,500/day (FY22) to $41,000/day (FY23), dry bulk fell >50% y-o-y in Nov 2022, and the offshore/rig arm went through a multi-year downturn with Saudi Aramco off-hiring ~24-25 jack-ups before recovering. Management refuses guidance, keeps most tonnage on spot, and by Feb 2026 admits current-yield returns on replacement-cost assets are only ~12-13% at peak-cycle asset prices — the classic peak-earnings/peak-asset-value problem. Rig demand is directly tied to customers' (ONGC, Aramco, Reliance) capex programmes, the most dangerous form.

> "that could affect demand for commodities, and that's what really drives our business" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Countercyclical discipline: deliberately net cash >$500mn and refusing to buy at high asset prices ('which is also the reason why we are not investing today'), levering up only in FY17-19 troughs; NAV growth shown to come from cash profits rather than mark-to-market; consistent dividends even in weak markets.*
