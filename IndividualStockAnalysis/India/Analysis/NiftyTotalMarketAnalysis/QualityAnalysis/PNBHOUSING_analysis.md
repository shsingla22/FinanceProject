# PNB Housing Finance Ltd. (PNBHOUSING) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

PNB Housing Finance is an Indian housing finance company that lends money to people buying, building or borrowing against homes, and it is listed on the Indian stock exchanges (symbol PNBHOUSING) with Punjab National Bank as a shareholder. Almost all of its lending is retail home loans to individual borrowers, sold through a pan-India branch network that had grown to 393 branches across roughly 20 states by March 2026, with a deliberate tilt toward tier-2 and tier-3 cities. It funds itself by borrowing from banks and the debt market and by taking fixed deposits from the public, and it earns the gap between the interest it charges borrowers (yield around 9.7%-10%) and what it pays on borrowings (around 7.5%), a net interest margin of roughly 3.6%, plus fees. The loan book reached INR 87,347 crores as on 31 March 2026, serving over 3.6 lakh live accounts, with profit after tax of INR 2,291 crores in FY26.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Retail (home loans to individuals)** — The core business of lending to individual home buyers, which management grew from about 87% of the book in March 2022 to 97%-plus, standing at INR 86,946 crores as on 31 March 2026 after 16% annual growth.
- **Prime** — The higher-ticket, better-credit end of the retail home loan business, which is the oldest and largest slice of retail but the slowest-growing by design; it grew disbursements 43% year-on-year in Q4 FY26 and is where management says it faces the most balance-transfer and yield pressure.
- **Emerging Markets** — A separate vertical started in FY25 with dedicated branches (50 at launch, 87 by March 2026) targeting higher-yielding customers with an average ticket size around INR 25 lakhs; management repeatedly calls it the outperformer, growing disbursements 25%-34% year-on-year.
- **Affordable (earlier called Unnati)** — Small-ticket housing loans to lower-income borrowers, restarted as a dedicated vertical in January 2023 and built to INR 2,361 crores of book in 18 months, run out of 229 dedicated branches by March 2026; together with Emerging Markets it made up about 40% of the retail book.
- **Corporate / construction finance** — Loans to property developers, which the company deliberately ran down from INR 6,006 crores in June 2022 to INR 272 crores by December 2025 after heavy bad-debt problems, then restarted in Q4 FY26 with INR 335 crores of fresh disbursement and plans for calibrated construction finance in tier-1 cities plus 'Emerging Developer Finance' with INR 25-30 crore tickets.
- **Deposits** — A public fixed-deposit franchise that is one of its funding sources rather than a lending business; deposits grew 7% year-on-year as of Q1 FY25 with 88% coming from the retail public.
- **Co-lending with banks** — An arrangement where the company originates a loan and shares it with a bank in a 20:80 split; it was small (INR 76.5 crores disbursed in Q1 FY23) and management said it was in talks with more banks.
- **Government subsidy schemes (PMAY / CLSS)** — The company channels central-government housing subsidies to its borrowers, having sanctioned CLSS loans to over 65,000 customers for INR 13,000 crores and facilitated 5,000 subsidies under the newer PMAY round; management treats it as a demand driver for the Affordable and Emerging segments rather than a separate profit line.
- **Recoveries from the written-off pool** — An ongoing collections effort on loans already charged off, which recovered INR 332 crores in FY26 and actually pushed credit cost negative (minus 45 bps); around INR 500 crores in corporate and INR 325 crores in retail remain in that pool.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

PNB Housing's record over the last few years reads as a genuine repair-and-rebuild story, and that is where every positive mark sits. The end market itself scores the highest mark in the whole framework (+2): Indian mortgage lending is still only about 10% of GDP, PMAY has been widened to 3 crore houses, and urbanization plus the formalization of tier-2 and tier-3 towns give the demand a long runway. The company has spent into that runway rather than harvesting it — branches went from 34 affordable locations in FY23 to 393 by FY26, adding 40-50 a year, alongside an in-house technology programme (Salesforce systems, the Infinity onboarding app, AI-assisted calling). The payoff is visible: bad loans fell from 6.35% to 0.93% by March 2026, return on assets rose from 1.47% to 2.66%, and the loan book grew from Rs.56,301 crores in June 2022 to Rs.87,347 crores, with retail lending compounding 14-16% and disbursements up 19% in FY26. Growth scores 0.8 and capital allocation 0.67 — the two best areas — and management has largely delivered what it promised.

The problem is that none of this has bought the company any power over price, and that single fact links almost every negative finding together. The framework gives the worst possible mark (-2) three times: no pricing benefit from image, no consolidated industry structure, and switching costs that work against the company rather than for it. The same trait knocks out four of the eleven multibagger patterns outright. Pricing Power is a NO FIT because yields have fallen steadily from 10.03% to 9.72% to 9.47% as competition, repo cuts and customers refinancing away all pull the same direction. Toll Roads is a NO FIT because a home loan is one of the biggest items in a household budget, not a small charge nobody notices, and borrowers demonstrably move for 50-150 basis points. Low Price Plus is a NO FIT because borrowing costs 7.35% and management concedes it must match bank rates rather than undercut them. Cost to Replicate is a NO FIT because the stated advantages — three-to-four-day approval for self-employed borrowers, and branch count — are things a funded newcomer could rebuild. With a spread of only 2.12% and debt at 3.63 times equity, earnings come from leverage rather than from any protected margin, which is why return on capital scores -1.0 and industry structure -1.17.

The risk review then finds the same weakness wearing a different hat. Good Enough Goods is rated HIGH RISK for precisely the reason Pricing Power fails — the product is undifferentiated, rates are near-identical to banks for good customers, and a 100-150 basis point gap once triggered heavy refinancing away. New Entrants is a WATCH for the same reason from the other side: banks fund themselves more cheaply and compete directly in prime mortgages. Notably, the company's main strength is also its main defence — the branch build-out into affordable and emerging lending (now 39-40% of the retail book, targeted at 50%, with 79% of branches dedicated to it) is what earns credit under Forward Integrators, Geographical expansion and Capex for growth, and it is simultaneously the answer to the New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks. Whether it works is unproven. The second HIGH RISK, Government Dependency, cuts across the one asset that does help — PNB's backing and AA+ ratings, which support 88% public deposits and cheaper funding under a PARTIAL Brand Strength verdict, but also mean an RBI circular can add Rs.800 crores of bad loans and a rights issue needs regulatory approval. Add cyclicality, single large accounts that still move results (one Rs.275 crore account declared fraudulent in January 2026), and four managing directors in the period, and the picture is a well-run business inside a structurally hard industry. Every one of the eleven patterns comes back PARTIAL or NO FIT — none is a full fit — resilience is recorded as UNKNOWN, and the combined rating of 47 out of 100 (business quality 46, risk safety 48) lands squarely on Mixed.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Fortunes are repeatedly set by policy: the Nov-2021 RBI circular added ~Rs.800cr of NPAs, PNB's Rs.500cr rights-issue participation required RBI approval, PMAY/CLSS subsidy scheme is framed as 'a huge opportunity' with the scheme's lending cap and permitted margin determining NIM, pricing follows RBI's repo, and a state government ordinance forced a strategic retreat from affordable lending in Tamil Nadu, causing a 15% year-on-year disbursement decline in that segment.

> "the Affordable segment saw a 15% Y-o-Y drop and 4.5% QoQ decline in disbursement , driven by our strategic decision to recalibrate affordable business in few challenging geographies due to government ordinances" — *management, on an earnings call*
