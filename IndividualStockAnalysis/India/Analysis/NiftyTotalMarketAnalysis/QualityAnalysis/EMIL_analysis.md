# Electronics Mart Ind (EMIL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Electronics Mart India (EMIL) is a consumer-durables and electronics retailer in India — it buys branded goods from around 70 manufacturers and sells them to ordinary shoppers through large stores, mostly under the "Bajaj Electronics" name in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana and "Electronics Mart" in Delhi NCR. Its shelves carry air conditioners, televisions, washing machines, refrigerators, mobile phones, small appliances and IT products — over 6,000 SKUs — with easy EMI options, and management described the company at listing as the fourth largest such retailer in India and the largest organised player in the south. It makes money on the retail margin between buying and selling the goods, plus a sizeable stream of "other operating income" from brand incentives and commissions (about INR 2,021 million in H1 FY23), and the business is highly seasonal, peaking in the summer quarter (Q1) and the Diwali festive quarter (Q3). By FY26 it operated 223 stores across roughly four states, with revenue of INR 7,183 crores, EBITDA margin of 6.1% and PAT of INR 107 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Retail** — The core business — selling electronics and appliances to walk-in customers through company-run stores; it is essentially the whole company, contributing INR 11,105 million of the INR 12,277 million of Q2 FY23 revenue.
- **Multi-brand outlets (MBOs)** — Large one-stop stores stocking many brands under one roof, branded Bajaj Electronics in AP/Telangana and Electronics Mart in Delhi NCR; they are the bulk of the network (157 of 170 stores in June 2024).
- **Exclusive brand outlets (EBOs)** — Single-brand stores selling only one manufacturer's products such as Apple, LG or Samsung; a small part of the network, around 12-13 stores.
- **Specialised store formats (Kitchen Stories, Audio & Beyond)** — Niche stores focused on high-end kitchen appliances and on home cinema/networking solutions respectively; only a handful of stores, including one Kitchen Stories outlet in Kerala.
- **Wholesale (B2B)** — A small side business supplying stock to roughly 200 individual and mom-and-pop shops in AP and Telangana, mainly to help liquidate inventory; tiny at INR 92 million in Q2 FY23 versus over INR 12,000 million total.
- **E-commerce / online** — Listing about 200 selected SKUs on major online marketplaces as an extension of the store business; the smallest channel at INR 21 million in Q2 FY23.
- **Other operating income (incentives and commissions)** — Payments and incentives earned from the brands it stocks plus commissions — a meaningful profit contributor, at INR 1,050 million in Q2 FY23 and INR 2,021 million in H1 FY23.
- **Large appliances** — Air conditioners, televisions, washing machines, refrigerators, dishwashers and coolers — historically the biggest product category at 53% of revenue in Q1 FY25, easing to roughly 42-43% by FY26.
- **Mobile phones** — Smartphone sales, driven by new OEM launches; roughly 35% of revenue in Q1 FY25 and about 44% by Q3 FY26, growing 28% in Q4 FY26.
- **Small appliances / IT and other categories** — Smaller-ticket items such as kitchen and personal appliances and IT products; the smallest slice at about 12% of revenue in Q1 FY25.
- **South cluster (Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Kerala)** — The home market and profit engine, centred on Hyderabad where it claims very high market share; it held an EBITDA margin of about 6.5% in FY26 despite most new stores being opened there.
- **North / NCR cluster** — The newer Delhi NCR expansion started with 8 stores in August 2022, growing to 24 by mid-2024; it turned EBITDA positive but at thin margins (around 0.5% for 9M FY26) as stores are still maturing.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Electronics Mart is a consumer-durables retailer that scores as a solid, honestly-run business rather than an exceptional one — an overall 51 out of 100, or three stars, built from decent business quality (60), weak multibagger fit (18) and a fairly safe risk profile (76). What the quality framework likes is real: sales have compounded at roughly 15% a year inside the steady band the framework prizes, growth has been entirely organic with no acquisitions and prudent borrowing (net debt/equity 0.2x, net debt/EBITDA 0.36x), and the promoter-CEO has run the same cluster-deepening playbook since 2022 — including sticking with a loss-making Delhi NCR cluster for four full years until it finally turned profitable at the EBITDA level. The calls are plain and operational, run by just the CEO and CFO with no grand vision statements. The end market genuinely has room to grow: Indian households are under-penetrated across appliance categories, and newer ones like dishwashers, built-ins and induction are only beginning.

The trouble is that the traits driving the growth are the same traits that cap the quality. Only one of the eleven multibagger patterns is a likely fit — Market Share Gainers, supported by store count rising from about 100 in 2022 to 223 by March 2026, AP revenue up 18.2% in Q3FY26 and the NCR cluster up 30% — and even that fit is qualified because no actual share percentages are disclosed and most of the growth comes from opening stores rather than selling more from existing ones. Brand Strength and Corporate Culture are only partial: the 'Bajaj Electronics' name carries decades of weight in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana but travelled poorly to Delhi, which needed heavy launch advertising and four years to stabilise. Eight patterns are outright misses, and they miss for one shared reason: EMIL resells other companies' boxes. It keeps only ₹14 of every ₹100 of sales after direct costs and runs a 6.1% FY26 EBITDA margin, so there is no pricing power — management says price increases are simply passed through, growth is volume-led with flat realisations, and the recent demand lift came from a GST rate cut, which is a price reduction someone else granted. There is no R&D, no subscription or service-contract revenue, and no manufacturing. The moat is cluster density and good locations, which management itself concedes a funded competitor can and does rebuild — it describes competition in its home markets as 'very stiff'.

The risk review is reassuring precisely because the business is ordinary: six risks sit at Watch, two at Low, and the overall resilience reads Sound. But several risks are the mirror image of the strengths above. The cycle upturn the quality framework counts as a positive — a weak prior summer creating an easy comparison, the GST cut, income-tax relief and an AI-phone replacement wave, with Q4FY26 same-store growth already back to 12% — is the same thing the cyclicality and government-dependency risks flag: results swing hard on weather and season (same-store growth ran 8.6% in Q1FY25 but 0.19% over nine months of FY26), and the recent recovery is repeatedly credited to policy rather than to anything the company did. Thin margins connect to two more: profitability leans on brand incentive schemes booked as other operating income (₹2,021mn in H1FY23 against EBITDA of ₹1,724mn), and because the goods are identical to what every rival stocks, the premium rests entirely on store experience and location — management admits blended gross margins were 'poor' where competition was heaviest. The largest structural question is online: for a store-based retailer, the migration to internet buying is a genuine erosion channel, and EMIL's own online business is negligible at ₹21 million in Q2FY23, with only about 200 of its 6,000 SKUs listed on marketplaces. Offsetting that, demand is functional and replacement-driven across 70 brands, the model is technology-agnostic so new products drive upgrades rather than threaten the format, and the company added stores counter-cyclically through the weak patch.
