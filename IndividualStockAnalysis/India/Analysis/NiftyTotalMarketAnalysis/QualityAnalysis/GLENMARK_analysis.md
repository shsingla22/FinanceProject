# Glenmark Pharmaceuticals Ltd. (GLENMARK) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: PHARMA*

Glenmark Pharmaceuticals is an India-based drugmaker that makes and sells finished medicines — both cheaper copies of off-patent drugs (generics) and its own or in-licensed branded medicines — mainly in skin (dermatology), breathing (respiratory) and cancer (oncology) treatments, plus heart and diabetes drugs in India. It earns money by selling these medicines to doctors, pharmacies, hospitals and distributors in about 80 countries, split across four regions: India, North America, Europe and Emerging Markets (Russia/CIS, Latin America, Middle East & Africa, Asia-Pacific); in some markets it sells through partner companies rather than directly. On top of product sales, it earns licensing income by discovering new drug molecules in its own labs and out-licensing them to larger drugmakers — management says it has earned over $300 million this way historically, including a deal with AbbVie in FY26 worth $700 million upfront. Full-year FY26 revenue was about INR 169,825 million (roughly INR 1.7 lakh crore-scale sales base of ~$1.5 billion when management last sized it), with the four regions each now contributing broadly similar amounts.

**The parts of the business:**

- **India formulations business** — Sells branded prescription medicines to Indian doctors across dermatology, respiratory, cardiac, diabetes and oncology; it is the single largest region (about 37% of revenue in FY23 and roughly INR 10,000-13,000 million a quarter by FY26), ranked 13th in the Indian pharma market with ~2.3% share and second in both dermatology and respiratory.
- **India Consumer Care (GCC)** — A small over-the-counter consumer brands arm selling products like Candid Powder, La Shield, Scalpe, Bontress and Elovera directly to shoppers; it is a modest but fast-growing piece, around INR 1,265 million in the March 2026 quarter.
- **North America business** — Mainly a US generics operation selling copies of off-patent drugs — 214 approved generic products, plus a growing injectables portfolio (about 20 products sold through partners) and generic respiratory sprays/inhalers — running roughly INR 9,000-9,700 million a quarter, and it also books the out-licensing income from the AbbVie deal.
- **Europe business** — Sells generics in Western Europe (Germany, Netherlands, Spain, Nordics) and increasingly higher-margin branded respiratory and dermatology products in Central & Eastern Europe (Poland, Czech Republic, Slovakia); about INR 8,000-8,900 million a quarter and management is deliberately shifting the mix toward branded.
- **Emerging Markets (earlier called ROW)** — Covers Russia/CIS, Latin America, Middle East & Africa and Asia-Pacific, where the company sells largely branded medicines in dermatology and respiratory; around INR 8,100-9,000 million a quarter and growing at high single to double digits.
- **RYALTRIS (global branded product)** — The company's flagship self-developed nasal spray for allergic rhinitis, filed in over 90 countries and commercialised in 52 markets — sold directly by Glenmark in some countries and through partners (Menarini, Hikma, Grand Pharmaceutical, Organon, Yuhan) in others — with 50%+ yearly sales growth and described by management as transformational.
- **Innovation R&D / Ichnos Glenmark Innovation (IGI)** — The in-house new-drug discovery arm working on novel cancer and other molecules using its BEAT antibody platform; it does not sell products but generates upfront and milestone payments plus royalties by licensing assets out, most notably ISB 2001 to AbbVie (up to $1.925 billion potential deal value), with innovation spend cut back to about $50 million a year.
- **In-licensed specialty/branded portfolio** — Medicines developed by other companies that Glenmark buys the local selling rights to and markets in India, Europe or emerging markets — such as JABRYUS (from Pfizer) in skin disease, WINLEVI (from Cosmo) in acne, and TEVIMBRA/BRUKINSA (from BeOne) and envafolimab in cancer — used to move the sales mix up-market.
- **Glenmark Life Sciences** — A separately reported subsidiary referenced in the earlier calls that supplies active pharmaceutical ingredients (the raw drug substances), reported on alongside the main formulations business.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Glenmark's strongest single trait is that its demand is created by doctors rather than bought from end-customers, and that one trait explains most of what the framework likes about it. The multibagger screen calls Friendly Middleman a strong fit — rankings of #2 in dermatology and #2/#3 in respiratory, 500 extra India medical representatives added in FY25, products judged by how prescribers receive them. That prescriber pull is what turns into the Brand Strength and Market Share Gainers patterns: 11 brands now in the Indian market's top 300, Candid Powder at 56% share of its category, India secondary sales growing 13.5% in FY26 against an industry at 9% — second-fastest among the top 15 players — while still holding only about 2.3% of the Indian market at rank 13, so there is plenty of room left. The quality checks agree, scoring both market share gain and structural end-market growth at the top mark, and the company keeps 72 rupees of every 100 of sales after direct input costs, with 31 rupees of operating cash generated per 100 rupees of capital employed. Competitive Advantage and Growth are the two best-scoring quality areas at 1.5 out of 2.

The same company, viewed from its other end, is where both high risks sit — and they are the mirror image of the strengths, not separate problems. In US generics the chooser is the pharmacist or the benefit manager, not the patient, so brand awareness counts for nothing; the risk review rates Good Enough Goods a high risk on exactly that basis, and management itself concedes continuous price erosion from 2022 through FY26 and describes the US business as structurally loss-making on price with poor fixed-cost absorption. That is why the Pricing Power and Low Price Plus patterns both come back as no fit, and why the Industry Structure quality area scores zero. The regulator, not the customer, has repeatedly set the company's results: the Monroe plant stayed out of commercial production until the clearance in November 2025 and Goa remediation ran for years, making Government Dependency the second high risk. Yet the same regulatory machinery also produces the moat — the first-to-file generic Flovent inhaler with its 180-day exclusivity is a regulatory prize, and it is one of the two assets the Cost to Replicate pattern rests on. Innovation cuts the same way: the proprietary BEAT platform and the AbbVie deal for ISB-2001 (700 million dollars upfront, up to 1.925 billion) score full marks for research and technology and carry the Innovation Dominance pattern, but the flip side is that portfolios go obsolete — the diabetes franchise was hollowed out when dapagliflozin went generic and GLP-1s reset the class, and small-molecule oncology is being displaced by newer formats, forcing the company to buy in what it lacks. Buying it in is precisely what creates the Stakeholder Concentration watch item: the seven or eight innovation-led global assets meant to drive the next five years come mostly from a handful of licensors including BeOne, Hengrui, Hansoh, Alphamab, Pfizer and Cosmo.

The combined rating of 60 out of 100 — Decent, three stars — comes from 73 on business quality, 49 on pattern fit and 48 on risk safety, and that spread is the story: a genuinely good business operating in a poor industry structure. Resilience is judged sound, helped by the two low risks — demand is chronic, prescription-driven and consumed continuously, so there is no cyclical link to any customer's capital spending, and it is clinically driven rather than fashion-driven. Management earns full marks for candor, having listed its own five or six failures publicly (US generics, FDA audits, overspending on innovation, litigation, leverage) and then delivered the specific promises of net cash by FY25 and no gross debt by March 2026, under the same promoter-CEO for 25 years. Even so, the Corporate Culture pattern only reaches partial and Capital Allocation is the weakest scored quality area after industry structure, at 0.67 — a reminder that owning past mistakes is not the same as having proved the money is now well spent. The remaining soft spots are consistent with each other: profit margin scores only 1 because the 27% operating margin has swung about 4 points a year, meaning key costs sit outside management's control; there is no repeat or subscription revenue at all, so every sale is a one-off funded by the company's own working capital, which is why management is targeting 115 days of net working capital; and the move toward the customer — taking Ryaltris US commercialisation in-house from its partner in April 2026, and the consumer-care franchise grown fivefold to 3 billion rupees — is real but too early to score more than partial.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Regulators, not customers, have repeatedly determined Glenmark's fortunes across the five years: adverse USFDA actions kept Monroe out of commercial production from before Aug 2022 until the VAI/EIR in Nov 2025, Goa remediation ran for years, and quarterly revenue and margin still hinge on approval timing — Q4 FY26 US margins missed purely because Flovent/nasal spray approvals slipped. Europe and US institutional business is also explicitly tender-driven, and India pricing sits under CDSCO/NLEM oversight.

> "product approvals coming out of the FDA are unpredictable" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Deliberate diversification away from the policy-exposed US generic channel — branded share moving from 55% (FY19) to 60% (FY24) with a 70-75% target by FY29, and revenue spread across India, Europe, LatAm, Russia/CIS, MEA and APAC*

### Good Enough Goods — HIGH RISK
*"Cheaper rivals that are good enough"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** A large part of the business is itself the good-enough good: US generics, where pharmacists/PBMs — not patients — choose, brand awareness is nil, and management concedes continuous price erosion that no manufacturer escapes; Glenmark's US revenue was flat-to-declining for years and core growth was still only ~4-8% in FY26 ex the AbbVie licensing income. The mitigation is exactly the Nobel Biocare lesson applied deliberately — retreat into niches challengers cannot cheaply copy (inhalation devices, injectables, in-licensed novel molecules, branded India/EM franchises).

> "We also continue to see price erosion in the US market. All manufacturers do" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Explicit 'move up the value chain' strategy — branded share 55%→60%→target 70-75%, scale/complexity-protected respiratory devices and injectables, and India brand equity (2nd in derma, 2nd in respiratory, Candid Powder >56% share)*

## What to watch

- Whether the branded share actually climbs toward the stated 70-75% by FY29 target from 60% — this is the single lever that would downgrade both high risks (Good Enough Goods and Government Dependency) at once.

- Whether the Monroe clearance holds and no new adverse FDA action lands; a repeat would re-confirm Government Dependency as the dominant driver of results rather than a fading one.
- Whether operating margin stops swinging by roughly 4 points a year and US fixed-cost absorption improves — the Profit margin check scores only 1 today, and steadier margins would lift it.
- Whether India share gain continues at the FY26 pace of 13.5% against a 9% market, since the Market Share Gainers and Friendly Middleman patterns — the strongest part of the case — rest on that continuing.
- Whether net working capital reaches the 115-day target, given that the Recurring Revenue pattern is a clear no fit and every sale is self-funded.
