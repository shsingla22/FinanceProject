# Adani Green Energy Ltd. (ADANIGREEN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

Adani Green Energy Limited (AGEL) builds and runs large-scale renewable power plants in India — solar farms, wind farms and combined solar-wind 'hybrid' plants — and sells the electricity they generate. Most of that power goes out under long-term power purchase agreements to government-backed buyers such as state distribution companies (management said about 87% of its portfolio was 'sovereign' in FY24), with the rest sold to commercial and industrial customers or on the open 'merchant' market when a plant is running ahead of its contracted start date. Essentially all revenue is 'revenue from power supply' — INR 11,602 crores for FY26, up 22% year on year — and because a built plant costs little to run, EBITDA margins are extremely high, around 91%-94%. The company has grown operating capacity from 8.3 GW in mid-2023 to over 20 GW by mid-2026, is targeting 50 GW by 2030, and is now adding battery and pumped-hydro storage so it can supply power at night and when the grid needs it.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Solar** — Ground-mounted solar plants that generate power during daylight hours; it is the largest technology in the portfolio, with capacity utilisation around 28% overall (higher, ~32%, at the Khavda site) and a build cost management quotes at about INR 4.5 crores per MW.
- **Wind** — Wind farms that generate at higher utilisation than solar (38.7% CUF in Q1 FY24) and, importantly, produce more in the evening — management notes wind fetched far better merchant prices (Rs 5.70/unit) than solar (Rs 2.20/unit) in Q1 FY26; wind costs more to build, roughly INR 6.5 crores per MW.
- **Hybrid (solar-wind)** — Plants combining solar and wind on one site and one grid connection, which lifts utilisation sharply (47.2% CUF in Q1 FY24) and gives steadier output through the day; hybrid made up the bulk of the 2.5 GW added in the year to June 2023.
- **Khavda, Gujarat mega-site** — A single 30 GW renewable complex described by management as the world's largest renewable energy installation and the engine of growth — 5.6 GW operational by June 2025 and over 10 GW by mid-2026 — prized for its exceptional sunlight and wind, though limited transmission lines out of the site have caused curtailment costing roughly 5%-7% of EBITDA.
- **Battery Energy Storage (BESS)** — A newer business, big enough to have its own CEO (Rajat Seksaria), that stores surplus daytime generation in batteries at Khavda and releases it in the evening — 3.5 GWh installed by June 2026 against a 10+ GWh target for the year — with returns management says are similar to or slightly better than solar and wind, and which also acts as a hedge against grid curtailment.
- **Pumped hydro storage** — A second, longer-duration storage technology still at an early stage, with the first 500 MW project at Chitravathi in Andhra Pradesh due to be commissioned in FY27.
- **Offtake mix: sovereign PPAs, C&I and merchant** — How the power is sold — long-term contracts with state distribution companies form the bulk and are the low-risk core, while capacity previously labelled 'merchant' (sold at volatile spot prices) has been re-contracted on an arm's-length basis to group company Adani Energy Solutions, about 4 GW, and now shows up as commercial and industrial (C&I) business; only plants running ahead of their PPA start date are still sold at market prices.
- **25% stake in Mundra Solar Energy (associate)** — A minority holding in a 2 GW solar module manufacturing unit set up under a manufacturing-linked PPA; it contributes only associate income — about INR 111 crores in Q1 FY26 — and is a small side item, not part of the core power business.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Adani Green Energy is, at its core, one thing: a very fast builder of renewable power plants. That single trait explains almost everything else in these records. Operational capacity went from 8.3GW in June 2023 to 15.8GW by June 2025 (up 45% year on year), 19.3GW by March 2026 and past 20GW by June 2026 — roughly 33% a year, faster than India's renewable base is growing — with 28GW of power purchase agreements already signed. The end market is genuinely expanding: India added a record 55GW of non-fossil capacity in FY26 against a 500GW target for 2030, from a base of only about 283GW, and new demand from AI and data centres is appearing on top. Both of the framework's positive checks — 'Gain in market share' and 'Structural end-market growth' — come from this, and it is the reason 'Market Share Gainers' is the one pattern rated a strong fit. There is also a real, if narrower, edge in how the company builds: the 30GW Khavda land bank with six years of its own wind data, secured grid connectivity, 5.2MW turbines developed for the site, bifacial panels and trackers, AI-based maintenance, and now batteries and pumped hydro. That supports a cost-and-scale story — 'low-cost green electrons', module purchases at around 15% below market at the peak, claimed returns 2-2.5% above competitors, and EBITDA margins of 91-94%.

The problem is that the same growth engine is what makes the business fragile, and the records say so in several places at once. Building fast means owning an enormous amount of capital equipment: ₹89,000 crore of gross block in Q1FY26 supports only about ₹13,000 crore of annual power-supply revenue, and every ₹1 of assets produces about ₹0.08 of sales. That heaviness shows up in the return: ₹7 of operating cash for every ₹100 of shareholders' funds and borrowings, a 7% cash return. Because the plants must be paid for before they earn, growth is funded by the company rather than by customers — FY27 capital spending guidance alone is ₹42,000 crore against an EBITDA run-rate of roughly ₹17,000 crore — which is exactly why the otherwise annuity-like 25-year contracts fail the 'Recurring Revenue' pattern. Scale also does not make life easier here: there is no network effect, and output gets harder to move as grid evacuation constrains it. Most importantly, the growth is volume without price. Energy sales rose 42% in Q1FY26 while revenue rose only 31%; new solar is being contracted at ₹2.60-2.80 against a blended book of ₹3.10, and merchant solar realisation has fallen to ₹2.20. So 'Pricing Power' and 'Brand Strength' are outright non-fits, 'Low Price Plus' and 'Cost to Replicate' only partial fits, and the industry-structure score is the weakest area on the board at -1.5.

The risk review lands on the same fault lines rather than new ones. Electricity here is an undifferentiated product sold into reverse auctions where the buyer picks the lowest tariff, entry barriers are low, bidder participation is heavy, and AGEL's own 5.1GW was under 10% of the FY26 national build — which is why 'New Entrants' and 'Good Enough Goods' are both flagged to watch and why the 'Oligopoly' and 'Barriers to entry' checks score badly, with margins swinging by ±11 points. Government dependency is the single highest risk: about 87% of offtake is sovereign, tariffs are benchmarked by the regulator, the demand story rests on the national 500GW target, and government-built transmission running late already cost roughly ₹500 crore of EBITDA. Concentration compounds it — about half the operating portfolio sits at one site, Khavda, behind one evacuation corridor, and by FY27 essentially all merchant, commercial and battery capacity is contracted to a related company, Adani Energy Solutions. That transfer is presented as de-risking and does reduce commodity exposure, but it also moves the company further from the end customer (hence the 'Forward Integrators' non-fit) and replaces market pricing with an internally-set, board-approved price. On the credit side, management largely delivered what it promised — the 50GW-by-2030 target, the 5GW annual run-rate, Khavda at 9.4GW, receivables kept current — and openly owned the FY26 evacuation error and its ₹1,300-1,500 crore EBITDA cost. Technology, consumer preference and fashion risks are all low, and resilience is judged sound. But the arithmetic is what it is: 38 for business quality, 33 for multibagger fit, 52 for risk safety, giving 40 out of 100 — Mixed, two stars. This is a company winning share in a growing market on terms that so far convert poorly into returns.
