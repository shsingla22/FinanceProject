# Adani Green Energy Ltd. (ADANIGREEN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

Adani Green Energy Limited (AGEL) builds and runs large-scale renewable power plants in India and sells the electricity they generate. It develops solar, wind and solar-wind "hybrid" projects, then sells the output mostly under long-term power purchase agreements — management says about 87% of the portfolio was with sovereign/government counterparties in 2023, with the rest going to commercial and industrial buyers (increasingly contracted to group company Adani Energy Solutions) or sold in the open "merchant" market when a plant is ready ahead of its PPA start date. Revenue is essentially a volume-times-tariff business: energy sales rose to 37.6 billion units in FY26, revenue from power supply to INR 11,602 crores, at an EBITDA margin management repeatedly calls industry-leading (91%–94%), because once the plants are built the running costs are small. It operates across resource-rich Indian states — Gujarat (notably the giant Khavda site in Kutch), Rajasthan and Andhra Pradesh — and has grown operating capacity from 8.3 GW in mid-2023 to over 20 GW by mid-2026, targeting 50 GW by 2030.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Solar power generation** — Utility-scale solar farms that are the largest single piece of the portfolio, running at a capacity utilisation factor of roughly 27%–28% (higher, around 32%, at the high-irradiation Khavda site) and with plant availability above 99%.
- **Wind power generation** — Wind farms that complement solar by generating at different times of day, with a much higher utilisation factor (about 38.7% in Q1 FY24) and, when sold merchant, fetching far better prices than solar (Rs 5.70/unit versus Rs 2.20/unit in Q1 FY26).
- **Hybrid (solar + wind) plants** — Combined solar-and-wind sites on shared land and grid connections that deliver the highest utilisation of the three (about 47% in Q1 FY24); 1,750 MW of hybrid was the bulk of the capacity added in that quarter.
- **Khavda project, Gujarat** — The flagship 30 GW single-location build-out described as the world's largest renewable energy plant — 5.6 GW operational by June 2025 and over 9.4–10 GW by 2026 — which management says drives both growth and better-than-average generation, though grid evacuation constraints there have curtailed roughly 5%–7% of EBITDA.
- **Battery energy storage (BESS)** — A fast-growing new line with its own CEO (Rajat Seksaria), storing daytime generation for evening supply; installed capacity went from 1.4 GWh in FY26 to 3.5 GWh by June 2026 with a 10+ GWh annual addition target, and management says returns are similar to or slightly better than solar and wind while acting as a hedge against grid curtailment.
- **Pumped hydro storage** — An early-stage storage business whose first project, a 500 MW plant at Chitravathi in Andhra Pradesh, was due to be commissioned in FY27.
- **Long-term PPA / sovereign (DISCOM) sales** — The core of the business — electricity sold to government-backed distribution companies under long-term contracts (about 87% of capacity in 2023), which management stresses has had no overdue receivables.
- **Commercial & industrial (C&I) and merchant sales** — The smaller, riskier slice sold outside sovereign PPAs; by 2026 most of what used to be merchant — including battery output — has been reclassified as C&I and contracted on an arm's-length basis to group company Adani Energy Solutions (about 4 GW), leaving only ahead-of-schedule plants selling into the spot market.
- **Stake in Mundra Solar Energy (25% associate)** — A minority holding in a 2 GW solar manufacturing operation set up under a manufacturing-linked PPA, which contributes only a small associate income line (INR 111 crores in Q1 FY26) and is not an operating segment of AGEL itself.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Adani Green is, first and foremost, a share gainer in a market that is itself growing fast. The one quality check that scores strongly positive is structural end-market growth: India sits at 283GW of non-fossil capacity heading to a 500GW target for 2030, added 55GW in FY26 alone, and now has storage and AI/data-centre demand on top. The company has taken more than its share of that: operational capacity went from 8.3GW in June 2023 to 15.8GW in June 2025 to over 20GW in June 2026, with 5.1GW added in FY26, a 33% five-year growth rate that management says beats national renewable growth, and 28GW of power purchase agreements lined up. That earns the strongest pattern verdict in the screen. Two supporting patterns rate as likely or partial fits and both trace back to the same engine: steady technology adoption (bifacial panels and trackers, 5.2MW turbines built specially for Khavda, an operations control centre, AI-based predictive maintenance, and now batteries and pumped hydro) feeding best-in-class generation rates and 92%+ availability, and a genuine low-cost-at-scale position with 92-94% operating margins and claimed returns 2 to 2.5 percentage points above competitors. Khavda itself is the one hard-to-copy asset in the story: a roughly 30GW single site with land, grid connectivity and six years of proprietary wind data, 10GW of it already running.

The trouble is that the very things driving those patterns also drive the risks, and they largely cancel out. Growth is bought with capital, not earned through the product. Every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.08 of sales, and on the calls the gap is starker still: ₹89,000 crore of gross block against roughly ₹13,000 crore of annual revenue, with FY27 capital spending guided at ₹42,000 crore. For every ₹100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings, the business generated ₹7 of operating cash. That is why the recurring-revenue pattern is a clear no-fit despite 25-year contracts — the revenue is long-dated but it is not a capital-light annuity. And because the product is an undifferentiated electron sold into reverse auctions, four patterns fail outright: no pricing power, no toll-road position, no brand strength, and no mini-monopoly or network effect, since each new gigawatt is a standalone asset whose scale brings grid curtailment rather than any self-reinforcing advantage. The pricing evidence is consistent and negative: FY26 energy sales rose 34% while revenue rose only 22%, the blended contract rate of ₹3.10 is being replaced by new solar contracting at ₹2.60-2.80, merchant solar realisation fell to ₹2.20, and curtailment plus weaker realisations cost ₹1,300-1,500 crore of operating profit in FY26. The 'good enough goods' risk and the falling-technology-cost risk are simply the risk-side statement of the same fact — buyers pick the lowest tariff, and each new vintage of technology resets tariffs lower, which quietly devalues older projects.

On top of that sits a concentrated dependency picture. Government dependency is the single high risk: roughly 87% of offtake is with sovereign counterparties, demand rests on national tenders and the 500GW target, and the expiry of the transmission-charge waiver forced a rush to commission that management admits cost near-term earnings. Counterparty concentration is rated elevated and has grown a new dimension — previously merchant solar, wind and battery capacity has been contracted to sister company Adani Energy Solutions at board-approved arm's-length terms. That is also why the forward-integration pattern is a no-fit: the company deliberately handed the customer-facing interface to the sister company to stay a pure builder-and-generator, moving away from the end customer rather than toward it. Earnings cyclicality is elevated for the same reasons — merchant prices, weather and curtailment swing results — though management has cut merchant exposure and capped annual building at 4.5-5GW against a 7-8GW capability. Two smaller risks, shifting preferences and fashion, are genuinely low: the energy transition runs in the company's favour and 37.6 billion units were sold in FY26 under long contracts. Balance-sheet resilience reads as sound, and management's conduct is a partial credit — early promises on the 45GW path, the Khavda build-out, the 5GW-a-year pace and the battery ramp were tracked and hit, and bad news such as the curtailment loss is owned openly on calls. But the company is the opposite of an overlooked situation: it is the world's largest renewable installation, among the most publicly scrutinised groups in India, with management leaning heavily on superlatives, rankings and awards. Netting it out — 37 out of 100 on business quality, 33 on pattern fit, 52 on risk safety — the combined rating is Mixed at 40 out of 100, or two stars: a real operator winning real share in a real growth market, with no durable pricing advantage to keep the winnings.

## What to watch

- Whether tariffs stop falling — the pricing pattern and the 'good enough goods' risk both turn on new solar contracting at ₹2.60-2.80 against the ₹3.10 blended rate; another step down widens the gap between volume growth and revenue growth already visible in FY26 (34% versus 22%).
