# Aether Inds (AETHER) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aether Industries makes specialty chemicals — complex, small-volume "advanced intermediates" that other companies use as ingredients — at its plants in Sachin GIDC, Surat, Gujarat, India (Sites 1 to 5, plus a large R&D centre and one of the world's biggest pilot plants). It sells to industrial customers rather than consumers: pharmaceutical and agrochemical companies (about 45-46% of sales in FY26), oil & gas / oilfield services (~22%), materials science (~17-18%), and more recently semiconductor and battery makers, with named clients including Baker Hughes, H.B. Fuller and Otsuka Chemical. It earns money three ways — selling its own catalogue of chemicals, manufacturing products exclusively under contract for a single customer, and doing paid research plus manufacturing for clients — and stresses that many of its products are made in India for the first time, replacing imports, at prices around $30-40 per kilo. Roughly 36-42% of revenue is exported (Europe, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, USA) and the rest sold in India; management is also spending heavily on new plants, R&D labs and captive solar power (16 MW and a further 15 MW) to cut electricity costs.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Large Scale Manufacturing** — Aether's own catalogue of chemical products made in bulk and sold to many customers — historically the biggest driver at about 66% of sales in Q1 FY25, easing to roughly 41-43% by FY26 as the other models grew, with pricing swinging on things like Chinese dumping (FY25) and later a conflict-driven supply shock that pushed prices up over 20% year-on-year in Q4 FY26.
- **Contract / Exclusive Manufacturing (CEM)** — Making specific products exclusively for one customer under a multi-year agreement — the fastest-growing leg, rising from about 18% of sales in Q1 FY25 to roughly 43% in Q3 FY26, anchored by a multi-year deal to make six products for Baker Hughes globally out of Site 4 (Site 4 sales grew from INR 50 crores to INR 220 crores, about 21% of total sales in FY26) plus a contract with Otsuka Chemical worth INR 35-40 crores in FY26.
- **Contract Research and Manufacturing Services (CRAMS)** — Paid R&D and process development for multinational innovator companies that can later turn into manufacturing orders — the smallest slice at around 8-14% of sales, but management says inquiries keep rising and expects CRAMS and CEM together to reach over 70% of sales in coming years; current focus is on non-pharma, non-agro work in oil & gas and materials science.
- **Sustainability and Renewables segment** — A newer segment launched in FY25 covering Converge polyols (a polymer ingredient containing up to 40% carbon dioxide by weight, developed with Saudi Aramco Technologies and first sold to H.B. Fuller), technology with Novoloop to turn post-consumer plastic waste back into virgin monomers, and organic electrolyte additives for lithium-ion batteries supplied to a major global battery producer — small today but described as high-margin with strong order visibility for FY27.
- **Electronic / semiconductor chemicals** — A recent extension out of Site 5 supplying chemicals to the semiconductor industry with customer bases in Japan, South Korea and Taiwan; validation batches have been dispatched and management says the opportunity could triple by 2030, so it is still at an early, pre-revenue-scale stage.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Aether's strength is that it makes chemistry other people find hard to make. The quality framework gives top marks to its technology advantage — high-pressure chemistry, extreme process conditions, continuous flow and now integrated polymerisation — backed by R&D spending held at about 7.5% of revenue, 50 live research projects and one of the world's larger pilot plants. That spending visibly turns into products and customers: Converge polyols with Saudi Aramco, a six-product agreement with Baker Hughes, work with Otsuka, and semiconductor chemicals. The multibagger screen agrees, calling Innovation Dominance a strong fit and pointing to fume hoods scaling from 18 to 55 with roughly 140-150 planned, a 200-reactor pilot plant and named new launches every year. Two other framework checks lean on the same engine: customers buy Aether for reliability and de-risked supply — it passed every health, safety and innovator audit after the Site 2 fire — and it is gaining share, with first-in-India molecules aimed at a stated import pool of about ₹1,200 crore, Site 4 revenue up four times to ₹220 crore, and 19 marquee clients added in FY26. Weak competitors are handing that share over: European chemical plants are closing and customers are urgently reshoring to India. Management scores well too — a promoter family running the same founding thesis for 13 years, rebuilding after the fire, and treating talent as a stated priority with ESOPs and grooming of the next generation of research leaders.

The awkward part is that several of these same traits are also where the risks live. Being the go-to partner for a handful of global innovators is exactly what earns the assurance-benefits score — and it is also why Stakeholder Concentration is the single high risk in the review, with results explained again and again by the same names: Baker Hughes (Site 4 alone at ₹220 crore, 21% of FY26 sales), Otsuka, Saudi Aramco Technologies, Milliken and H.B. Fuller, and analysts questioning the pricing and renewal of each relationship. Likewise, the import-substitution strategy that drives the share-gain check is what exposes the company on price: the screen rates Pricing Power a clear no fit, noting FY25 price deflation of 30-35% from Chinese dumping and a renegotiation of Otsuka pricing on a weaker yen, while the +20% price rise in Q4 FY26 came from an outside supply shock rather than from Aether's own strength. The risk review reaches the same place from three directions — Cyclicality is elevated, with margins swinging from about 29% in Q1 FY23 down to 11% in Q4 FY24 and back up; New Entrants is on watch because Chinese producers reset the price and Indian peers are all adding capacity; and Good Enough Goods is on watch because management itself says 'our competition is with China'. This is also why the screen only part-credits Toll Roads, Market Share Gainers and Cost to Replicate: the audit gates, exclusive multi-year contracts and 13 years of scale-up know-how are real barriers, but they have not translated into control over price. The offsets are genuine — contracted CEM work was 55% of Q4 FY26 sales, and the risks tied to technology change, government dependency, shifting end-preferences and fashion are all rated low, because Aether is doing the innovating rather than defending an old format.

Where the story clearly weakens is cash and capital. The framework's two zero-scored areas are Return on Capital and Capital Allocation, and the working-capital check is the one strongly negative mark in the whole record: roughly 330 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, with debtors around 140 days. The screen picks up the same fact from the other side — Recurring Revenue is a no fit precisely because revenue is per-batch product sales with no licence, subscription or maintenance element, and the business funds the customer rather than the other way round. Low Price Plus is also a no fit, with capacity use sitting at 49-76% and heavy assets turning slowly, so the high-value positioning (molecules at $30-40 a kilo, 28-34% margins) has to carry a lot of idle plant. Set against that, the operating margin check is strong — ₹31 of every ₹100 of sales is operating profit, and steady — and the culture check credits candid disclosure of the fire and its six-month revenue loss plus conservative, debt-free funding of capex and research. Putting it together, the combined rating is 55 out of 100 — 'Decent', three stars — from 65 on business quality, 34 on multibagger fit and 62 on risk safety, with resilience judged sound. In plain terms: an unusually good chemistry and customer-qualification business attached to a price-taking, cash-hungry manufacturing model, and the second half is what keeps the score in the middle rather than the top.
