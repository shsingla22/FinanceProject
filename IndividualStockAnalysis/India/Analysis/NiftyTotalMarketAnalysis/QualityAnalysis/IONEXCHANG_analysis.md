# Ion Exchange (IONEXCHANG) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Ion Exchange (India) Limited is a water treatment company: it designs and builds water and wastewater treatment plants, and separately manufactures and sells the chemicals, resins and membranes those plants run on. Its customers are mainly industrial and government buyers — refineries (it commissioned the raw water plant for IOCL's Panipat refinery, described as the largest industrial water package awarded in India), solar and semiconductor makers needing ultra-pure water, textile parks, data centres, and state water bodies such as UP Jal Nigam under the Jal Jeevan Mission — plus a smaller retail arm selling consumer water products. It makes money three ways: lumpy project/EPC contracts booked against an order book (INR 26,433 million at March 2026), repeat sales of consumable chemicals and resins, and consumer product sales; a 20-year build-and-operate contract in Oman also brings recurring revenue. Revenue for FY26 was INR 29,148 million, roughly 60-65% from engineering projects and about a quarter from chemicals, with operations across India and a growing overseas presence in the Middle East, Sri Lanka, Africa (a Malawi JV) and Europe (Portugal/Spain via the MAPRIL acquisition).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Engineering division** — Designs, builds and commissions water and wastewater treatment systems — ultra-pure water plants, effluent treatment plants and zero-liquid-discharge solutions — for industry and government; it is the largest segment by revenue (INR 5,539 million of the INR 8,633 million quarterly total in Q4 FY26) but runs on thin single-digit margins, and its visibility comes from an order book of INR 26,433 million.
- **Chemical division** — Manufactures and sells ion exchange resins, water treatment specialty chemicals and membranes (reverse osmosis, ultra-filtration, nano-filtration), including through a new technology tie-up with Germany's MANN+HUMMEL; it is roughly a quarter of revenue (INR 2,297 million in Q4 FY26) but is the profit engine, typically earning more EBIT than the much larger engineering arm, with a new plant at Roha recently fully commissioned and certified by the Water Quality Association.
- **Consumer Product division** — Sells branded water-related products to household/retail customers; it is the smallest part at INR 1,047 million in Q4 FY26 and grows fast (34% year-on-year) but has consistently lost money (a loss of INR 46 million that quarter) as the company keeps spending to build scale.
- **International operations and group companies** — Overseas subsidiaries, joint ventures and acquisitions that carry the same water business abroad — a 20-year design-build-own-operate-transfer contract worth OMR 73.46 million with Petroleum Development Oman, a USD 18.1 million water treatment JV in Malawi, project work in Sri Lanka and the GCC, and MAPRIL in Portugal giving a local base in Spain and Portugal; management describes this as a focused push to grow the product business in key geographies.

### The story in depth

Ion Exchange is a sixty-year-old water treatment company whose best qualities are real but sit on top of weak earnings. The framework scores its technology and its end markets highest: a genuinely broad technical stack spanning resins, RO/UF/NF membranes, ultra-pure water, zero liquid discharge and now membrane bioreactors via MANN+HUMMEL, aimed at markets in clear secular expansion — water and wastewater policy, semiconductors with a Rs.40,000 crore outlay, data centres, green hydrogen and textile parks, alongside a global membrane market growing at more than 10% a year. Sales have compounded at roughly 12% a year with unusually steady year-to-year growth. Yet for every Rs.100 of shareholders' funds and borrowings in the business, the company generated minus Rs.2 of operating cash this past year, and the overall verdict lands at Mixed — 44 out of 100, or two stars — with financial resilience judged strained.

The single trait that explains both the promise and the problem is that growth is driven by winning orders and volumes rather than by price or product mix. That same trait powers the one pattern that comes closest to fitting — Market Share Gainers, with order intake 40% above the prior full year, the integrated MAPRIL acquisition in Portugal, joint ventures in Oman and Malawi, a Gulf push and WQA certification opening US drinking-water resin markets — while simultaneously producing the two outright pattern failures. Pricing Power is a clear no fit: through FY26 the company absorbed input-cost and currency inflation and was still working to pass it on afterwards, with gross margin down about 260 basis points in the third quarter and the chemicals division's operating margin falling from roughly 25% to about 15%. Low Price Plus is also a no fit, because the company walks away from price-competitive work rather than winning on cost, and returns are falling — FY26 operating profit down 29% at a 7.2% margin. This is the same pressure the risk review flags as Good Enough Goods at elevated risk: resins are a globally traded product, and the company declined awarded semiconductor packages on price. Eight of the eleven patterns come back only partial, and the reason is consistent — the chemicals and membrane side has articulable barriers (product approvals, a Rs.450 crore resin plant needing about four years to fill, four decades of semiconductor water credentials), while the project engineering half, which is the majority of revenue, does not.

The risk picture follows from the same business shape rather than sitting apart from it. With roughly two-thirds of revenue in engineering work tied to customers' own capacity expansion, cyclicality is elevated and segment margins have swung hard across the sixteen calls. Government dependency is the highest-rated risk: the largest engineering contract, UP Jal Nigam under the Jal Jeevan Mission, has dragged results for years — first elections, then persistent delays in government funding release — and the Sri Lanka order stalled on that country's political crisis and financing, now expected to close only in FY27. The company's answer to this risk is precisely the international build-out and the export-oriented Roha plant that the framework credits under geographical expansion and capital allocation, so the escape route and the exposure are two sides of one decision. Execution is the recurring weak link: Roha slipped years from its original FY23-24 plan, and the acquisition brought an Iberian foothold but no evidence of margin improvement. That gap also caps the Corporate Culture pattern at partial — disclosure is candid, with the legacy project drag, the Roha cost hit and the funding failure named quarter after quarter, but the record of delivering against those promises is weak. Meanwhile new entrants are crowding the field, and when an analyst named five of them in August 2024 management answered on market size rather than on how it would defend its position.

Taken together, the report reads as a technically strong company in genuinely growing markets whose profits are being squeezed at exactly the moment its biggest investments need to start paying back. The upside case does not require new capabilities — it requires the existing ones to convert: the Roha plant filling up at the guided better-than-Ankleshwar margins and more than twice the asset turnover, the growing order book translating into pricing that leads cost inflation instead of trailing it, and cash returning to the business rather than leaving it. The downside case is that the engineering half keeps setting the pace — government-funded orders that pay late, competitive bidding that erodes margin, and a capital base that grows faster than the cash it produces.
