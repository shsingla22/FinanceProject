# Waaree Energies (WAAREEENER) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Waaree Energies is an Indian company that manufactures solar panels (called modules) and the solar cells that go inside them, selling them to large power producers and utilities, commercial and industrial buyers, overseas customers, and ordinary households through a retail network of franchisee stores. It makes money mainly by producing and selling modules in volume — roughly 12 gigawatts sold in FY26 on revenue of about INR 26,537 crore — and supplements this by building solar projects for customers (engineering, procurement and construction, plus ongoing maintenance) through its listed subsidiary. Its two biggest markets are India and the United States: modules are assembled in Indian plants such as Chikhli in Gujarat and Samakhiali in Kutch and exported, and the company also manufactures locally in the US (Texas and Arizona) to serve American customers, with roughly half of revenue domestic and about a third overseas. Management is now spending heavily (about $3.5 billion of committed capex) to make more of the supply chain in-house — polysilicon, ingots and wafers, cells, glass — and to add batteries, inverters, transformers, smart meters and green hydrogen equipment, positioning itself as a one-stop 'energy transition' supplier.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Solar modules (panels)** — The core business — manufacturing and selling solar panels including standard, building-integrated, flexible and advanced TOPCon/HJT types, with capacity grown to about 26 gigawatts (management calls itself the largest non-Chinese module maker in the world) and record production of 12.6 gigawatts in FY26.
- **Solar cells** — The company makes the cells that go inside its own panels at a 5.4 gigawatt plant described as India's largest, producing around 0.6-0.7 gigawatts a quarter, with plans to scale to 15.4 gigawatts by 2027.
- **Ingots and wafers** — A further step backwards up the supply chain — a 10 gigawatt ingot-and-wafer plant under construction at Nagpur costing about INR 6,200 crore, so the company can make the raw material its cells need instead of importing it.
- **Polysilicon sourcing (Oman)** — A stake in United Polysilicon, an Oman-based company, to lock in fully traceable non-Chinese polysilicon — important because US import duties depend on where the cells and their inputs come from.
- **US manufacturing** — Module plants in the US — 2.6 gigawatts today (including assets bought from Meyer Burger in Arizona), heading to 4.2 gigawatts — so American orders can be supplied locally rather than exported from India and hit by tariffs.
- **Retail / B2C** — Selling panels to households and small buyers through 370-plus franchisee and channel-partner stores, helped by the government's PM Surya Ghar rooftop scheme; revenue of INR 5,515 crore in FY26, about 20.8% of the total and growing 84% year-on-year.
- **EPC and O&M (Waaree Renewable Technologies)** — A listed subsidiary that designs and builds solar projects — rooftop, floating and utility-scale — and maintains them afterwards; about 11.6% of FY26 revenue.
- **Battery energy storage systems** — A new segment being scaled from 3.5 to 20 gigawatt-hours (cells, packs and containers) with roughly INR 8,000 crore of investment, aimed at India's plan for 236 gigawatt-hours of storage by 2032.
- **Inverters** — Manufacturing capacity being expanded from 3 to 4 gigawatts a year — the equipment that converts solar output into usable grid power.
- **Green hydrogen electrolysers** — A 1 gigawatt electrolyser manufacturing plant expected to be operational in 2027, an early bet on hydrogen supported by central and state policy.
- **Transformers, smart meters and T&D** — Bolt-on acquisitions into grid equipment — 64% of Kotsons (transformers, heading to 20,000 MVA), 76% of Racemosa Energy India (smart meters), and about 55% of Associated Power Structures for INR 1,225 crore, entering transmission and distribution.
- **Solar glass** — A board-approved INR 3,900 crore plant for 2,500 tonnes per day of PV glass — management notes glass is about 23% of a module's cost and 75% of its weight, so making it in-house cuts costs.
- **Power generation / IPP** — A smaller effort to own and sell renewable power directly, with power purchase agreements signed for 413 megawatts and grid connectivity secured for about 6.1 gigawatts.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Waaree Energies is, on this evidence, a genuinely strong operating business sitting in a genuinely strong end market. The quality framework gives its top marks to what customers actually buy: assurance — 25-year reliability backed by 50+ certifications and years of field validation that management treats as a barrier rivals cannot copy — and convenience, delivered through a franchise network of 370-plus stores that brought in INR5,515 crores in FY26, about 21% of sales and up 84% in a year. That same network scores full marks as a distribution advantage and is the one asset management itself calls unreplicable. Behind it sits heavy spending for growth rather than upkeep: modules from 12 to about 26 GW, cells from 5.4 to 15.4 GW, plus ingot-wafer, batteries and glass, roughly $3.5 billion committed, delivered on schedule while returns on capital stayed at 32-42%. The end market is expanding for real — India solar 127 to 280 GW by 2030, global solar 2.5 to 8 TW by 2035 — and the multibagger screen picks up the two clearest signatures of all this, rating Waaree a strong fit as a Forward Integrator (its own retail counters, its own US plants) and as a Market Share Gainer (module output up 77% to 12.6 GW, order book from INR40,000 crores to INR53,000 crores, share won while returns rose rather than fell).

The interesting part is how often one fact does double duty — the same trait that earns a pattern also creates a risk. The retail counter network is the Forward Integrator case, and it is also the company's stated defence against two of the flagged risks: it is the answer management gives when analysts ask whether modules are a commodity (Good Enough Goods, watch) and when they ask what stops new domestic capacity from crushing prices (New Entrants, watch). The screen is more cautious than management on both counts — Friendly Middleman is only a partial fit because installers, not end customers, do the specifying and there is no trade-training programme behind it, and Pricing Power is only partial because the premium that is claimed (1 to 1.5 cents a panel at retail, 300-350 basis points more on exports and domestic-content sales) shows up as better sales mix and cost control rather than as an ability to simply set prices. Technology works the same way. The pace of change — Mono PERC to TOPCon to HJT, with all equipment written off over three years to absorb it — is why Innovation Dominance is a partial fit, and that identical three-year re-platforming cycle is exactly what makes Technological Innovation an elevated risk: management's own depreciation policy is an admission that today's plant becomes yesterday's plant quickly. Likewise, the certifications and the four-to-five-year US bankability approval cycle are real barriers, but the screen notes the factories themselves are not, which is why Cost to Replicate stops at partial. The one pattern that clearly does not apply is Toll Roads: the module is the biggest single cost in a solar project, not a small unavoidable one, and management's own charts show 80% price deflation over time.

Against that, the weaknesses cluster in a recognisable place. Growth, industry structure, management and return on capital all score neutral in the quality framework, and capital allocation barely above it. Sales have compounded at roughly 54% a year but arrive lumpily, which costs points for persistence; about 90 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers; and the company is the opposite of overlooked, having listed in a high-profile 2024 IPO into the most capital-hungry sector in India with Nomura, UBS and others already covering it. The two high risks tie straight back to the strengths. Demand comes from other people's capital spending — utility and project customers are 34.7% and 11.6% of FY26 revenue — and is talked about in commodity terms of cents per watt, so the same $3.5 billion expansion that earns full marks on the quality side is being committed into a cyclical order book; retail and service revenue, being less tied to project spending, is the partial offset. And across all eight calls the demand case leans on government policy at both ends: domestic content rules coming for cells in June 2026 and ingots and wafers in 2028, customs duty, production incentives, rooftop and farm schemes, the GST cut from 12% to 5%, and in the US the 45X credits and foreign-entity rules. Management says the model is agnostic to tariffs; the record does not yet show that tested. Supplier concentration is a watch item too, since the US business depends on non-Chinese cell supply from a handful of sources — which is precisely why the backward integration into cells, wafers, glass and Omani polysilicon exists. The combined rating lands at 52 out of 100 — a Decent, three-star verdict, with resilience judged sound: a high-quality operator whose scores are held down not by execution but by cyclicality, policy dependence and the fact that everyone can already see it.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Technological Innovation, Fashion Risk. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.2× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 69% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 30% to 39% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Demand is customer-capex-driven (utility/IPP solar farms, EPC projects = 34.7%+11.6% of FY26 revenue) and pricing is discussed in commodity terms (cents per watt peak, 80% price declines, GST-driven price cuts), while the company is spending ~$3.5bn of capex at a cycle peak where EBITDA margin jumped from 17% to 25%+ and revenue grew 84% — classic expansionary over-investment with no way to know how long the peak lasts.

> "the amount of decline in the prices that have happened on the solar as well as battery side in cents per watt peak... in the last eight years, we have seen 80% decline" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Retail/B2C counter sales (~21% of FY26 revenue, growing 84%) and O&M are less capex-linked; back-to-back sourcing to protect gross margin*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±48 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 4% and 22% (±7.4 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 13% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 23%).

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Across all eight calls the demand case rests on policy: ALMM for modules then cells (June 2026) and ingots/wafers (2028), basic customs duty, PLI, DCR premiums, PM Surya Ghar and PM-KUSUM, GST cut 12%-to-5%, and in the US the 45X credits, FEOC rules and a preliminary 126% countervailing duty that forced a dedicated investor call in Feb 2026.

> "the regulatory environment continues to be conducive with initiatives like extension of ALMM for cells since June 2026 and for ingots and wafers from June 2028" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Management claims the business model is 'agnostic to tariffs'; diversification into inverters, transformers, BESS, T&D and exports spreads policy exposure, though most adjacencies are themselves scheme-linked (electrolyser PLI INR444cr, hydrogen PLI INR510cr, RDSS)*

## What to watch

- Retail counter revenue growth and the 1-1.5 cent per panel premium — the Forward Integrator pattern, the Pricing Power case and the defence against Good Enough Goods all rest on this one channel holding up.
- Policy milestones landing as assumed — domestic content rules for cells in June 2026 and ingots/wafers in 2028, plus US 45X credits and foreign-entity rules — since Government Dependency is rated high risk and management's 'agnostic to tariffs' claim is untested.
- Whether the roughly $3.5bn capex programme keeps earning 32-42% returns as new domestic capacity arrives — this is where the Capex for growth check meets the Cyclicality and New Entrants risks head-on.
