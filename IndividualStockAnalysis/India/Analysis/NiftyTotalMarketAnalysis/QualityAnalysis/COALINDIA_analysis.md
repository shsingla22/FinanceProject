# Coal India Ltd. (COALINDIA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: METALS*

Coal India Limited (CIL) is a government-owned mining company — a "Maharatna" Government of India enterprise, registered in Kolkata and listed on the BSE and NSE — that digs thermal (power-grade) coal out of the ground in India and sells it domestically. Most of its coal goes to electricity generators under tied-up long-term supply contracts at government-notified prices (in 9M FY24 it dispatched 454 million tonnes to the power sector out of 531.9 million tonnes produced), with the rest going to non-power industrial buyers such as sponge iron makers and to spot buyers through e-auctions. Management says roughly 90% of volume is sold under tied-up contracts where there is "hardly any profit," while the 10-15% sold by e-auction — at a premium over notified prices that has ranged from ~35-50% in normal years to 250-300% in tight years — is where most of the profit comes from. It mines through seven regional subsidiary companies (South Eastern/SECL, Central/CCL, Mahanadi/MCL, Northern/NCL, Western/WCL, Eastern/ECL and others), and its economics hinge on pushing volumes up — 700 million tonnes in FY23, ~770-780 in FY24, guidance of 875 in FY26 and 900-plus in FY27, against a long-standing 1-billion-tonne government target — because higher output spreads fixed costs and cuts cost per tonne.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Tied-up / linkage supplies to the power sector (FSA/SSC coal)** — Coal sold to electricity utilities under long-term supply agreements at notified prices; it is by far the biggest part — about 90% of volume and 454 million tonnes of the 531.9 million tonnes produced in the first nine months of FY24 — but management says it carries hardly any profit.
- **Long-term linkages to the non-power sector** — Long-term coal commitments to industrial buyers like sponge iron plants and other non-power consumers, grown about 28% in two years from 90 to 115 million tonnes, with management still scouting for another 30-40 million tonnes of such commitments.
- **E-auction / spot sales** — Roughly 10-15% of volume auctioned on the open market at a premium over notified non-power prices (43% in Q4 FY25, over 100% in Q3 FY24, up to 250-300% in the tight 2022-23 years) — a small slice of tonnage that management describes as the source of essentially all its profit.
- **Producing subsidiaries** — Seven regional mining companies (SECL, CCL, MCL/Mahanadi, NCL, WCL, ECL and others) that actually run the mines; performance varies by subsidiary — SECL and CCL missed targets on rainfall, land acquisition and environment/forest clearances, while coal grade also differs, with ECL producing good G6-G8 quality and Mahanadi coal running as high as ~42% ash.
- **Coal evacuation and first mile connectivity (FMC) infrastructure** — Silos, rail sidings and railway lines (e.g. Tori-Shivpuri, Jharsuguda-Sardega doubling) built to get coal from pithead to customer; rake loading through FMC grew ~32% last year to 87.1 rakes per day in early FY26, with a target of at least 100 per day.
- **Mine development and capex programme** — Buying land, obtaining environmental and forest clearances, and awarding Mine Developer-Operator (MDO) contracts — about 100 million tonnes of capacity awarded as of FY23 — funded by capex of about ₹16,500 crore in FY24, all aimed at lifting output toward 1 billion tonnes.
- **Overburden removal** — The waste rock stripped away to reach the coal, tracked as a headline operating measure — 1,404.85 million cubic metres in 9M FY24, up 22% year on year.
- **Diversification (early stage)** — Small ventures outside core mining mentioned only in passing: CIL won about 300 MW of solar projects from Gujarat Industrial Power Corporation at Khavda via e-auction in January 2024, and analysts asked about rare earth and lithium mining interests in India and Australia.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

## The verdict: Mixed — 35 out of 100 ★★☆☆☆

In one breath: the quality framework finds an average business today (50/100); no winning pattern is strongly confirmed yet; the risk review found 1 high risk (Government Dependency). Weighing those together gives 35 out of 100 — mixed.

**The exact arithmetic:** Overall = 45% × 50 (business quality) + 30% × 10 (multibagger fit) + 25% × 38 (risk safety) = 35 out of 100 → Mixed (2 stars).

- **Business quality (50/100):** The 34-check quality framework scored the business -0.02 on its −2 (poor) to +2 (excellent) scale, with 91% of checks backed by evidence; mapped onto 0–100 that is 50 points.
- **Multibagger fit (10/100):** the foundation test partly passed (+10); no pattern found meaningful support (+0) → 10 of 100.
- **Risk safety (38/100):** started from a clean 100; 1 high risk (Government Dependency) (−20); 3 elevated (−30); 3 worth watching (−12) → 38 of 100.

### The story in depth

Coal India earns a genuinely high cash return on the money invested in it: for every ₹100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings, the business threw off ₹32 of operating cash this year, the single strongest item in the whole quality review (Return on Capital scores +0.67 as an area). Capital allocation is orderly rather than clever — capex is rising and genuinely aimed at growth (₹15,500cr in FY22 to ₹17,500cr in FY25, on top of an ₹80,000cr programme for silos, rail lines and land), and a published dividend policy is actually paid (₹20.50 per share interim in FY24) and openly balanced against that capex. But the incremental returns on this new spending are thinner than the headline, distributions are dividends only with no valuation-sensitive buyback discipline, and everything below the cash-return line is weak: Competitive Advantage scores -1.0 and Growth -0.67. There is no network effect (extra scale brings evacuation congestion and stockpiles, not compounding advantage), no technology edge at all — management itself concedes it cannot lift coking-coal usability, is short of washery capacity and still imports blending coal — and no obscurity, since a ₹2.3 lakh crore Maharatna sits under permanent policy scrutiny. So the profitability is real, and the reasons to expect it to persist are not.

The multibagger screen tells the same story from the other side: five of eleven patterns come out PARTIAL and six are outright NO FIT, and almost every partial is partial for the same reason — the good thing is granted by the government rather than earned by the company. Coal India does sell far below import parity at enormous scale, and operating leverage genuinely cuts cost per tonne as headcount falls roughly 5% a year through retirements, but the low price is an administered notified price, not the output of a costing culture, which is why Low Price Plus stops at PARTIAL. Cost to Replicate is partial for the identical reason: the best legacy blocks, the exemption from the royalty premium that auctioned mines pay, and decades of rail and first-mile-connectivity infrastructure are truly un-replicable, but the barrier is legislated — and the same government that wrote it is dismantling it. Forward Integrators is partial because the downstream push is real but early, capital-hungry and slipping (fertiliser plants, gasification tie-ups with BHEL and GAIL, an aluminium ambition, 3,000 MW of solar, with one fertiliser project delayed about 18 months) — the same unrelated-diversification deals that the quality framework marks down as neither simple bolt-ons nor shown to earn their cost of capital. Corporate Culture is partial on the strength of unusually candid disclosure — naming the laggard subsidiaries, admitting a railway line slipped from September 2023 to 2026, voluntarily aligning overburden-removal accounting to international standards — while funding coal capex from internal accruals. The six NO FITs cluster around one fact: this is an undifferentiated commodity with a shrinking price lever. E-auction premiums fell from 250–300% in FY23 to 116% in Q3 FY24 to 43% by Q4 FY25, with management guiding 30–40% as the new normal, so Pricing Power fails outright; buyers pay for grade, ash and landed cost and will not pay extra even for demonstrably better washed coal, so Brand Strength fails; no R&D spend or product pipeline is disclosed across four calls and the washery promises made at IPO still had not delivered volumes by FY24-25, so Innovation Dominance fails; and share is being structurally lost rather than gained.

The risk review is largely a restatement of those same traits with the sign flipped. Government Dependency is the one HIGH RISK, and it is precisely the trait that underwrites Cost to Replicate and Low Price Plus — the state sets notified prices for both power and non-power, sets production targets (850 mt cut to 838 mt), and has rewritten the allocation policy repeatedly across 2007, 2011-12, 2017, 2019 and again in 2025. The collapse in e-auction premiums that kills the Pricing Power pattern is the same evidence behind ELEVATED Cyclicality, since management links those premiums directly to imported coal prices and to seasonality. New Entrants is ELEVATED for the reason Market Share Gainers is a NO FIT and the market-share check scores -1: captive and commercial miners, which pay no compensation cess on self-consumed coal, have reached 198 mt and are projected at 320 mt by FY30, and they were auctioned explicitly to take share. Stakeholder Concentration is ELEVATED on an 80–82% skew to the power sector, mostly state generators and distributors, with receivables jumping from about ₹11,000 crore to ₹14,000 crore in a single quarter — the same fact that sinks Recurring Revenue, because rather than customers pre-funding the business, Coal India is financing its customers while admitting it must keep supplying stressed boards to avoid power cuts. The three WATCH items — technological disruption, shifting attitudes to coal, and good-enough substitutes for a high-ash (up to 42%) product where buyers are becoming choosy — line up with the -2 technology check. Real offsets exist and are counted: roughly 90% of volume moves on regulated linkage contracts at notified prices, which is why resilience reads SOUND; non-power long-term linkages are up about 29% from 90 mt to over 115 mt in under two years toward a 75:25 power/non-power mix, which pushes back on both the concentration and the share-loss problems at once; import substitution of 60–100 mt (imports already down about 10%) and new thermal capacity absorb some incremental supply; and the diversification is funded from internal accruals rather than leverage. Netted out, the combined rating is 35 out of 100 — 45% weight on business quality at 50, 30% on multibagger fit at just 10, and 25% on risk safety at 38 — a Mixed, two-star verdict: a high-cash-return, financially sound monopoly whose protections and prices are set by the same authority that is now opening the market to others.

### Every check, area by area

**Capital Allocation — Neutral (+0.25)** · 4 of 6 checks had evidence

  > "last year we did something like Rs.15,500 crores, this year we'll reach Rs.16,500 crores... we are investing in a big way in our FMC projects" — *management, on an earnings call*
- **Distribution to shareholders — Good** *(from the conference calls)*: A published dividend policy consistently executed (₹20.50/share interim in FY24) and explicitly balanced against the capex program, but distribution is dividend-only with no valuation-sensitive buyback discipline.
  > "Dividend policy is already hosted on the website... we are balancing the shareholder expectation as well as the complete capex program." — *management, on an earnings call*
- **Cost of working capital — Neutral** *(from the financial statements)*: About 31 days of cash stays stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers — a long cycle that ties up capital.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Cyclicality, Stakeholder Concentration, New Entrants. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.1× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 66% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital moderated from about 52% to 49% — lower, but still well clear of the deep slide this check looks for.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Effectively every lever of the business is set by the state: notified prices for power and non-power, ministry-set production targets (850 mt cut to 838 mt), the Shakti allocation policy rewritten repeatedly (2007, 2011-12, 2017, 2019 and again in 2025), coal compensation cess extended to 2026, EC/FC clearances gating output, a Supreme-Court-enabled Rs.150 royalty hike at CCL, and an explicit analyst question on windfall-profit intervention on e-auction pricing. Coal India is a majority government-owned Maharatna selling a fixed-infrastructure extractive product mostly to state gencos.

> "850 million, now ministry has 838 million because of huge stocks." — *management, on an earnings call*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but none of the 3 computed fingerprints confirms it in the numbers yet — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Coal India is a pure commodity producer whose realisations swing violently with the coal cycle: e-auction premiums went from 250-300% in FY22-23 to ~117% in Q3FY24 to 43% by Q4FY25, and management explicitly links premiums to imported coal prices and to seasonal/cyclical demand. Volume and profit growth in FY23-24 came off an abnormal energy-shortage peak that management itself later described as exceptional.

> "if we take out those two years where there was an abnormal increase to 250%, 300% premiums were obtained in the market due to whatever coal availability scenarios" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: ~90% of volume moves on regulated FSA/linkage contracts at notified prices tied to power-plant operations (a flow/consumables-like base), which dampens the swing versus the 10-15% spot book*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 2 of the last 11 years with swings of ±11 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 11% and 33% (±6.6 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 10% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 12%).

## What to watch

- E-auction premium: management guides 30-40% as the new normal after the fall from 250-300% to 43%; a further slide or a drop below the 63% FY25 booking level would confirm the Pricing Power no-fit and worsen the ELEVATED Cyclicality risk.
- Captive and commercial mine output against the 320 mt by FY30 projection — running ahead of that would deepen the ELEVATED New Entrants risk and the -1 market-share check.
- Power-sector receivables, which jumped from about ₹11,000 crore to ₹14,000 crore in one quarter; continued build would sharpen the ELEVATED Stakeholder Concentration risk and confirm that the company is funding its customers.
- Progress on the non-power linkage push from 90 mt toward the 75:25 mix — the single offset that works against both the concentration and share-loss problems; stalling would remove it.
- Delivery on the ₹80,000cr capex programme and the diversification JVs (the ~18-month fertiliser delay, gasification, solar, alumina) — further slippage would push Forward Integrators from PARTIAL toward no fit and weaken the Capital Allocation and M&A checks.
- Any change to notified prices, allocation policy or production targets, or renewed windfall-tax debate — the HIGH RISK Government Dependency channel that also props up the Cost to Replicate and Low Price Plus patterns.
