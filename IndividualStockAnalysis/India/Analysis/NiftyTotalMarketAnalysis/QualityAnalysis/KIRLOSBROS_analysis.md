# Kirloskar Brothers (KIRLOSBROS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Kirloskar Brothers Limited (KBL) is an old, family-run Indian engineering company that designs, manufactures and sells pumps and pumping systems — from small retail pumps used by farmers and households to large engineered pumps for industrial projects. It sells to a wide mix of end users: agriculture and irrigation, water and waste-water supply schemes, building and construction, industry, oil and gas, power stations (coal and nuclear), and marine and defence. It makes money by selling equipment in two ways — large 'made-to-order' and 'engineered-to-order' pumps that sit in an order book and are billed as they are built and dispatched, and standard 'made-to-stock' small pumps sold through a long-standing dealer and distributor network on cash terms (no order book) — plus a growing spares, maintenance and subscription-style service business that brings repeat revenue. Roughly 60-65% of sales come from India (including its own foundry at Kirloskarvadi) and the rest from overseas units in the UK, USA, Netherlands, South Africa and Thailand; FY26 consolidated revenue was about Rs.4,538 crores with EBITDA margin around 13.7% and PAT of about Rs.377 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Standalone / domestic pumps business (India)** — The core Indian operation that designs and builds pumps across small, medium and large ranges at plants including the Kirloskarvadi foundry; it is the biggest single piece of the group, contributing roughly two-thirds of consolidated revenue (about Rs.2,828 crores in FY26) with a domestic order book of about Rs.2,468 crores, up 30% year-on-year.
- **Small pumps / retail business** — Standard off-the-shelf pumps made to stock and sold through generations-old dealers to farmers and households on cash terms; management says it carries no order book, and its dispatches were hurt in FY26 by weak monsoon-linked demand and by Jal Jeevan Mission funding not reaching dealers (JJM itself is only about 4-5% of revenue but matters a lot to the water segment).
- **Made-to-order / engineered-to-order project pumps** — Large, customised pumps for irrigation, water supply, industry, oil and gas, power and marine and defence, booked only against confirmed orders with advances or letters of credit; this is where the visible order book and much of the recent growth sits, with management calling out building and construction, marine and defence, oil and gas, power and water/irrigation as the strongest areas.
- **International business (Kirloskar Brothers International B.V. and subsidiaries)** — Overseas manufacturing and selling units — SPP Pumps UK, SPP USA, the Dutch entity, South Africa and Kirloskar Brothers Thailand — serving oil and gas, water management and industry; it accounts for roughly a third of revenue, grew 7% in FY26, and its order book was about Rs.1,481 crores, up 21% year-on-year.
- **Spares, maintenance and subscription/service platform** — Aftermarket parts, servicing and a subscription platform sold mainly through the international business, valued by management because it brings recurring revenue, upfront cash and closer customer contact; described as a business the company wants to scale up rather than a major revenue line today.
- **Karad Projects and Motors Limited (KPML)** — A domestic subsidiary making motors and doing project work that management described as growing healthily (revenue up 27% in H1 FY23); it also absorbed the group's Kolhapur Steel business in 2024 to simplify the structure.
- **Kolhapur Steel Limited** — A small steel/castings subsidiary that was being turned around with rising revenue and production, and whose entire stake KBL sold to Karad Projects and Motors in FY25 to tidy up the group structure.
- **Kirloskar Ebara Pumps Limited (KEPL)** — An associate company (not fully consolidated) run as a joint venture-style pump business under Rama Kirloskar; it shows up in KBL's accounts as share of associate profit and is lumpy — one delayed Rs.100 crore order pushed it to a loss of about Rs.5 crores in Q1 FY25.

### The story in depth

Kirloskar Brothers comes out of these three reviews as a solid, well-run pump maker rather than a rare compounder — a combined score of 54 out of 100, or "Decent," built from 69 on business quality, 33 on multibagger fit and 52 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged SOUND. The strongest parts of the business are the ones customers actually pay for. The quality framework gives top marks to distribution and service reach, calling it the standout asset: a multi-generational dealer network in India, 110-plus managed sites abroad and exclusive utility service frameworks in a business where service is critical. It also gives top marks to what the framework calls assurance benefits — customers buying certainty in the form of lowest life-cycle cost, efficiency guarantees and nuclear and defence qualification, and paying a premium for it even against multinational rivals. After direct input costs the company keeps Rs.51 of every Rs.100 of sales. The end markets it serves — nuclear power against a 100GW national target, data centres, defence, water infrastructure and oil and gas — are all described as in long-term expansion. Where the quality scoring is weakest is on the financial side: return on capital scores zero and capital allocation 0.4, the two lowest of the seven areas, so the operating strengths are not yet showing up as strong returns on the money invested.

The pattern screen tells a consistent story: the company is culturally excellent but structurally ordinary. Only one of eleven patterns is a STRONG FIT, and it is Corporate Culture — and it rests on exactly the same evidence as the quality framework's top marks for candor and disciplined stewardship. Management quantifies its own failures precisely (a Rs.50 crore hit from a botched foundry ERP rollout that cut castings from 700 a day to 200, and Rs.50-100 crores lost to Jal Jeevan Mission delays), admits missing its own double-digit growth goal, refuses to book uncertain orders, and insists on advance or letter-of-credit payment terms rather than discounting. That same refusal to force product out the door also shows up in the Friendly Middleman pattern, where management explicitly protects dealers from cash-flow strain rather than pushing equipment onto them — good behaviour that scores as PARTIAL because it costs revenue in the short run. Pricing Power is a LIKELY FIT and everything else — recurring service revenue, brand, innovation, market-share gains and cost to replicate — lands at PARTIAL, generally because the evidence is anecdotal rather than tracked: R&D spend is never quantified, market share is claimed but almost never given as a percentage, and there is no advertising disclosure. Three patterns are outright NO FIT: the business is not a toll road (pumps are large capital equipment won through competitive tenders and dependent on government funding release), not a low-price player (it positions on efficiency and premium realisation), and not a forward integrator (it sells through dealers, distributors and contractors and deliberately declines to contract with government directly). That is why the multibagger score is only 33 and drags the overall rating down.

The risk review shows that the very traits behind the strengths are also the source of the two biggest dangers. The structural growth story — nuclear, water infrastructure, defence — is rated HIGH RISK for government dependency precisely because those end markets are set by policy: Jal Jeevan Mission state funding delays cut FY26 revenue by Rs.50-100 crore, KUSUM subsidies shape competitors' small-pump sales, and the nuclear opportunity depends on NPCIL and DAE. The offsetting comfort is that the company takes no direct government contracts, routes orders through dealers on advance or letter-of-credit terms, and has a broad non-government base in building and construction and industry. Cyclicality is the other HIGH RISK: the engineered project pumps that justify the premium pricing are also the lumpiest, swinging with customer capex in oil and gas, mining, marine and defence, with management describing its own results as in line with capital goods industry trends. The partial credit given to recurring revenue in the pattern screen is the same thing the risk review names as the silver lining here — spares, annual maintenance contracts, the 110-plus site framework contracts and cash-and-carry retail pumps are being built up deliberately to smooth those swings. Two risks are on WATCH: the premium positioning is defended rather than taken for granted, especially in retail small pumps where dealers and competition press on price, which is the mirror image of the Pricing Power pattern only reaching LIKELY rather than confirmed; and concentration bites on the supply side, where one American engine supplier delayed 13-14 large offshore packages in FY25 and Kirloskar Ebara is limited to an approved vendor list for seals, motors and fabrication. Technology, consumer preference and fashion risks are all rated LOW — pumps are mature, functional, repeat-purchase equipment — and no new-entrant threat shows up in the calls at all.

*"A culture that compounds"*

*"Raises prices, keeps the customers"*

**What the calls show:** By May 2026 Sanjay Kirloskar claims premium realisation tied to lowest life-cycle cost and European efficiency norms, and the company enforces advances/LCs rather than discounting — but there is no discussion of regular above-inflation price increases, and margins swing with product mix and commodity prices (14.4% in Q3 FY26 vs prior-year moderation) rather than showing steady pricing-led expansion.

> "customers have starte d understanding lowest life cycle cost... And therefore, we do get premiums for our orders" — *management, on an earnings call*
