# I R F C (IRFC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

IRFC (Indian Railway Finance Corporation) is a government-owned Indian finance company — an NBFC — that exists to raise money from lenders and investors and pass it on to Indian Railways and, more recently, to companies connected with the railway ecosystem. It borrows cheaply through bonds, bank term loans, foreign-currency loans (ECBs) and instruments like zero-coupon bonds, then leases rolling stock (locomotives, coaches, wagons) and funds railway project assets under a cost-plus lease agreement with the Ministry of Railways, earning a fixed, pre-agreed spread over its own weighted-average cost of funds — historically 40 basis points on rolling stock leases and 35 basis points on project asset financing. Because its borrowing costs are among the lowest in the market and its overheads are under 0.1% of revenue, the thin spread on a very large book still produces large profits; the company reports no NPAs in nearly four decades and had roughly INR 4.85 lakh crore of assets under management at end-FY26. It operates only in India, funding Indian Railways and, since FY26 (what management calls 'IRFC 2.0'), other government-linked borrowers with backward or forward linkages to railways.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Rolling stock leasing to Ministry of Railways** — IRFC funds locomotives, coaches and wagons and leases them to Indian Railways under a standard cost-plus lease at a consistent spread of about 40 basis points; together with project financing this was historically almost the entire book, with over 98% of AUM being direct exposure to the sovereign.
- **Project asset financing / project loans to Ministry of Railways** — Loans for railway infrastructure projects that the government budget does not cover, carried at a spread of about 35 basis points; management says IRFC is the sole extra-budgetary borrowing arm for this and funded around 31.4% of the Ministry's total capex in one recent year.
- **Lending to railway PSUs and SPVs** — Loans to railway-owned entities such as RVNL and IRCON, which made up only about 1.38% of AUM as of June 2022 — a small sliver next to the direct Ministry of Railways exposure.
- **Railway ecosystem lending — 'IRFC 2.0' diversification** — Started April 2025, this is lending to many clients linked to railways — central PSUs, state government entities, metros, joint ventures and SPVs (examples given: NTPC and its subsidiaries, Dedicated Freight Corridor Corporation, Hindustan Urvarak & Rasayan) — won through competitive RFPs and open bids at margins management describes as 2x to 3x the fixed railway margin; it delivered roughly INR 74,000 crore of sanctions and INR 35,000 crore of disbursements in FY26 and is targeted to be about 40% of the business mix by 2030.
- **Borrowing / treasury operations** — The funding engine behind everything else — a diversified mix of bonds (about 44% of borrowings in mid-2022), term loans (about 33%) and external commercial borrowings (about 17%), plus newer instruments like yen-denominated ECBs and zero-coupon bonds; top domestic credit ratings and sovereign-equivalent international ratings are what let IRFC quote the lowest rates and still make a margin.

### The story in depth

IRFC is best understood as two businesses stacked on top of each other, and almost every strength and worry in these records traces back to which of the two you are looking at. The old business is a statutory monopoly: for roughly 38 years IRFC was the sole extra-budgetary borrowing arm of the Ministry of Railways, which earns the highest mark in the whole framework (Mini-monopolies, +2) and underpins the Toll Roads and Cost to Replicate patterns. But that same arrangement is precisely what the risk review flags as HIGH RISK on Government Dependency and Stakeholder Concentration: the company is 86% government-owned, a single counterparty was 98.6% of assets, and the lending spread of 40bps on rolling stock and 35bps on projects was set annually by a standard lease agreement rather than by the market. The monopoly and the dependency are not two separate facts — they are the same fact described from the upside and the downside. That is why Toll Roads and Cost to Replicate only score PARTIAL: the moat was granted by the government, not built, and what is granted can be withdrawn.

The new business is what changed in FY26, and it explains most of the positive checks. After three years in which railway disbursement was effectively zero and assets sat flat around INR4.6 lakh crore, IRFC pivoted into the wider railway ecosystem — a market of roughly INR2.5 lakh crore where it holds only about 5% of its own asset base and is winning over 60% of the bids it enters. That single pivot drives four of the favourable checks at once (Capex for growth, Cyclical market growth, Gain in market share, Pricing, Mix and Volume) and the Market Share Gainers pattern, because the new lending earns 100-120bps against the legacy 35-40bps, so INR35,000 crore of new business is worth about INR1 lakh crore of the old. It also produced a clean promise-then-deliver year — INR74,000 crore sanctioned against INR60,000 crore guided, INR35,000 crore disbursed against INR30,000 crore — which is the basis of the PARTIAL score on Corporate Culture. The catch is that this record is one year old and the diversification is still only about 5% of a INR4.85 lakh crore book, with a stated long-run intention to keep Railways at 60%.

The uncomfortable link is that the very thing making the new business work is also what caps its quality. IRFC wins by being the cheapest quote — opex under 0.1% of revenue and the industry's lowest cost of funds, passed straight into price — which is the one STRONG FIT in the entire pattern screen (Low Price Plus) and simultaneously the reason three checks score negative and three risks sit on WATCH. Management itself calls the market cut-throat, with 7 to 15 bidders per deal, and admits IRFC induces the competition by undercutting banks and NBFCs; that is why Barriers to entry, Intangible benefits and Asset turn all score -1 or worse, why Pricing Power and Brand Strength are outright NO FIT, and why New Entrants and Good Enough Goods are flagged. There is no premium and no switching cost — only a cost advantage that must be given away to win. Layer on a balance sheet running INR4.85 lakh crore of assets at roughly 1.39% return on assets with gearing near 9-10 times, no geographic expansion beyond India, and no innovation beyond funding-instrument variety, and the combined score of 38 out of 100 — Mixed, two stars — follows directly: real monopoly heritage and a genuine growth inflection, wrapped around economics that competition can compress at any time.

Two things the records do not settle are worth naming. Resilience is marked UNKNOWN, so nothing here supports a view on how the company behaves under stress. And the return-on-capital area scores -1.0, the worst of the seven quality areas, against Growth at +0.6 — meaning the growth story and the capital-efficiency story point in opposite directions, and the case rests entirely on whether the higher-spread mix can outrun the price war that IRFC itself started.
