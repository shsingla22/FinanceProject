# Adani Power Ltd. (ADANIPOWER) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

Adani Power Limited is an Indian electricity generator that runs a fleet of large coal-fired (thermal) power plants — sites named on the calls include Mundra, Udupi, Raipur, Raigarh, Mahan, Godda, Korba, Dahanu, Tuticorin, Butibori/Vidarbha and Churk — totalling about 18.15 GW of installed capacity as of December 2025. It sells that power mainly to state electricity distribution companies (DISCOMs) such as Maharashtra's, plus a cross-border customer in Bangladesh supplied from the Godda plant, under long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs), and sells whatever capacity is not contracted into the merchant and short-term markets at prevailing market prices. Under the PPAs it earns fixed capacity charges simply for keeping plants available (management notes fuel cost is largely a pass-through), while merchant sales earn whatever the market clears at — Q1 FY27 realisation was INR 5.93/unit under PPAs versus INR 7.04/unit merchant. Revenue for the first quarter of FY27 was INR 17,936 crore with profit after tax of INR 4,867 crore, and the company is building toward a 45 GW portfolio, largely across central and eastern Indian states.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Power sales under long-term PPAs (contracted capacity)** — Electricity sold to state DISCOMs and the Bangladesh offtaker under multi-year contracts that pay fixed capacity charges for availability with fuel costs passed through; management says over 90% of the 18.15 GW operating fleet is now tied up this way, and PPA volumes were 25 of 29 billion units sold in Q1 FY27 — this is the core, most stable earnings stream.
- **Merchant and short-term / bilateral sales** — Untied capacity sold into the day-ahead, short-term and bilateral markets at market prices, which boosts profit when demand and tariffs are high but swings with weather and renewable output; it has shrunk from 6 billion units in Q1 FY26 to 4 billion in Q1 FY27 as more capacity moved into PPAs.
- **Capacity expansion programme (greenfield and brownfield projects)** — A roughly 24 GW pipeline of new coal units — Mahan Phase-II, Raipur Phase-II, Raigarh Phase-II, Korba Phase-II, Mirzapur, a 3,200 MW Assam project won on a DBFOO basis — with all boiler-turbine-generator equipment already ordered and 56% of the new capacity pre-sold under long-term PPAs; management says these new PPAs carry higher capacity charges than legacy ones and are the main driver of future earnings growth.
- **Acquisitions of distressed / operating power assets** — Buying existing plants cheaply, often out of insolvency, and turning them around — Mahan, Coastal Energen (renamed Moxie/Tuticorin), Korba (ex-Lanco Amarkantak), Dahanu, the Vidarbha/Butibori plant restarted from a multi-year shutdown, and in 2026 Jaiprakash Associates' assets including the 180 MW Churk plant plus minority stakes in Jaiprakash Power Ventures and Prayagraj Power; this has been a steady source of added capacity and volume growth.
- **Fuel security / captive coal mining** — Securing coal near its plants, including acquiring Stratatech Mineral Resources (the Dhirauli mine next to the Mahan plant) which is expected to supply 5 million tonnes of coal for Mahan's untied capacity — a supporting activity rather than a revenue line, aimed at protecting cost advantage.
- **One-time / prior-period regulatory income** — Past regulatory claims for things like domestic coal shortfall, carrying cost and late payment surcharge that get recognised in lumps — INR 2,781 crore in Q2 FY24 and INR 9,227 crore in 9M FY24, now shrinking to INR 1,353 crore in 9M FY26 as the old disputes are settled; meaningful to reported profit but explicitly not part of recurring earnings.
- **New areas: international hydropower and nuclear** — Early-stage diversification the CEO flagged in July 2026 — hydropower projects abroad and preparing for nuclear opportunities — with no capacity, revenue or timeline given on the calls.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Adani Power comes across as a fast-growing, low-cost builder of thermal power capacity rather than a business with a protective moat. The growth area scores highest of all seven quality areas (0.83), and the reasons are consistent across the three reviews: growth capex is massive, rising and largely self-financed — roughly ₹15,000 crore in the first nine months of FY26 against a programme of more than ₹2 lakh crore that takes capacity from 18.15 GW to 45 GW — and it is translating into real share gains, with capacity moving from 15.25 GW in Q3 FY24 to 18.15 GW and letters of award won in Assam, Maharashtra and Karnataka. Much of that growth has been bought cheaply from distressed sellers: Lanco Amarkantak, Coastal Energen, Butibori/Vidarbha and the Jaiprakash Associates assets all came through insolvency, which is why the 'share donators' check scores a full +2. The multibagger screen picks up the same story from a different angle, rating 'Low Price Plus' a strong fit — management anchors its whole competitive case on cost, through fuel logistics, plant locations next to captive coal mines such as Dhirauli, in-house execution and ordering turbine equipment in advance — and 'Market Share Gainers' a likely fit. The company earns about ₹37 of operating profit per ₹100 of sales and a 17% cash return on the capital employed, and the risk review judges overall resilience sound.

The awkward part is that several of these same traits are what create the vulnerabilities. Being the cheapest producer is exactly why the screen rates 'Pricing Power' and 'Toll Roads' as no fit: the company wins on the lowest bid in reverse auctions, so prices are set by tenders, regulators and merchant clearing levels rather than by the company. Merchant realisation has fallen from ₹6.86 a unit in Q3 FY24 to ₹4.54 and then ₹4.37, and revenue on contracts linked to imported coal falls automatically when the coal index falls. That mechanical link is also why the margin check, despite a healthy 37%, only scores +1 — margins have swung about 6.2 points a year, meaning key costs sit outside management's control — and it feeds directly into the cyclicality risk, rated elevated, where results visibly move with weather, demand and commodity indices. Similarly, the enormous asset base that makes the plants hard to replicate is the same reason the asset turn check scores -2: every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.38 of sales, and the screen notes that the site, land, captive mines and pre-ordered equipment are ultimately barriers made of money and time, not of anything a competitor with capital could not eventually buy. Management's main defence is contractual rather than competitive: open capacity has been cut from 20% to 10%, with new power purchase agreements carrying higher fixed capacity charges and fuel treated as a pass-through. That is what earns 'Recurring Revenue' a partial fit, and it is the stated silver lining under both the cyclicality and new-entrants risks — but it converts price risk into policy and counterparty risk rather than removing it.

That conversion is where the biggest single risk sits. Government dependency is rated high risk because essentially the whole business is policy-determined: revenue comes from state distribution company contracts won in government tenders, fuel comes through the SHAKTI coal allocation route, and large parts of profit are regulatory claims, including ₹9,227 crore of prior-period income in the first nine months of FY24. The competitive advantage area is the weakest quality score at -0.33, and the individual checks explain why — no network effects (-2), no image- or taste-driven pricing power (-1), and no obscurity to hide in (-2), since this is one of India's most closely watched listed names courting brand rankings and heavy analyst coverage. Three further risks are flagged as things to watch rather than immediate problems: cheap solar near ₹2.50 a unit resetting the order in which plants are called on, with management conceding that tariffs have to be lower in solar hours and plants back down to their technical minimum; the concentration of the Godda plant's single Bangladesh customer at roughly ₹2,473 crore of quarterly revenue, about 14% of Q1 FY27, with USD 400 million of receivables outstanding; and running a coal fleet into a decarbonising mood, met with a defensive posture of reciting improved ESG scores on every call. Set against that, the company is itself the low-cost option rather than the premium incumbent being undercut, so the 'good enough goods' risk is low, and delivery against past promises has been real — the FY24 plan for about 6,000 MW beyond 15,000 MW became a funded 24 GW pipeline, and Mahan Phase-II moved from 40% to 80% to commissioning. The combined rating of 48 out of 100 — 54 for business quality, 33 for multibagger fit and 54 for risk safety — lands the company at Mixed, two stars: a genuinely capable builder and operator whose economics are decided largely by parties other than itself.

*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Essentially the entire business is policy-determined: revenue comes from state DISCOM PPAs won in government tenders, fuel comes via SHAKTI coal allocation, large chunks of profit are regulatory claims (INR 9,227 crore of prior-period income in 9M FY24, coal-shortfall and tariff-compensation claims with Haryana settled at 50%), a regulator blocked the Rajasthan 3,200 MW PPA, the Bangladesh PPA is a sovereign-counterparty exposure with ~USD 400m receivables, and the entire nuclear ambition waits on government rules. ### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Results visibly swing with weather, power demand and commodity indices: merchant realisation fell from INR 6.86/unit (Q3FY24) to INR 4.54 and INR 4.37, 9M FY26 EBITDA declined YoY, then Q1FY27 EBITDA jumped 22% on a record heatwave — and revenue on import-linked PPAs moves directly with the HBA coal index.
