# Havells India Ltd. (HAVELLS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: CONSUMER GOODS*

Havells India Limited is an Indian company that makes and sells electrical goods and home appliances, selling under brand names including Havells, Lloyd, Crabtree and Standard. Its products run from building-electrical items like cables, wires, switches and switchgear and lighting, through household products like fans, air coolers, kitchen appliances, air conditioners and refrigerators, and more recently solar panels and inverters. It earns money by manufacturing these goods in its own plants and selling them mainly in India through a dealer/distributor trade channel plus an omni-channel retail presence, with a smaller export business (about 15% of switchgear sales), passing raw-material cost swings in copper, aluminium and electronics through to customers via calibrated price hikes. Demand splits between consumer/residential buyers — heavily seasonal, since roughly 65%-70% of air conditioner sales fall in the January-June cooling season — and industrial/infrastructure customers, and the company spends heavily and continuously on advertising and brand building (around Rs 600 crores in FY26) as a long-term investment.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Cables and Wires** — Makes industrial cables and domestic house wires; called one of the largest revenue contributors, its margins swing with copper and aluminium prices and with dealers stocking or destocking, and lately industrial cables have grown much faster than domestic wires.
- **Lloyd (consumer durables / home appliances)** — The air-conditioner-led appliance brand, being built into a 'full stack home appliances player' with a new refrigerator plant at Ghiloth; it is one of the biggest revenue contributors and a long-term investment case — it went from losses to only about 3%-3.5% EBIT margins by FY25, with management stressing small market shares in a very large industry.
- **ECD (Electrical Consumer Durables)** — Fans, air coolers and kitchen appliances sold to households; another top-three revenue contributor, growing about 20% in one quarter with margins helped by premiumisation (higher-priced fans) and cost savings.
- **Switchgears (switches and switchgear)** — Electrical switches and protection devices for buildings; about 10% of revenue but roughly one-third of margin contribution, with contribution margins consistently in the 38%-41% band, and roughly 15% of it exported.
- **Lighting and Fixtures** — Lighting products where selling prices had been falling for years but have now stabilised, with contribution margins that management says average 30%-32% over the long run.
- **Renewables** — A fast-growing solar business — panels, inverters and solar pumps — backed by a Rs 600 crore investment in Goldi Solar for module manufacturing (on which a Rs 283 crore fair-value gain was booked in Q4 FY26); it became a separately reported segment from Q1 FY27, with panels carrying lower margins than inverters.
- **Industrial and infrastructure categories** — The project- and industry-facing side of the portfolio (as opposed to consumer/residential), repeatedly described as the stronger, more resilient demand pocket in recent quarters.

### The story in depth

Havells earns its quality score on the strength of its returns and its end markets. Every ₹100 of capital in the business threw off ₹16 of operating cash this year, every ₹1 of assets produced ₹1.53 of sales, and ₹10 of every ₹100 of sales is operating profit with margins moving only about 1.8 points a year — steady, asset-light and well controlled. Behind that sits a genuinely wide distribution reach across Havells, Crabtree, Standard, REO and Lloyd, from deep rural coverage to online, backed by the CARE 360 service network. Management is spending into that base rather than harvesting it: roughly ₹1,400 crore of capital spending planned for FY27 including ₹800 crore on cables, capacity phases at Tumkur, a new refrigerator plant at Ghiloth, and a dedicated ₹200 crore research centre. The end markets are growing on their own — air conditioning is under-penetrated with first-time buyers arriving, and renewables, batteries and vehicle charging have now been carved out as a separate reporting segment. The leadership has also shown unusual patience, sticking with the Lloyd turnaround through four years of analyst pressure and refusing to manage the business to quarterly margin targets.

The multibagger screen picks up the same facts but finds most of them half-formed. Only brand strength scores as a likely fit, resting on advertising held at about 2.7% of revenue long-term (4-4.5% inside Lloyd), roughly ₹700-800 crore budgeted for FY27, spending more than doubling year-on-year in the June 2026 quarter, and a deliberate ladder of brands from value to premium. Seven other patterns come out only partial. The dealer-led model that makes distribution a real asset never gets quantified — no dealer economics, no own-store counts, no direct-to-consumer share — so the middleman and forward-integration patterns stay unproven. Pricing power is mixed: the 7-8% average staggered price increases of FY26-27 were absorbed, but the same record contains years of falling lighting prices and cables that simply track copper and aluminium. Innovation is visible in language and capital plans but not in disclosed research spending or patents. Market share ambition is stated every year but never evidenced with numbers, and Lloyd's own share is admitted to be small in many categories. Two patterns fail outright: nothing suggests customers are locked in by standards or by the product being a trivial line item, and the strategy is explicitly premium-led rather than cheap-and-scaled. That gap is what pulls the combined score down — business quality and risk safety both rate 68 out of 100, but pattern fit rates only 33, giving an overall 58 and a 'Decent' three-star grade.

The risk review shows several of these strengths are the same thing viewed from the other side. The channel that gives Havells reach is also where it is most exposed: in switches and switchgear, cheaper rivals buy dealer shelf space with 100-150 day credit terms while the electrician or dealer effectively makes the choice for a buyer who does not know the brands, and contribution margins have drifted down from about 41% — the 'good enough goods' risk sits directly on top of the distribution advantage. The same dynamic underlies the new-entrant watch, with Reliance moving in through BPL and Kelvinator and national switch players pushing hard on credit. Cyclicality is the other elevated risk and comes from the same product mix that makes the business asset-light: copper and aluminium swings and channel stocking and destocking repeatedly move the results, and Lloyd concentrates its demand in the summer. The renewables segment that supplies much of the growth story is openly framed as riding government support, and the energy-efficiency ratings that keep forcing product redesign in fans and air conditioners connect the government and technology watch items to each other. On the reassuring side, no single customer, licensor or distributor drives the numbers, demand is functional and repeat-purchase rather than fashion-driven, and consumer preferences are moving toward these categories rather than away — which is why overall resilience still reads as sound. One structural negative to note plainly: heavy advertising and constant coverage by six major brokerages mean nothing here is overlooked, so the price is unlikely to reflect a story the market has not heard.
