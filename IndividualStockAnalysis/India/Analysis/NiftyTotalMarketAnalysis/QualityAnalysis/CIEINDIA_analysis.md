# CIE Automotive (CIEINDIA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

CIE Automotive India (formerly Mahindra CIE, majority-owned by its Spanish parent CIE Automotive) makes metal and composite parts that go into vehicles — it does not make vehicles itself. It supplies these components to vehicle manufacturers (OEMs) and their suppliers across passenger cars and light vehicles, two- and three-wheelers, tractors, heavy trucks, and off-highway/construction equipment, winning multi-year orders for specific parts on specific vehicle platforms and then earning revenue on the volumes the customer actually builds; steel and energy cost swings are passed on to customers through negotiated price adjustments, so profitability depends on volumes, cost pass-through and plant efficiency. Roughly 65% of sales come from its plants in India and about 35% from Europe and a small Mexican operation, with consolidated quarterly sales around INR 23–25 billion and EBITDA margins in the 16–18% range in recent quarters. Management describes the company as a large, deliberately diversified auto-component group spanning many manufacturing processes, product lines, plants and customers, and it is adding a growing portfolio of parts for electric vehicles (about 11% of new orders won in Q1 CY26).

**The parts of the business:**

- **India operations** — The plants located in India, making a wide spread of components across technologies; about 65% of group sales, with the India end-market mix in CY25 being light vehicles 53%, two- and three-wheelers 23%, tractors 13% and heavy trucks 11%, and EBITDA margins around 17–18%.
- **European operations (incl. Mexico)** — Plants in Europe plus a small Mexican forging unit, contributing about 35% of sales; roughly 80% of European sales are forgings and more than half go into light vehicles, with margins recovering to about 15.7% in Q1 CY26 after restructuring in CY25.
- **Forgings** — Hot-forged steel parts such as crankshafts and other engine and driveline components — the company's biggest vertical in Europe (about 80% of European sales, through units like CIE Galfor and CIE Legazpi) and a major India business too, though the European forgings market has been weak lately on Chinese and Indian competition and the shift to EVs.
- **Bill Forge** — A forging business supplying two-wheeler parts including crankshafts and bearing races; management calls the two-wheeler crankshaft project one of its most exciting growth drivers and is adding capacity for it.
- **Iron castings** — Cast-iron components; a growth area where the company has won sizeable export orders as well as other new domestic projects.
- **Stampings** — Sheet-metal pressed parts, made at units such as Zaheerabad, with several new orders tied to customer Mahindra; the Zaheerabad unit has also received state subsidies that occasionally flatter reported margins.
- **Composites** — Non-metal composite parts, a smaller vertical, again with new order wins linked to Mahindra.
- **Aluminium** — Aluminium components (including the Aurangabad Electric operation); a smaller vertical currently being restructured, and from Q4 CY25 only the value-add portion of sales is recognised for some categories, which trimmed reported growth.
- **Magnetics** — A small vertical whose business portfolio management was restructuring during CY25, which held back overall India sales growth.
- **Metalcastello** — An Italian gear business selling into off-highway/off-road, earth-moving and mining equipment, heavily exposed to the US construction-equipment cycle; sales fell from roughly EUR 75–80 million a year to about EUR 50–60 million in CY24 and it carried one-off restructuring costs in CY25.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

The best things about this business are how it is run and how it handles money, not the position it occupies in its industry. Management scores highest on candour: every margin wobble is itemised for investors — a 0.8% gratuity charge, a 0.4% aluminium subsidy, a EUR2mn restructuring at Legazpi — and the company publishes its own growth against weighted market growth quarter after quarter, including the quarters it lost. That same disclosure habit is the entire basis for the 'Corporate Culture' pattern registering as a partial fit. The finances back it up: about ₹15 of operating profit on every ₹100 of sales, with margins moving only about ±2.6 points a year; ₹15 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital employed; and, unusually, cash arrives from customers 33 days before suppliers have to be paid, so customers effectively fund the working capital. On the framework's own area scores, Management and Return on Capital are the strongest (both about 0.67) and Capital Allocation is close behind (0.6).

The weakness is structural, and it is what pulls the combined rating down to 43 out of 100 — a Mixed, two-star verdict built from 56 on business quality, only 13 on multibagger pattern fit, and 54 on risk safety. Competitive Advantage scores zero and Industry Structure scores -0.5: this is a fragmented supplier base selling to a handful of powerful vehicle makers, and the proof is that even an extraordinary energy cost shock could only be passed on 60-70%, with the company absorbing the rest. That single fact is why four separate patterns come back as no fit at all — Toll Roads, Pricing Power, Low Price Plus and Brand Strength. Growth comes from volume, raw-material pass-through and currency translation rather than from charging more; management itself says pass-through dilutes the gross margin percentage while leaving absolute profit unchanged, and European sales fell about 2% in one quarter purely because steel prices fell. Demand is driven by which vehicle platforms launch (an XUV 3X0, a Bajaj CNG bike, Maruti SUVs), not by any brand of its own — the company even dropped the Mahindra name in 2023-24 with no revenue consequence. The one bright spot on the pattern screen, Recurring Revenue, is flagged as a numbers-only signal with nothing in seventeen earnings calls to confirm it, which is a screening hint rather than a finding.

The most useful way to read this company is to notice how often one trait shows up as both a cushion and a problem. The large and growing net cash pile (negative net debt of ₹18.8bn) is explicitly the silver lining that makes the elevated cyclicality risk survivable — and it is also exactly why the framework marks shareholder distributions at -1, with the dividend held flat at ₹7 and no buyback, and why management concedes the idle cash drags the return shareholders see down to 11.1% against 18.4% earned by the operating assets themselves. Likewise, the European forging business being undercut by cheaper Chinese and Indian suppliers appears three times over: as the reason 'Cost to Replicate' is no fit, as the elevated 'Good Enough Goods' risk, and as the New Entrants watch item, with two rounds of restructuring at Legazpi and Metalcastello as the visible cost. The Bajaj CNG bike volumes collapsing to roughly 10% of the prior-year quarter is simultaneously the customer-concentration risk (four anchor customers are 50-55% of India sales) and the shifting-preferences risk landing at once. And the product pipeline — fuel rails, EV housings, high-tonnage machined castings, EV-capable gears — is at the same time the +1 on research and development, the reason 'Innovation Dominance' rates partial, and the main defence against the elevated technology risk of moving from engine parts to electric ones, though no research spending figure is ever disclosed to size it. Growth capex is disciplined at roughly 5% of sales and order-backed, but the framework notes returns on it have been slow to appear, which matches share gains being episodic — strong through CY21-23, then five straight quarters at or below the market's own growth rate. Overall resilience is judged sound; the story is a well-run, well-financed, honestly reported business in an industry that does not let it keep much of what it creates.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Technological Innovation, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but none of the 3 computed fingerprints confirms it in the numbers yet — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across all 17 calls results swing with OEM production cycles, steel/aluminium prices and customer capex: Metalcastello (off-highway gears for Caterpillar) fell from EUR75-80m to EUR50-60m run-rate and required restructuring, Legazpi took a EUR2m restructuring charge in CY25, and European EBITDA margins have oscillated from 9.1% (Q4C21) to 16% (Q1C24) to 12.7% (Q4C25) on energy, steel and volume swings. > "As you know, this is a cyclical business, and we are now in the bottom side of the cycle" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Diversified segment mix in India (LV 53%, 2/3W 23%, tractors 13%, trucks 11%), raw-material pass-through clauses, negative net debt of INR18.8bn and continued countercyclical capacity investment in India while European peers restructure*

## What to watch

- Cash deployment — the 'Distribution to shareholders' check is the framework's worst governance mark: watch for a buyback or a raised dividend to close the 11.1% shareholder return versus 18.4% operating return gap, or for the pile to keep sitting idle.
- Growth versus the market — the 'Market Share Gainers' pattern is partial only because five quarters ran at or below weighted market growth; sustained outperformance, not one strong quarter, is what would upgrade it.
- The Metalcastello recovery — the 'Cyclical market growth' check gives credit for a Caterpillar-led upturn that has been promised before; actual movement off the EUR50-60m run-rate back toward EUR75-80m is the test.
- European restructuring outcome — whether Legazpi and Metalcastello stabilise or need a third round decides the 'Good Enough Goods' and 'New Entrants' risks and whether 'Cost to Replicate' stays a no fit.
- EV orders converting to revenue — the 'Technological Innovation' risk cites repeatedly delayed programme ramp-ups at Hosur, Bill Forge and aluminium; the roughly 10-11% EV share of new order intake actually reaching the top line is what would firm up 'Innovation Dominance'.
