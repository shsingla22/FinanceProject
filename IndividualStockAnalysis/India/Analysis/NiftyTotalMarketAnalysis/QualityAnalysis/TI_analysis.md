# Tilaknagar Inds (TI) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Tilaknagar Industries is an Indian alcoholic-drinks company that makes and sells branded spirits — what the calls call IMFL (Indian Made Foreign Liquor) — with brandy as its historical core and, since late 2025, whisky as well; it started in 1933 as a sugar mill and exited sugar roughly 50 years ago. It earns money by selling bottled spirits by the case to state-controlled liquor markets and distributors across India, plus the armed-forces canteen stores department and paramilitary forces, and exports to the Middle East, Africa and Southeast Asia; revenue is essentially cases sold multiplied by net sales realisation per case (about INR 1,165 per case in FY26). Production runs through a mix of company-owned plants and contract bottling units — 20 units across 12 states at the time of the FY24 call, of which 4 were owned — with its own Prag Distillery in Andhra Pradesh expanded from 6 lakh to 36 lakh cases a year. It is strongest in the southern states, where it says it is the largest prestige-and-above IMFL player with close to 40% share in Q4 FY26; FY26 volumes were nearly 20 million cases and revenue INR 2,346 crore.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Brandy portfolio (Mansion House Brandy, Courrier Napoleon Brandy, Flandy, Monarch)** — The original core of the company — around 90% of volumes as of FY24 — led by Mansion House Brandy, described as India's largest-selling brandy, which crossed 10 million cases in FY26, alongside Courrier Napoleon Brandy, the flavoured brandy Flandy, and Monarch Legacy Edition at the luxury end.
- **Imperial Blue business division** — A whisky brand bought from Pernod Ricard for INR 3,442 crore (plus EUR 28 million payable in FY30), completed 30 November 2025 and funded with INR 2,100 crore of bank/NBFC debt and a INR 2,093 crore share issue; it added 4.6 million cases in Q4 FY26 alone and management wants to make it India's largest-selling whisky, though its standalone margin (about 11.7%) is lower than the rest of the business.
- **Other own brands / non-brandy (Mansion House Whisky, Blue Lagoon gin, Seven Islands malt whisky)** — A smaller but growing set of non-brandy brands used to widen the portfolio beyond one category and one region — Mansion House Whisky selling in Assam's semi-premium segment, an entry-level gin, and the luxury Seven Islands pure malt whisky launched in Maharashtra and export markets in Q3 FY26.
- **Spaceman Spirits Lab partnership** — A tie-up (described as an investment plus a usership agreement) under which TI distributes third-party premium brands such as Samsara Gin, Sitara Rum and Amara Vodka through its own sales network; the calls present it as the route into luxury launches rather than as a large revenue line today.
- **Manufacturing and bottling (owned plus contract units, incl. Prag Distillery)** — The supply side of the business — mostly third-party contract bottlers with a few owned plants — where the main investment named is roughly INR 59 crore to expand the wholly-owned Prag Distillery in Andhra Pradesh, expected to save about INR 10 crore a year in bottling costs.
- **Exports and institutional sales (CSD, paramilitary)** — Sales outside the open domestic market — to the armed-forces canteen stores department and paramilitary forces, and to the Middle East, Africa and Southeast Asia — mentioned as part of the sales footprint but never sized in the calls.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Tilaknagar's case rests on a small number of things it does unusually well. Two of the eleven multibagger patterns are outright strong fits, and both are visible in the underlying checks. The first is brand: a family business founded in 1933 whose Mansion House brandy crossed 10 million cases in FY26, whose customers keep buying even when the trade stumbles (shop-level sales in Andhra grew 8% while sales into the trade were collapsing), and which keeps 46 rupees of every 100 rupees of sales after direct input costs. The second is culture: gross borrowings taken from about 1,200 crore rupees in 2019 to 45 crore with cash to spare by the end of 2024, the Prag distillery expansion from 6 lakh to 36 lakh cases delivered as promised for about 59 crore rupees and now saving roughly 10 crore a year in bottling, and bad news disclosed rather than buried. Around these sit the strongest individual checks: a deep southern distribution network the company rents out by carrying other people's brands, steady share gains from a non-dominant base (brandy from roughly 14-15% to 20%, southern spirits from 5.8% to 6.2%, and close to 40% of premium-and-above sales in the South after the Imperial Blue deal), share taken mostly from rivals who under-invested in marketing, and an end market adding around 10 million new legal-drinking-age consumers a year.

The uncomfortable part is that several of these same traits are also the largest risks, not separate from them. The distribution advantage is deep because it is concentrated: for most of the period about 85% of volumes came from South India and Andhra alone was roughly 30%, which is exactly why one state's change to how liquor is routed to market swung the whole group's revenue and is scored as an elevated concentration risk, worsened by state corporations being effectively the only buyer in some markets. The share gains came from a category rivals ignored, but that same brandy leadership is described by management as a legacy 'grandfather's drink' skewed to the South, while the new drinkers arriving each year are not attached to any one category, which is why shifting consumer preference is flagged for watching. Pricing power is only a likely fit rather than a strong one for the same reason government dependency is the single high risk: prices are set by state governments, not by the company, and Tilaknagar was pushed into cutting about 200 rupees a case in Andhra. And where rivals attack from below, the company has already been hurt once, when a new cheaper state-created format in Maharashtra pulled volume out of the very segment Imperial Blue sells into. The Imperial Blue purchase is the honest attempt to fix the concentration problem, taking the distribution reach pan-India, but it also explains why the 'hard to copy' pattern is only partial: the 30-year brand legacy was bought for 3,442 crore rupees rather than built, management could not articulate a barrier of its own, and the company now leans on the seller during the transition.

The financial record has not yet caught up with the strategic one, and that is what holds the rating down. On the headline measure, every 100 rupees of shareholders' money and borrowings in the business produced minus 8 rupees of operating cash this year, and about 217 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Those two checks pull the return-on-capital and capital-allocation areas negative even while industry structure, customer benefits, growth and management all score well, and they are the main reason the overall financial standing is judged strained. The pattern screen tells a matching story about the shape of the business: there is no subscription-like or repeat-contract revenue, no position as a small-but-essential input in someone else's costs, no ownership of the route to the customer (integration runs backwards into bottling and spirit-making, not forwards into stores), and no expert intermediary recommending the product. Every case has to be won again each year through advertising, new launches such as Flandy, Monarch Legacy Edition and Seven Islands, and through a state-controlled path to the shelf. Blended together, business quality of 59, pattern fit of 64 and risk safety of 50 give 58 out of 100 - a decent, three-star business: a genuinely good consumer franchise with a management team that has done what it said, currently mid-way through a large acquisition whose cash returns have not yet appeared, and whose fortunes are decided in large part by state governments rather than by the company itself.

### Every check, area by area

**Capital Allocation — Neutral (-0.17)** · 6 of 6 checks had evidence

  > "This expansion entailed an investment of around INR 59 crore, which has been fully invested... TI expects savings in bottling costs to the tune of INR 10 crore per annum" — *management, on an earnings call*
- **Advertisement and promotion — Good** *(from the conference calls)*: Management deliberately invested to build brandy's low share of voice ('A Warm Welcome') and plans to revamp A&SP on the under-invested Imperial Blue, with Mansion House crossing 10 million cases as evidence of durable brand equity, though at times margin expansion came from cutting S&D rather than investing.
  > "we feel that IB is a slightly underinvested brand for the last 4 - 5 years. We will look at revamping our investments in A&SP across markets" — *management, on an earnings call*
- **Research and development — Good** *(from the conference calls)*: No formal R&D line, but a consistent new-product pipeline has delivered commercially — Flandy (India's first premium flavoured brandy), Monarch Legacy luxury brandy, Mansion House Whisky and Seven Islands Pure Malt — with management still describing a live NPD pipeline.
  > "we launched our luxury whisky, Seven Islands Pure Malt whisky in the state of Maharashtra and certain export markets" — *management, on an earnings call*

**What the calls show:** Repeated across all four calls: a 1933 family heritage, Mansion House as India's largest brandy crossing 10 million cases in FY26, brand equity proven when Andhra secondaries grew 8% during a primary-volume collapse, and consistent stretch into adjacencies (Flandy, Monarch luxury brandy, Mansion House Whisky, Seven Islands) plus the acquired 30-year Imperial Blue legacy.

> "secondary volumes in Andhra Pradesh grew at 8%, indicating strong brand equity for our products" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Keeps ₹46 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs — room to fund brand, R&D and price leadership.
- ❌ Operating margin of 18% but swinging ±14.7 points a year — too volatile for a protected franchise.

### Corporate Culture — STRONG FIT
*"A culture that compounds"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** Fourth-generation family business with a documented promise-to-delivery record: gross debt cut from ~Rs 1,200 crore (2019) to Rs 45 crore with net cash by Q3 FY25, the Q3 FY26 TSMA-exit commitment met in Q4, Prag capex delivered as guided; bad news is disclosed promptly (AP price cut, April integration disruption, Telangana receivables), though four CFOs in five years and a Rs 2,100 crore acquisition loan temper the conservatism claim.

### Pricing Power — LIKELY FIT

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Virtually every driver of revenue and margin discussed on these calls is a state government decision — excise duty cuts driving Karnataka volumes, the Andhra route-to-market change that caused primary-volume degrowth and a forced Rs.200/case price correction, price increases that must be 'approved' state by state, MML created by Maharashtra policy, India-UK FTA duty on scotch, and state corporations as the buyer of record; by FY26 the company is carrying overdue government receivables in Telangana.

*Silver lining: Pan-India, pan-category diversification via Imperial Blue reduces reliance on any single state's policy, but the revenue base remains wholly policy-administered*
