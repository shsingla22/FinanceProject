# Orient Cement (ORIENTCEM) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Orient Cement (ticker ORIENTCEM) is an Indian cement manufacturer: it mines limestone, makes cement at its own plants, and sells it in bags and in bulk under the Birla A1 brand and premium labels such as StrongCrete, Orient Green and Dolphin. Its buyers split into two groups — individual home-builders and retail dealers (what management calls B2C or 'trade') and bulk institutional buyers on infrastructure and construction projects (B2B or 'non-trade'), which was about 56% of volumes in the December 2023 quarter. It earns money on tonnes sold times price per tonne, with realisations around Rs 5,400 per tonne and EBITDA around Rs 840 per tonne on roughly 14 lakh tonnes a quarter (about 44 lakh tonnes in the first nine months of FY24), so profits swing with cement demand, prices, and input costs like fuel, power and freight. It sells mainly in western and southern India — Maharashtra is its biggest market at around 60% of volumes, with Telangana as its 'home' state plus Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh; it later became part of the Adani group's cement platform and is being merged into Ambuja Cements.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Trade / B2C sales** — Bagged cement sold through dealers to individual house-builders; management treats this as the higher-priced, better-margin half of the business, and was unhappy when it shrank (Telangana B2C volumes fell 29% in one quarter) and pushed the trade share back up to 74% by early 2026.
- **Non-trade / B2B sales** — Bulk cement sold directly to infrastructure and large construction projects, mostly plain OPC cement; it was the growth engine when retail demand was weak (56% of volumes in Q3 FY24) but management calls the resulting mix shift adverse for margins.
- **Premium cement brands (StrongCrete, Orient Green, Dolphin)** — Higher-priced branded products sold mainly to retail customers, grown by word of mouth; they crossed 20-22% of sales by early 2024 and are the main reason realisations per tonne rose even when volumes fell.
- **Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) vs blended cement** — The two product types it makes — unblended OPC, which infra buyers prefer and which hit 50% of the mix in Q3 FY24, versus blended cement (using materials like fly ash), which is cheaper to produce and which the company prefers to sell more of.
- **Limestone mines and the Rajasthan greenfield project** — Its captive limestone mining leases, including Rajasthan mines restored to the company in 2022, on which it planned a brand-new plant to break out of its concentrated southern/western market exposure — described at the time as a major diversification opportunity still needing land acquisition and environmental clearances.
- **Part of the Adani / Ambuja 'One Cement Platform'** — From FY26 the company is reported as an acquired asset inside Ambuja Cements' group calls, with an amalgamation of ACC and Orient Cement into Ambuja under way and the acquired plants' capacity utilisation being lifted from 37% to 58% and targeted at about 80%.

### The story in depth

Judged on how the business runs day to day, Orient Cement has some real strengths. Customers effectively fund it: cash comes back 24 days before suppliers have to be paid, which is a rare and genuinely valuable feature. After direct input costs the company keeps about 76 rupees of every 100 rupees of sales. There is an itemised, long-running cost discipline behind that — power and fuel at about Rs.1,600 a tonne in August 2022, 687 kilocalories of heat and 63 units of power a tonne, a quarter of fuel from alternatives, waste-heat recovery worth about Rs.5 crore a month, and a stated target of Rs.3,650 a tonne of cost by March 2028. Sustained brand spending has visibly moved the mix upmarket: premium products went from roughly 14-15% of sales in FY23 to 21-22% in FY24, and to 35-36% of trade sales under the Adani-owned brands by FY26. Management on both watches has been unusually straight with investors — the earlier chief owned weak quarters without spin, and the successor put numbers on its own missed cost target and integration delays. This is why the framework scores customer benefits at 0.75 and growth at 0.67, and why the brand-strength pattern is the one clear fit on the multibagger screen.

The trouble is that the very traits behind those strengths also create the biggest risks, and in several cases they are the same fact seen from two sides. The premium brand push is scored as a likely fit on brand strength, yet the record shows the brand did not protect price: top brands sold at Rs.20 a bag below Birla A1 in Telangana, and refusing to match that price cost 29% of home-state consumer volumes — which is exactly why 'good enough goods' is flagged as a high risk and why the pricing-power pattern is marked no fit. The deliberate choice to hold price and give up volume (realisation of Rs.5,400 a tonne, up 5%, on 3% lower volumes) is scored as a positive on pricing and mix and simultaneously shows up as lost market share. The cost culture that supports the 'low price plus' pattern only earns a partial mark because delivery slipped, with FY26 costs and branding spend rising without matching realisation. The market-share gain to 16.6% at twice the industry's volume growth belongs to the acquirer's combined platform, not to Orient standalone, which was losing ground. And the cost advantages that might look like a moat — waste-heat recovery, solar, alternative fuels, grinding units, premium sub-brands — are all things money can buy, which is why 'cost to replicate' is no fit and competitive advantage scores zero: Adani proved the point by simply buying Orient, Sanghi and Penna, the same rollup that makes new entrants an elevated risk.

What finally sets the verdict is the return on the money invested and how exposed that money is. For every 100 rupees of shareholders' funds plus borrowings, the business produced minus 2 rupees of operating cash this year — a negative cash return, and the weakest area score in the whole framework. Spending has been for growth rather than upkeep — the Rajasthan greenfield after the mining lease was restored, waste-heat and solar, and roughly Rs.7,500 crore of capital spending in FY26 — but the payoff is unproven, with acquired plants running at only 46% to 58% of capacity. The acquisition record is the one outright negative check: large, complex deals for weak assets, with Penna needing unexpected maintenance spending and still at 46% utilisation. Around that sit high risks from the cycle itself (EBITDA a tonne collapsing from Rs.1,384 to Rs.750 in one quarter on flat volumes, and a Rs.400-500 a tonne cost spike in FY26 from a West Asia war) and from government decisions that gate both growth and demand — mining leases, land, environmental clearances, named projects and state elections. Add narrow market exposure, institutional sales swinging to 56% of volumes on longer credit, and fuel that leans on state-owned coal plus imported petcoke, and resilience reads as strained. The combined rating is 31 out of 100 — 45% weight on business quality at 54, 30% on multibagger fit at 11, and 25% on risk safety at 12 — a Weak, one-star verdict: a decently run, honestly managed cement business with no durable pricing edge and, so far, no cash return on the capital being poured in.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Good Enough Goods, Stakeholder Concentration, New Entrants. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.0× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ⚠️ Only about 46% of recent operating profit became cash (was 102%) — profits are getting stuck in paper, an early stress signal.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 8% to 14% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 2 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across all 13 calls the business swings with cement demand, monsoon, elections, infra capex and imported fuel prices: Q1FY23 EBITDA/t collapsed from Rs.1,384 to Rs.750 on flat volumes, FY26 saw cost spike ~Rs.400-500/t on a West Asia war while the industry was 'not able to pass on the price', and demand guidance was cut to 5-5.5% with capacity plans pushed from FY28 to FY30 — classic expansion-then-retrenchment behaviour.

> "EBITDA per tonne from 1384 last year it has come down to 750" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Cement is a consumption/flow product for construction rather than customer capex equipment, and premiumisation plus WHRS/AFR/green power has damped some cost cyclicality*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 4 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 10% and 24% (±4.7 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 60% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 4% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Fortunes repeatedly hinge on the state: the Rajasthan limestone mining lease restoration, land acquisition and environmental clearances gating growth; demand triggers named as government projects (Kaleswaram phase two) and stalled by state elections and administrative churn in Telangana; PSU Singareni coal pricing, railway rake allocation and lean-season freight discounts; Himachal goods tax, GST 2.0, limestone block auctions in Assam and railway bulk-terminal policy driving capex recalibration.

> "In the absence of demand triggers in the southern markets like the Kaleswaram phase two" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Geographic diversification (Rajasthan greenfield, later the pan-India Ambuja network) reduces dependence on any single state government*

### Good Enough Goods — HIGH RISK
*"Cheaper rivals that are good enough"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Management repeatedly defends a price premium against cheaper cement in a market where the mason/contractor intermediary often decides: top brands sold Rs.20/bag below Birla A1 in Telangana and Orient's refusal to match cost it 29% of home-state B2C volumes; by FY26 realisations were flat Q-o-Q while peers rose, and the industry could not pass on a Rs.400-500/t cost increase.

- ✅ Operating margin (20%) remains near its historical peak (24%) — no collapse in the premium.

## What to watch

- Cash return on capital turning positive from the current minus 2% — the single worst check, and the one that would most change the verdict.
