# Global Health (MEDANTA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Global Health Limited, which runs its hospitals under the Medanta brand, is an Indian hospital chain that treats patients directly — it sells medical care, from routine consultations to the most complex surgeries and transplants, and management repeatedly stresses it operates at the high-end 'tertiary and quaternary' level with about 30% of beds being ICU/critical care. It earns money by charging for inpatient stays (the key drivers management tracks are number of beds, how full they are, and average revenue per occupied bed, or ARPOB, which was about INR 66,550 in FY26), plus outpatient visits and an outpatient pharmacy; patients pay through cash, private insurance, corporates/PSUs, and government schemes such as Ayushman Bharat and CGHS. Everything is in India, concentrated in the northern and central 'heartland' — a flagship 43-acre, ~1,400-bed campus in Gurugram plus hospitals in Lucknow, Patna, Indore, Ranchi and, from September 2025, Noida — serving a population management describes as roughly 400 million people, with new projects underway in Delhi (Pitampura and South Delhi), Mumbai, Guwahati and Varanasi. Full-year FY26 total income was INR 45,089 million, up 20%, with EBITDA of INR 10,560 million and profit after tax of INR 5,541 million.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Mature hospitals (Gurugram, Indore, Ranchi)** — The established, already-profitable hospitals led by the Gurugram flagship; they are the bulk of the business at INR 28,482 million of FY26 revenue (roughly two-thirds of the group) with steady ~24% EBITDA margins and high-single-digit growth.
- **Developing hospitals (Lucknow, Patna, Noida)** — Newer hospitals still filling up their beds — Lucknow and Patna are now the fast-growing engine (22% revenue growth and 31.7% margins in Q3 FY26), while Noida, opened September 2025, is still loss-making by design (INR 906 million revenue against an INR 783 million EBITDA loss in FY26) with losses already narrowing quarter to quarter.
- **International patients business** — Treating patients who travel to India from abroad — Africa, Southeast Asia, the CIS region and the Middle East — worth INR 2,780 million in FY26, up 33%, and about 7% of consolidated revenue.
- **OPD pharmacy** — Selling medicines to outpatients through the hospitals' own pharmacies; a smaller but fast-growing line, up 30% year-on-year to INR 465 million in the December 2025 quarter alone.
- **Operation & Management (O&M) agreements** — An arrangement where Medanta runs a hospital it does not build itself — announced for a 750-bed hospital in Pitampura, New Delhi, to be developed with a society over about four years; still in the regulatory-approval stage and not yet contributing revenue.

### The story in depth

Global Health (Medanta) comes out of the three reviews as a high-quality, founder-built hospital group that is still in the middle of building itself. The quality framework gives full marks to the things that make a hospital hard to copy: doctors employed full-time rather than paid per case (1,400+ on the payroll, 550+ added in FY26 including 200+ senior clinicians at Noida), a brand that keeps INR 77 of every INR 100 of sales after direct input costs, and a management team that says awkward things out loud - naming Noida's INR 783mn FY26 loss and its worst quarter, admitting Lucknow was weak, calling the Indore litigation ambiguous and the South Delhi project roughly a year late. The balance sheet supports the ambition: net cash of INR 5,906mn, operating cash flow compounding at 21% a year over four years, and customers who pay 26 days before suppliers need to be paid, so the business is partly funded by its own patients rather than by lenders. Growth is organic and greenfield, with no large acquisitions in the five-year record - 623 beds added in FY26 and about 2,700 more planned through five greenfield projects and roughly INR 45,000mn of spending over five years.

The multibagger screen mostly just re-describes those same strengths from a different angle, which is why it scores so highly (94). Brand Strength, Corporate Culture and Cost to Replicate are all strong fits, and they rest on the identical facts: a seventh straight Newsweek best-hospital-in-India ranking and the only private Indian hospital in the world top 150; the same Trehan-Sahni-Gothwal team across all 14 calls with bad news disclosed promptly; and the sheer difficulty of assembling a 43-acre 1,400-bed campus, INR 45,000mn of new construction and 1,400+ super-specialists poached from AIIMS-calibre institutions, followed by a ramp that took Lucknow five years to go from developing to mature. Market Share Gainers and Innovation Dominance are rated only 'likely' rather than strong, and for an honest reason - the volume growth is plainly there (beds +20.5%, inpatient +16%, outpatient +19%, international revenue +33% to 7% of the mix in FY26) but management never quotes a market-share figure or names a competitor, and the clinical firsts (lung transplant, Da Vinci Xi, CAR T-cell therapy, a fifth radiation oncology machine) are bought-in technology absorbed into existing infrastructure rather than something invented in-house. Pricing Power is likewise conditional: ARPOB has climbed from INR 58,494 in 9MFY23 to INR 66,550 in FY26, with mature units at INR 74,941, but management attributes most of that to case mix and shorter stays and openly refuses to push tariffs.

That refusal is exactly where a strength turns into a risk, and it is the clearest link across the three reviews. The founding promise of world-class care 'at a fraction of the cost' is what makes the Low Price Plus pattern partially fit - and the same self-imposed restraint, combined with insurers, TPAs and government schemes sitting between the hospital and the patient on price, is what puts the Good Enough Goods risk on watch (ARPOB grew just 1.9% in Q2FY25 and 4-7% since). The founder-and-flagship story works the same way: Dr Trehan's visibility and the Gurugram campus are the source of the brand, and also the reason Stakeholder Concentration is on watch, with Gurugram holding about 1,220 of roughly 2,665 beds, still carrying all corporate manpower cost, and the three mature hospitals producing INR 28,482mn of FY26's INR 45,089mn. Government Dependency is real but bounded - 80-85% of the payor mix is cash and TPA and no beds are reserved by mandate, yet policy has cost real time and money, with Delhi pollution rules stalling the DLF site for about a year. The remaining four risks - cyclicality, technology, changing patient preferences and fashion - all come back low, because demand is medical necessity rather than taste and margins have held in a tight 23.9-26% band; new-entrant threat simply never appears in the calls. The one genuinely weak reading is inside the quality framework itself: return on capital scores zero and industry structure 0.5, which is the arithmetic cost of a company that keeps ploughing cash into hospitals not yet earning - the same behaviour that builds the moat. Net of all this the rating is 82, 'Outstanding', with resilience judged sound: 45% on business quality of 71, 30% on multibagger fit of 94, and 25% on risk safety of 88.
