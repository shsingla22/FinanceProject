# Ajanta Pharmaceuticals Ltd. (AJANTPHARM) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: PHARMA*

Ajanta Pharma is an Indian drug maker that manufactures and sells prescription medicines, mostly generic versions of established drugs sold under its own brand names. It earns money four ways: selling branded medicines to doctors and pharmacies in India through a field force of about 3,750 medical representatives; selling branded generics in Asia (Middle East, South-East Asia, Central Asia) and Africa (West and East Africa); selling plain generic pills in the United States under approved ANDA filings to distributors; and supplying anti-malarial drugs to aid agencies in Africa through tenders. Its therapy focus is chronic, doctor-prescribed areas — cardiology, ophthalmology, dermatology and pain, with gynaecology and nephrology being added. In FY2026 the company crossed Rs. 5,453 crores of revenue with net profit above Rs. 1,000 crores, gross margins around 78%, and it spends roughly 5% of revenue on R&D.

**The parts of the business:**

- **India branded business** — Sells its own branded medicines to Indian doctors and chemists through a large sales force; contributed 30% of total revenue in FY2026 (Rs. 1,654 crores, up 14%), with cardiology 36%, ophthalmology 31%, dermatology 23% and pain 10% of India branded sales, and the company ranked 24th in the Indian pharma market.
- **Trade generics (part of India business)** — A smaller unbranded/generic-counter channel inside India; recorded Rs. 188 crores in FY2026 versus Rs. 179 crores the year before — a modest slice of the India total.
- **Asia branded generics** — Branded medicines sold in the Middle East, South-East Asia and Central Asia, largely chronic therapies with 15 new launches in FY2026; sales were Rs. 1,175 crores in FY2026, down 1%, hurt by Middle East supply-chain and logistics disruptions.
- **Africa branded generics** — Branded medicines sold in West and East Africa; sales rose 15% to Rs. 861 crores in FY2026 with 8 new launches, and together with Asia this branded generic block made up 38% of company revenue.
- **US generics** — Plain (unbranded) generic products sold to US distributors, which requires filing ANDAs with the US FDA; the fastest-growing part of the company, up 49% to Rs. 1,557 crores in FY2026 on the back of 8 new launches, lifting it to 29% of total revenue.
- **Africa institution business** — Anti-malarial medicines supplied in bulk to government and aid procurement agencies in Africa; small and lumpy at roughly 3% of revenue (Rs. 160 crores in FY2026), and management repeatedly calls it unpredictable because it depends on donor funding and order flow.

### The story in depth

Ajanta Pharmaceuticals sells branded generic medicines, and almost everything good about the business traces back to one asset: a 5,680-person field force, including 3,750 medical representatives in India, that calls on doctors directly. That sales machine is why the quality framework gives full marks for customer convenience, why the multibagger screen calls the 'Friendly Middleman' pattern a strong fit, and why the company keeps taking share from a position that was never dominant — its rank in the Indian pharmaceutical market improved from 29th in 2022 to 24th by March 2026, growing 13% a year against the market's 10%. Because the medicines carry Ajanta's own brand names rather than competing purely on price, the company keeps ₹78 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs, and gross margins have held at 77-79%. Sales have compounded at roughly 13% a year with unusual consistency (₹951 crore in the June 2022 quarter to ₹5,453 crore in FY26, up 17% in FY26 alone), and research spending held steady at 5-6% of revenue has actually converted into products: eight US launches in fifteen months behind 49% US growth, and 26 India launches including five that were first in the country. Management is owner-run, stable, measured on returns (33-34% return on capital employed, 25-26% return on net worth), and notably honest about its own misses — flagging that one quarter's growth looked flattering on a weak base, admitting Asia ran below internal plan, and disclosing an inventory write-off. That combination earns a 'Strong' rating of 73 out of 100 and a resilience read of 'Sound'.

The uncomfortable part is that the same features producing the strengths also produce the two elevated risks. Selling through doctors means the doctor, not the patient, chooses the product — which is exactly what makes the field force so valuable and also what leaves Ajanta exposed to the 'Good Enough Goods' problem, since a prescriber can switch to an equivalent medicine without the patient noticing or objecting. That risk is already visible in the numbers: the US business, now 29% of revenue and the fastest-growing piece, is the archetypal commodity arena, and management disclosed 15-18% price erosion hitting US profitability in FY22-23. This is why the screen rates pricing power only a partial fit — India shows real pricing contribution (7 of 22 points of growth in one quarter came from price increases, with volumes still rising), but the US ran the other way. The US strength is genuine and different in kind — Ajanta wins there on supply reliability, quality and execution rather than price, which is what earned preferred-partner status and share gains — but it reaches customers through a structurally concentrated distributor and consortium channel that management repeatedly credits for the results, which is the core of the elevated stakeholder-concentration risk. The same concentration appears again in the new GLP-1 weight and diabetes opportunity: it is a real growth avenue in a secularly expanding end market, but the entire emerging-market version of it rests on a single supplier, Biocon, across 26 countries. That tie-up is also flagged under technology risk, because Ajanta had to buy its way into the biggest platform shift affecting its largest therapy area, cardiology at 36%, rather than develop it in-house.

The weaker spots are less about the trading and more about the balance sheet and the industry itself. About 246 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers — the single worst-scoring check in the framework — and that shows up in the low scores for capital allocation and return on capital as areas, even though the headline return figures look excellent. The industry structure score is the lowest of all, consistent with the screen's finding that several protective patterns simply do not apply: there is no subscription or service revenue, no position as a small-but-critical input that a customer cannot switch away from, no owned stores or direct-to-consumer channel, and no cost-leadership posture — management explicitly chooses to spend on representatives, products and research and absorb input inflation instead. What replication barrier exists is the field force and decades of distribution depth across India, West and East Africa, the Middle East and South-East Asia, which is real and slow to build but which management itself is currently demonstrating can be built. Against that, the quieter risks come out clean: demand is prescription-driven and repeat, tilted deliberately toward chronic therapies, so cyclicality, changing consumer taste and fashion risk all score low, and revenue is spread across four regions with none above about 38%. Two policy-linked dependencies remain on watch — the donor-funded Africa institutional business, which management calls unpredictable and has deliberately shrunk from 8% of revenue to about 3%, and US approvals that were held hostage for over a year to the timing of factory inspections. In short: a well-run, honestly managed compounder whose growth engine and its main vulnerability are the same thing, and whose cash cycle is the least attractive feature of an otherwise high-return business.
