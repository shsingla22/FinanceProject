# JSW Energy Ltd. (JSWENERGY) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

JSW Energy Limited is an Indian electricity generation company (part of the O. P. Jindal Group, listed in Mumbai) that builds and runs power plants and sells the electricity they produce. It earns money mainly by selling power under long-term Power Purchase Agreements to government and state buyers such as SECI, NTPC, SJVN, UPPCL, Tamil Nadu's TANGEDCO and Rajasthan discoms, plus group-captive and industrial customers including sister company JSW Steel, and by selling the remaining untied 'merchant' power on the day-ahead power exchanges at prevailing spot tariffs (about Rs 3.86/unit through FY26, rising to Rs 5.10/unit in Q1 FY27, with management claiming a ~20% premium to exchange averages). Its fleet mixes coal-fired thermal, wind, solar, hydro and battery storage, spread across many Indian states — Chhattisgarh, West Bengal, Odisha, Himachal Pradesh, Karnataka and the seven states of the acquired O2 Power platform. Installed capacity grew from about 7.7 GW in late 2024 to roughly 14.6 GW by July 2026, with another 14 GW under construction (all tied to long-term PPAs) and a target of 30 GW of generation plus 40 GWh of storage by 2030 backed by roughly Rs 1,30,000 crore of planned capex; FY26 EBITDA was a record Rs 11,041 crore.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Renewable energy generation (wind, solar, hybrid)** — Wind and solar farms — including hybrid and FDRE projects, agrivoltaics and floating solar — sold mostly under long-term PPAs to SECI and similar buyers; this is now the biggest slice of the company at roughly 61% of the 14.6 GW installed base, hugely boosted by the Rs 12,468 crore acquisition of the 4.7 GW O2 Power platform (blended tariff Rs 3.37/unit) announced in December 2024.
- **Thermal power generation (coal-based)** — Coal-fired baseload plants totalling about 5.7 GW — including the 1,800 MW operational KSK Mahanadi in Chhattisgarh bought through insolvency proceedings, the Utkal (Ind-Barath) plant in Odisha, the imported-coal Vijayanagar plant tied up with JSW Steel, and a 300 MW Maruti Clean Coal plant being acquired — described by management as regaining prominence as reliable, dispatchable, contracted capacity, with 3,200 MW being built at Salboni and 1,800 MW of brownfield expansion optionality at KSK.
- **Hydro power** — Run-of-river hydro plants — Karcham-Wangtoo, Baspa, the newly built 240 MW Kutehr project and the 150 MW Tidong plant bought from Statkraft — which management says make JSW Energy the largest private independent power producer in Indian hydro, and which generated 13-16% above design energy in FY26.
- **Energy storage (battery and pumped hydro)** — Grid-scale storage to firm up intermittent renewable power, with locked-in capacity of 29.6 GWh — 3.2 GWh in batteries and 26.4 GWh in what management calls the 'remunerative' pumped-hydro space — an area they say they are well ahead of the market on, though one early 1 GWh SECI battery project was put on hold after the regulator refused to adopt its tariff.
- **Merchant / short-term power sales** — The untied portion of generation sold on power exchanges and through back-to-back short-term contracts; management has deliberately shrunk this exposure to roughly 1.1 GW (about 8% of capacity, heading to 5%) by signing new PPAs, while noting it still delivers attractive 'dark spreads' when coal prices are low.
- **Equipment manufacturing / vertical integration** — In-house making of key power equipment to cut cost and supply risk rather than as a standalone revenue engine — a 5 GWh battery assembly plant in Pune (commissioned Q4 FY26, first external order about Rs 440 crore), wind blade factories at Halol and Chitradurga, a partnership with SANY for wind turbines, a raised 10.7% stake in the Toshiba JSW turbine-generator JV, and the pending purchase of GE Power India's boiler business.

### The story in depth

JSW Energy's strongest suit is doing what it says it will do, at speed, in a market that is genuinely growing. The culture check is the one outright strong result in the whole record: the 10 GW-by-FY25 target was met, Kutehr was built in under six years, Tidong was commissioned ahead of its October 2026 schedule, and 36% of the FY27 three-gigawatt build target was already done by the first quarter. That execution shows up directly as market share: installed capacity roughly doubled from 7.7 GW to 14.6 GW in two years and generation rose 52-60% while national electricity demand grew only about 1-3%, including 47% of the country's wind additions in one quarter and the position of largest private hydro producer. It is pointed at a market management describes as growing 5-6% a year for the long haul on industrialisation, urbanisation, electric vehicles and data centres, and the company earns ₹53 of operating profit on every ₹100 of sales. Two supporting patterns sit underneath this: a real and itemised cost discipline (coal from nearby mines at KSK and Utkal, in-house blades, boilers and turbines, borrowing cost down 67 basis points to 8.36%) and assets that are hard to copy (the finite hydro sites, 26.4 GWh of pumped storage, and grid connections management itself calls a national bottleneck).

The problem is that the very thing driving the share gain is also what drives most of the risks. Growth here is bought, not compounded: every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.15 of sales, the balance sheet carries about ₹62,000 crore of net debt against roughly ₹5,400 crore of quarterly revenue, and spending runs at about ₹20,000 crore a year heading toward ₹1.3 lakh crore by 2030. Funding that has made the company a serial issuer of shares — ₹10,150 crore raised through a promoter preferential issue, a QIP and the sale of a JSW Steel stake — with no dividend or buyback discussed, so existing owners are paying for the expansion rather than being paid by it. And the capacity being added does not come with the ability to set prices. Growth is almost entirely volume-led while realisations fell: day-ahead prices averaged ₹3.86 a unit in FY26 against ₹5 two years earlier, and first-quarter FY27 revenue was flat even after 1,558 MW of new capacity came online. That is why the pricing power, toll road and recurring revenue patterns all come back as no fit, and why the industry structure scores poorly — capacity is won in reverse auctions against many bidders (eight competed for the battery storage tender), and the buyer side holds the pen. The same regulatory counterparty that awards the contract can unwind it: the regulator refused to adopt the tariff JSW won on the 1 GWh storage project, putting it on hold pending appeal, and a rule change on depreciation cut hydro tariffs outright. That is the single highest-rated risk in the review.

So the picture is a well-run company operating in a structurally weak position, which is what produces the mixed rating of 42 out of 100 and the stressed resilience reading. Business quality scores 48, pattern fit only 33, and risk safety 42. Several risks are genuinely low — electricity is a functional, repeat-purchase product with no fashion element, sold on 22-25 year contracts, and there is no premium brand to be undercut by cheaper rivals — and the company has already moved ahead of the decarbonisation shift with renewables at about 61% of installed capacity. The main cushion against the earnings swings is that roughly 96% of capacity sits under long-term contracts where capacity charges are still collected even when plants are told to stop generating, and the whole 14 GW under construction is already contracted. But the swings are real: margins move about six percentage points a year, merchant prices ran from ₹5.00 down to ₹3.71 and back to ₹5.10, imported coal went from $116 to $81 to $113 a tonne, and hydro output fell 26% year on year in the latest quarter. Two watch-level risks compound this. Results still hinge on a handful of named buyers — Vijayanagar sells entirely to group company JSW Steel, KSK Mahanadi is 95% tied to two state distribution companies — and cheap solar plus storage is changing when thermal plants get dispatched, with analysts already asking about midday curtailment and plants running below their technical minimum. The portfolio does look to be just past a cyclical low, with demand up 8.5% and prices recovering, but that is a cycle turning rather than a moat forming.
