# ACME Solar Hold. (ACMESOLAR) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

ACME Solar Holdings is an Indian renewable-power producer that builds and then owns electricity-generating plants — mainly solar, plus some wind and, more recently, large battery storage — and sells the power they produce. It is "fully integrated," meaning it designs, engineers and constructs its own plants in-house (it does no third-party EPC work) and then runs them for the 25-year life of the contract. Most of its money comes from selling electricity at a fixed, pre-agreed tariff under long-term 25-year power purchase agreements, overwhelmingly with central government off-takers such as SECI, NHPC and REMC/Indian Railways (about 86% central off-takers), which makes revenue highly predictable; a newer slice comes from batteries sold on merchant and short-term terms. It operates entirely in India, with the bulk of its generating capacity in high-sunlight states, Rajasthan alone accounting for 2,250 MW of the operating fleet. Scale as of FY26: revenue of about INR 2,507 crore with ~90% EBITDA margin and PAT of INR 498 crore, roughly 2,990 MW of operating generation capacity and a total portfolio of 8,071 MW.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Solar (utility-scale)** — Large ground-mounted solar farms selling power under 25-year PPAs — historically 100% of the portfolio and still the core of it, including one of India's largest plants at 1,800 MWp.
- **Wind** — A small and newer addition, with the first 50 MW wind project commissioned in Q1 FY26 and 72 MW more in Q3 FY26 — used mostly as a component inside hybrid/FDRE projects rather than as a standalone business.
- **FDRE / Hybrid / Round-the-Clock (RTC)** — Contracts that promise firm, dispatchable power around the clock by combining solar, wind and batteries — the main area of new order wins (e.g., 250 MW FDRE with NHPC at Rs 4.33/unit, 130 MW RTC for Indian Railways at Rs 4.35/unit), and management says the company captured a large share of this segment.
- **Battery Energy Storage (BESS)** — Batteries that buy cheap off-peak power and sell it at peak, both bundled inside FDRE projects and as standalone contracts (first was 550 MWh with NHPC); about 2.3 GWh was commissioned by May 2026 earning roughly INR 2.2 crore a day on merchant/short-term sales, with a total portfolio requirement of around 17 GWh — the fastest-growing part of the business.
- **In-house EPC and O&M (the "integrated" model)** — Not a separate revenue business — the company does its own design, engineering, construction and 25-year operation solely for its own plants, which management says lets it control cost, timing and technology and stay competitive on tariffs.
- **Land, grid connectivity and financing** — A supporting activity management stresses heavily: securing grid connectivity and land in the best solar/wind zones (Rajasthan, Gujarat) before bidding, then borrowing long-term against the completed assets and refinancing to cut interest cost — the operational fleet's average debt cost fell to about 8.4%.

### The story in depth

ACME Solar builds, owns and runs power plants under 25-year contracts, and almost everything good and bad about it flows from that one fact. The company has been rewarded on how it deploys money: capacity roughly tripled from about 1.3 GW to 2,990 MW plus 2.3 GWh of batteries, the under-construction book stands at 5.1 GW within a total portfolio of 8,071 MW, and nearly all spending is growth spending at a disclosed EBITDA-to-capex yield of about 14.5% with a self-imposed floor of 16% returns per project. That is why capital allocation and management score positively. But the same asset-heaviness shows up as the worst numbers in the file: every rupee of assets produces only about seven paise of sales, and FY26 revenue of INR2,507 crore sits against INR12,475 crore of committed capex. The end market is genuinely expanding — India added roughly 55 GW of renewables in FY26 to reach 283 GW and crossed 50% non-fossil capacity five years early — but the combined rating of 34 out of 100 reflects that the growth is bought with capital rather than earned from customers.

The multibagger screen makes the same point from the other side: six patterns fit only partially and five do not fit at all, which is why that component scored just 10. The reason is a single trait — the product is electricity sold to central agencies at a price discovered in reverse auctions. That kills Pricing Power, Brand Strength, Toll Roads and Friendly Middleman outright, and it is the same trait behind the Industry Structure score of -1.0 and the Good Enough Goods and New Entrants risks: the buyer picks purely on the lowest tariff, so returns rest entirely on cost of capital and execution. Management has already conceded a tariff discount on an already-awarded NHPC greenshoe contract below the original 4.56 tariff, and it attributes the government's cut in battery subsidy from INR27 lakh to INR18 lakh per MWh directly to how aggressive rival bidding has become. The company's answer is cost, not price — in-house EPC rather than third-party, no trackers, over 10% capex savings against budget, modules and batteries pre-bought ahead of China's VAT change, and borrowing costs cut from about 9.5% to 8.4% through refinancing and rating upgrades. That is a real cost culture, but the screen rates it only a partial fit because cost leadership in a commodity auction is a defence, not a moat.

Two other traits cut both ways in the same manner. Concentrating offtake in central counterparties (86% central in Q1 FY26, moving toward 100% for the under-construction book) is what makes the revenue look like an annuity — availability above 99% and receivable days down from roughly 180 to 14 — and it is simultaneously the source of the Elevated stakeholder-concentration risk and a large part of the Highest-rated risk in the file, government dependency, where tariffs, subsidies, transmission waivers and purchase obligations are all policy-set. Likewise, the early move into firm/round-the-clock power and standalone batteries is what earns the Market Share Gainers partial fit and pushes the consumer-preference risk down to Low, yet the very speed of that shift is what makes a 100%-solar installed base a partial legacy asset, with new deviation-settlement rules from April 2026 penalising unfirmed output and management conceding that retrofitting batteries onto existing sites is necessary. The counterweight is credibility: founder-led since 2009 on one consistent contracting model, targets delivered as stated (450 MW met, merchant battery guidance raised from 1 GWh to 2 GWh, refinancing and a CRISIL AA- rating achieved as promised), and bad news quantified rather than glossed — the INR17.5 crore Sikar curtailment loss, the wind delay from a Gujarat sand-mining ban and extended rains, the per-module VAT impact. Resilience is judged Sound and the build-out is pre-secured with connectivity queued out to FY33, but on the framework's own weighting this is a well-run, honestly reported operator inside a structure that does not let good operators keep much of what they earn.
