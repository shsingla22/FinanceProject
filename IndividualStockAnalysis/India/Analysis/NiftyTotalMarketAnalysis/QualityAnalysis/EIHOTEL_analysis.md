# EIH Ltd. (EIHOTEL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: SERVICES*

EIH Limited (stock code EIHOTEL) is the Indian hotel company behind the Oberoi and Trident brands — it owns, builds and runs upmarket hotels and resorts, and also runs hotels for other owners under management contracts for a fee. It makes money mainly by selling rooms (the calls track it as occupancy x average room rate, giving 'RevPAR'), plus food and beverage, banquets/MICE events, and a separate non-hotel service line management calls OFS, which is lower-margin and tied to airport levies. Guests come through direct bookings, corporate accounts, MICE/social events (including weddings) and leisure/international travellers, with foreign arrivals concentrated in the October-March half. As of Q3 FY26 it had 4,209 rooms ('keys') — 3,801 in India and 408 overseas, of which 3,338 owned and 871 managed — with international properties in places like Mauritius, Al Zorah, Marrakech and Egypt, and a pipeline of about 30 hotels/2,450 keys over three to four years, including a 21-key luxury hotel in Mayfair, London.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Oberoi Hotels & Resorts (luxury segment)** — The premium flagship brand — city hotels like The Oberoi New Delhi/Mumbai/Gurgaon and resorts like Amarvilas, Rajvilas, Vanyavilas and the new Rajgarh Palace and Vindhyavilas; it is the largest and most profitable piece, described in FY23 as roughly a third of net capital employed at a 45% return on capital, and it consistently prices at a premium to its competitive set.
- **Trident Hotels (upper upscale segment)** — The step-down brand aimed at business and mid-market travellers, with a return on capital employed of about 22% in FY23; management says it has recently grown RevPAR faster than its industry segment (12.5% in Q3 FY26 versus 8.6%).
- **Owned hotels** — Hotels owned by EIH itself or through its joint ventures and associate companies (for example EIH Associated Hotels, which is building Trident Vizag) — 3,338 of the 4,209 keys, and the source of most revenue since EIH takes the full room and F&B income and the capital risk.
- **Managed hotels** — Properties owned by third parties but operated under EIH's brands for management fees — 871 keys, and the main way the pipeline is growing lately (all four hotels signed in Q3 FY26 — Kabini, Hampi, Coorg and one in Cairo — were management contracts).
- **Food and beverage / MICE** — Restaurants, banquets, conferences and social events inside the hotels; F&B revenue including managed hotels rose from Rs 143 crore to Rs 222 crore (+55%) in Q4 FY23, and MICE is repeatedly named as one of the fastest-growing demand segments, lifting cities like Bhubaneswar and Hyderabad.
- **OFS** — A separate, faster-growing but lower-margin service business linked to airports (the calls cite an airport levy as a cost); its rapid growth is repeatedly given as the reason consolidated EBITDA grew slower than revenue in FY26 — the calls never spell out the acronym.
- **International hotels and cruises** — Overseas properties (Mauritius, Al Zorah, Marrakech, Sahl Hasheesh in Egypt, plus Nile cruisers/luxury boats such as Oberoi Zahra) totalling 408 keys, with a 21-key Mayfair London hotel approved at GBP 69 million funded roughly half debt/half equity and with a partner intended to cut EIH's own exposure to under GBP 18 million.
- **Investment property / other assets** — Non-hotel property such as the investment property in Gurgaon, which together with hotel assets made up about 62% of net capital employed in FY23 at returns of 16-35%.
- **Surplus cash and treasury** — A large net-cash position kept for expansion — Rs 592 crore in Q2 FY25 rising to Rs 1,335 crore by end-FY26 — which earned Rs 113 crore of investment income in FY26 and funds the capex pipeline (Rs 680 crore spent in FY26) alongside debt.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

EIH earns its money by charging more, not by selling more. Every strong finding in the three reviews traces back to the same root: a heritage luxury brand that guests willingly pay a premium for. The framework scores full marks on pricing/mix (growth is explicitly rate-led with occupancy flat — Q4 FY26 room rates at owned hotels rose from ₹24,548 to ₹28,198, and management names rate as the primary lever), on intangible benefits (after direct input costs the company keeps ₹91 of every ₹100 of sales), and on market share (revenue-per-room index above 100 for years, 13 of 15 hotels ranked first or second against their benchmark group, FY26 Oberoi revenue per room up 10.4% against 6.4% for the luxury segment). Those same facts are what make Pricing Power, Brand Strength and Market Share Gainers the screen's best-fitting patterns, and they are also why three separate risk channels come back LOW: the premium is holding rather than being competed away (Good Enough Goods), it rests on decades of awards and service reputation rather than a passing trend (Fashion Risk), and the structural shifts discussed run in the company's favour (Shifting Consumer Preferences).

The catch is that the very same trait — a premium-priced, discretionary luxury stay — is what makes Cyclicality the one HIGH RISK in the review. Because growth comes from rate with occupancy flat, there is no volume cushion when demand wobbles, and the record shows it wobbling hard: a 621% jump in quarterly profit off a pandemic base, then FY26 described as 'a very volatile year' with Operation Sindoor, the West Asia war and flight cancellations. That is the same reason the Toll Roads pattern is a clear NO FIT (a hotel stay is a big discretionary line item, not a small unavoidable one) and it is echoed in the framework's single worst check: persistence of growth scores -2, with sales averaging only about 5% a year and lumpy from year to year. Alongside this sit three narrower WATCH items that have each already cost real money or growth — assets on government land or leases (the Mashobra/Wildflower Hall dispute, ₹69 crore of provisioned lease rentals and penalties, a ₹109 crore exceptional loss), single-counterparty exposures (the lapsed Adani-controlled Mumbai airport lounge lease, repeatedly named as why FY26 growth trailed peers), and rivals adding rooms faster in places like Bangalore. Notably, the deliberate choice to price at the top is also why Low Price Plus is a NO FIT: management explicitly rejects cost-cutting as a strategy.

What keeps the story steady is the balance sheet and the owners. Customers effectively fund the business — cash comes back 295 days before suppliers need paying — and the company moved from ₹271 crore of net debt in FY23 to ₹1,335 crore of cash by FY26 while still spending ₹680 crore on growth, all organic, with a hard 15% return hurdle on every project and returns disclosed brand by brand (45% on Oberoi hotels, 22% on Trident). That discipline is simultaneously a top framework score, the evidence behind the Corporate Culture pattern, and the stated cushion against the cyclicality risk. The honest gaps are that several patterns only half-fit: the recurring, capital-light management-fee stream is real (871 managed keys of 4,209 today, 24 more managed hotels planned by 2030) but never quantified on renewals; direct booking has plateaued; and hotel rooms, however prime the sites, can ultimately be built by well-funded rivals, which is why Cost to Replicate lands at PARTIAL and why the framework's own competitive-advantage and return-on-capital areas score zero despite the strong narrative. Netting it out, the combined rating is 66 out of 100 — Strong, four stars — with resilience judged SOUND: a genuinely high-quality operator whose main uncertainty is the timing and steadiness of demand, not the durability of its brand.

## What to watch

- Rate momentum: if room-rate growth stalls while occupancy stays flat, the core evidence for the Pricing Power pattern and the 'Pricing, Mix and Volume' check disappears at once.

- Balance-sheet discipline: any erosion of the ₹1,335 crore net cash, or a relaxed 15% return hurdle, to fund the 30-hotel pipeline would weaken the 'Disciplined stewards' check and the main defence against the HIGH Cyclicality risk.
- Another government-lease or concession dispute of the Mashobra/Wildflower Hall type — the Government Dependency risk has already produced a ₹109 crore exceptional loss.
- Replacement of the lapsed Mumbai airport lounge business and the direction of Oberoi Flight Services — the named driver of the Stakeholder Concentration risk and of FY26 growth trailing peers.
- Growth consistency: continued lumpy, roughly 5%-a-year sales growth would keep the 'Persistence of growth' check at its -2 score and cap the overall rating.
