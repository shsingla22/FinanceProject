# Sun Pharmaceutical Industries Ltd. (SUNPHARMA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: PHARMA*

Sun Pharma is an India-headquartered drug maker that sells prescription medicines — both branded/generic formulations and its own patent-protected "innovative" (specialty) products such as Ilumya, Cequa, Odomzo and Winlevi — to doctors, pharmacies and patients in roughly 100 countries. It makes money by manufacturing and selling these medicines at scale, with gross margins around 80% and EBITDA margins of roughly 27%-30%; full-year FY26 sales were about INR 582 billion (~US$6.2 billion) with adjusted net profit of about INR 124 billion. Its biggest markets are India (~33% of sales, where it is the number-one ranked company with about 8.4% market share) and the United States (~29% of sales), followed by emerging markets (~19%) and rest of the world. It grows through heavy R&D spending (about 6%-7% of sales, roughly half of it on specialty) and through acquisitions — Taro (2010), Ranbaxy (2015), Alchemee, and the pending purchase of Organon & Company announced in April 2026, which will be funded with significant debt (about 2.3x combined EBITDA).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Global Specialty / Global Innovative Medicines** — Sells the company's own patented, higher-margin branded drugs — Ilumya (psoriasis), Cequa, Odomzo, Winlevi, Leqselvi, Unloxcyt — worldwide; it grew from about US$191 million a quarter in FY23 to US$1,420 million for full-year FY26 (22% of company sales) and crossed US$1 billion in the US alone, where it is now bigger than the US generics business.
- **India formulations (India business)** — Sells branded prescription formulations to Indian doctors and chemists through a large field force, launching dozens of new products a year (11 in Q4 FY26, including semaglutide injections); it contributed INR 192.9 billion in FY26, about 33% of consolidated sales, and is the number-one ranked company by prescriptions in the Indian pharmaceutical market.
- **US business** — Combines the specialty/innovative products with a generic-medicines business (including Taro, taken fully private in 2024, and Alchemee); US sales were about US$1.9 billion in FY26, roughly 29% of the company, with generics under pressure from added competition while innovative products grew.
- **Emerging Markets** — Sells branded formulations across developing markets outside India, running at about US$306 million in Q4 FY26 (up 17.4%) and roughly 19% of consolidated revenue.
- **Rest of the World** — Formulation sales in other developed markets outside the US, described as around US$190 million a quarter and roughly 12%-13% of consolidated revenue.
- **R&D pipeline (generics and specialty)** — Develops new generic filings and innovative drugs and takes them through the US FDA and European regulators; consolidated R&D was about 6.3% of sales in Q1 FY25, with specialty accounting for roughly 45% of that spend.
- **Organon & Company (announced acquisition, April 2026)** — A pending purchase of a similar-sized, similar-profit but no-growth global company — leading in women's health (No. 2 in contraception, No. 3 in fertility), plus 50-plus established brands (55% of its revenue) and six biosimilars, present in 140 countries with ~30% EBITDA margins and ~US$1 billion of free cash flow.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Sun Pharmaceutical earns a 67 out of 100 overall score and a "Strong" (four-star) rating, built from 67 on business quality, 56 on multibagger fit and 80 on risk safety, with resilience judged sound. The strongest parts of the business are how it reaches customers and how much it keeps from each sale. Distribution is the clearest durable advantage: the largest field force in India after a deliberate expansion, the No.1 prescription ranking across doctor categories, a combined 24,000-strong commercial front end after the Organon deal, and a presence in more than 150 markets, all of which also make Sun the partner of choice when other companies want to license products out. That reach is paying off in share: India share moved from 8.3% to 8.6% on one measure and 8.1% to 8.4% on another, with management calling FY26 the best share gain since Ranbaxy, and 6% volume growth against the wider Indian market's 1.6%. After direct input costs the company keeps about ₹80 of every ₹100 of sales, operating profit is ₹28 per ₹100, and margins have been steady. Sales have grown around 7% a year, consistently, in end markets that are themselves expanding — an Indian pharma market of Rs.2,450bn, women's health at $35bn growing 6-10%, and a $70bn biosimilar opening from expiring patents.

The patterns the screen picks up are the same strengths seen from another angle. "Friendly Middleman" is a strong fit precisely because Sun sells through prescribing doctors rather than to patients — the very field-force and prescription-rank machinery that scores well on distribution and convenience. "Market Share Gainers" is a likely fit for the same share and volume numbers above, and the screen notes the gains were won through volume and new launches rather than bought. "Innovation Dominance" is a strong fit on rising R&D — from Rs 4.6bn a quarter in early FY23 to Rs 9.76bn, or 6.7% of sales, with innovative work moving from 21% to 37-45% of the spend — funding a steady run of named products including Ilumya, Cequa, Winlevi, Odomzo, Leqselvi and Unloxcyt. That same patience shows up in the quality checks, where taking Ilumya from an in-licensed product to $796mn of global sales and pushing Innovative Medicines past $1bn in the US, overtaking generics there, is scored as independent, long-term behaviour. "Brand Strength" is a likely fit on the strength of owned Indian brands such as Pantocid, Levipil, Susten and Serlift and the 50 established brands Organon brings, though the calls never tell a heritage story around them. Several patterns land only as partial — culture, cost to replicate, recurring revenue, toll roads and forward integration — and the reason for the cost-to-replicate limit matters: the innovative franchise would be genuinely hard to copy, but roughly half of revenue still sits in generics and branded generics that would not be.

The risks are largely the flip side of those same traits, which is why the review lands on five "watch" items rather than anything clean. Selling through doctors makes the middleman position strong, but it also means the prescriber, not the patient, chooses the brand — part of why "Good Enough Goods" is a watch item alongside persistent price erosion in US generics and Cequa competing against a generic Restasis. The same generics half of the business that caps the cost-to-replicate pattern is what "New Entrants" flags: described as competitive as far back as Q1 FY23 and still driving the US decline in FY26. The in-licensing model that powers the innovation cadence also concentrates the growth — Ilumya alone was $796m of FY26 innovative sales, with management conceding that lenalidomide and China royalty income are lumpy. And the screen finds no pricing power at all, because management explicitly disowns price-led growth; growth comes from volume and launches instead, which is a strength for share but leaves no price lever if volumes slow. Regulators, not subsidies, gate a lot of the outcome: the Halol plant's FDA status ran unresolved from Q1 FY23 into FY25, US launches depend on FDA action dates and court decisions such as the injunction on Leqselvi, and India sells into a price-controlled market. Offsetting all this, revenue is spread across more than 100 markets (US 28.8%, India 33.2%, emerging markets 19.2%, rest of world 13.8%), demand is prescription-driven rather than tied to anyone's capital spending, and net cash rose from $2.0bn in FY23 to $3.2bn in FY26 — which funded the Organon deal. The weakest internal number is working capital: about 228 days of cash sits between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and capital allocation is the one quality area scoring below zero.
