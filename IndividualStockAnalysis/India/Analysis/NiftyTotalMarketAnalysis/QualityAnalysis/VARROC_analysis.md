# Varroc Engineering (VARROC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Varroc Engineering is an India-based auto-parts maker (registered office in Aurangabad, Maharashtra; listed on the NSE and BSE) that designs and manufactures components and sells them to vehicle makers, with two-wheeler manufacturers as its most important customers and three-wheeler, passenger-vehicle and commercial-vehicle makers alongside them; it also sells spares through an aftermarket business. Its products span plastic/polymer body parts, lighting (headlamps and small lamps), electrical and electronic parts, and metal/forged transmission and powertrain parts, and increasingly EV-specific items such as traction motors, controllers, telematics and chargers. It earns money on a per-part basis under multi-year programmes won from customers, so management tracks 'new business wins' (lifetime and annualised peak revenue) as the driver of future sales, with margins moving on volumes, commodity price pass-throughs and cost control. Manufacturing and sales are concentrated in India — the profitable, faster-growing part — plus overseas operations in Europe (Italy, Romania), Vietnam and Thailand and a joint venture in China; the loss-making four-wheeler lighting business in Europe and America was divested in FY23, and by FY26 group revenue was about INR 8,891 crore (INR 88.9 billion) at a 9.4% EBITDA margin, with EV-linked models about 13% of sales.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Polymer / plastic components (PBU)** — Moulded plastic body parts and panels for vehicles — described in FY23 as the single largest product line at more than 30% of revenue.
- **Lighting** — Headlamps and small lamps — four-wheeler lighting in India (and now a Thailand plant) plus two-wheeler lighting in India and globally from Italy, Romania and Vietnam — about 24% of revenue in FY23; the overseas piece struggled on customer concentration but is winning large orders again, with a turnaround expected from H2 FY27.
- **Electrical and electronics** — Electrical and electronic parts made in India (about 18% of revenue in FY23) and an overseas electronics business, including newer high-voltage e-powertrain electronics for a global EV customer to be built in Romania and wall chargers.
- **Metallic components / forging** — Metal transmission and powertrain parts such as gears and crankpins (about 12% of revenue in FY23), including the IMES forging operation in Italy, which management flags as one of the challenged overseas units.
- **EV-specific products** — Traction motors, traction controllers (worth over INR 15,000 per vehicle for the pair), telematics and electronic fuel injection systems; sales to EV models rose from roughly 5% of revenue in FY24 to about 13-14% in FY26, and around 65% of FY26's record INR 33 billion order intake was EV-related.
- **Aftermarket** — A replacement-parts business selling into the spares market, listed by management as one of the revenue lines alongside the OEM product groups.
- **Business Unit 1 and Business Unit 2** — From FY26 the group is run as two units under separate CEOs (Arjun Jain and Dhruv Jain) with their own finance controllers, the reporting structure through which the India and overseas product businesses are managed.
- **China joint venture** — A joint venture whose profit or loss is reported separately from operating PBT — a drag during the 2022 Shanghai lockdowns, later a contributor (INR 444 million of profit in FY24).

### The story in depth

Varroc earns good money on the capital it uses, and that is the clearest strength in the record. Every ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.83 of sales, cash comes in from customers eight days before suppliers have to be paid, and ₹100 of capital in the business generated ₹20 of operating cash this year, with reported return on capital employed of 24.4%. Much of that return comes from deliberately not spending: capital spending was held well below depreciation for years (about ₹200 crore against ₹380 crore of depreciation in FY24) while debt was paid down, taking net debt from roughly ₹2,700 crore in FY23 to about ₹495 crore in FY26. The same discipline shows up as the one management-behaviour pattern that partly fits — the European four-wheeler lighting sale closed on schedule, the dividend was raised, and bad news such as an EV customer receivable problem was disclosed rather than buried. But the trait that produced the high returns is now reversing: capital spending is stepping up to ₹450–500 crore to serve the highest-ever order book, so the returns from here depend on new spending working as well as the old restraint did.

On the growth side, the story is real but narrow. Electrification is genuinely lifting how much Varroc sells per vehicle — revenue from electric models went from 5.3% of sales in FY24 to roughly 13–14% in FY26, growing about 50% a year, and 65% of the record ₹33 billion order intake is electric-vehicle linked. Engineering output backs this up: traction motors and controllers put into mass production correctly the first time, fuel injection developed in record time, more than 130 patents. Yet on the eleven-pattern screen only one item is even a likely fit (the roughly 10% aftermarket business, which is faster-growing and higher-margin), four are partial, and six do not fit at all — which is why the multibagger score is just 18 out of 100. The reason is the same in every case: Varroc sells parts to vehicle makers who both choose the supplier and set the price. There is no consumer brand, no installer channel, no certification lock-in, and pricing is explicitly a lagged recovery of raw-material and currency costs rather than a lever management can pull. That single fact explains both the good news and the bad — margins have moved only about ±1.4 points a year, which the framework reads as rational pricing and few new entrants, and it equally explains why margins sit at 9–10% against a 12% target and why most of the portfolio is treated as replaceable, standard-specification content.

The main risks are the flip side of the same traits. Selling to a small number of vehicle makers is what makes the negative working capital possible, and it is also the elevated risk: Bajaj alone is 42% of revenue in FY24 rising to 46% by Q3FY26, the whole reported e-mobility segment is purely Bajaj, and management itself blames the multi-year overseas losses on depending on one customer. Overseas expansion is the weakest check in the file — Romania electronics and Italian forging still are not breaking even at the operating level four years on, with the turnaround date repeatedly pushed back. The electrification shift is simultaneously the structural growth driver and a watch-level risk, because the older engine-related products still dominate sales and were the explicit drag on India growth in the December 2025 quarter, when India grew 12.3% against industry growth of 15–20% — the same quarter that softens the market-share pattern to partial. Demand is also policy-sensitive without being regulated: management credits a GST cut for the FY26 volume surge, and a state incentive worth about ₹50 crore a year for ten years flows through margins. Nothing here is fragile — resilience reads as sound, one risk is low and one shows no signal — and the combined rating of 52 out of 100, a three-star "Decent", is exactly what you would expect from a well-run, cash-generative supplier whose economics are set by its customers rather than by itself.
