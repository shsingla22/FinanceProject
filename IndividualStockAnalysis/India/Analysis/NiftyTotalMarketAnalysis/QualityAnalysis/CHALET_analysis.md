# Chalet Hotels (CHALET) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Chalet Hotels is an Indian company that owns and develops hotels, plus some office and residential real estate, mainly in and around big cities like Mumbai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Delhi NCR and Goa/Udaipur. Its main business is selling hotel rooms and food and beverage to business travellers, wedding and conference (MICE) groups, airline crew and, increasingly, leisure guests — the hotels carry outside brands such as JW Marriott, Westin, Novotel, Hyatt, Taj and Ritz-Carlton, with Chalet owning the buildings and the brands running/marketing them, plus its own new resort brand 'Athiva'. Alongside the hotels it earns steady rental income from office towers it owns (the 'CIGNUS' buildings at Powai and Whitefield) and one-off cash from selling flats in a residential project in Bengaluru. By FY26 the group did about INR25 billion of consolidated revenue and roughly INR10 billion of EBITDA, with 3,389 hotel rooms operating and about 1,655 more in the pipeline.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Hospitality** — Owning and operating branded hotels and resorts (rooms plus restaurants, bars and banquets), which management calls the core of the platform — roughly 88% of revenue in Q1 FY23 and still the bulk of the INR20.7 billion ex-residential FY26 revenue, at margins of about 41-46%.
- **Rental and annuity / Commercial real estate** — Leasing out office space it owns (about 2.4 million sq ft leasable as of 2024, branded CIGNUS at Powai and Whitefield), described as a deliberate move into a different asset class to smooth out the hotel cycle — small but growing, at roughly INR355 million of quarterly revenue in FY25 rising to an INR280 million monthly run-rate by March 2026, with high margins around 75-80%.
- **Residential** — Selling apartments in a project at Koramangala, Bengaluru — a finite, one-time cash generator rather than an ongoing business; 138 units had been sold since relaunch by mid-2024, and by FY26 it added only about INR166 million on top of the ex-residential revenue.
- **Development / pipeline** — Building new hotels and towers and buying land or existing properties to convert — e.g. the Taj hotel at Delhi International Airport, Hyatt Regency at Airoli, CIGNUS Powai Tower II, a Goa resort, the acquired Udaipur resort (INR1,710 million for 144 keys) and a 330-key Ritz-Carlton in Hyderabad — the main use of capital and the source of future growth in both segments above.

### The story in depth

Chalet Hotels owns rather than manages its hotels, and the whole story starts there. The properties sit where nobody else can build — attached to the arrivals hall at Delhi's T3, inside office parks, at a metro doorstep, on scarce South Goa beachfront with approvals already in hand, on prime Udaipur land — alongside 2.4 million square feet of offices and a residential business. That physical scarcity is what earns the top marks on convenience (+2) and drives the strongest pattern in the screen, Cost to Replicate, which a well-funded copycat simply cannot buy its way past. It also shows up in the economics: after direct input costs the company keeps ₹80 of every ₹100 of sales, with hospitality margins of roughly 46-49%, helped by a genuine and itemised cost discipline (payroll down to 13% of revenue from 15% pre-COVID, utilities 6% versus 7%, a room-to-employee ratio of 0.87). Management scores well on the human side too — candour marked +2 for openly owning misjudgements such as the election-impact call, the Vashi RevPAR dip and Powai construction hurting occupancy; disciplined stewardship marked +2 for growing organically with no transformative acquisitions; and seven straight years of Great Place to Work certification with an internal promotion of Shwetank Singh to MD & CEO. Those same traits are what make Corporate Culture the second STRONG FIT in the pattern screen, and they sit in an end market described as being in secular expansion, with supply persistently lagging demand.

The trouble is that the very design that creates the moat also creates the biggest weaknesses, and this is the central tension in the file. Owning the bricks means every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.38 of sales — the worst check in the framework at -2 — and growth has to be bought with enormous capital rather than funded by customers: about ₹15 billion of capex over seven quarters in FY25 rising to roughly ₹30 billion planned for FY27-29. That is exactly why Recurring Revenue is a NO FIT — there are no subscriptions, renewals or float anywhere in sixteen calls — and why the headline 20% cash return on capital, decent on its own, still leaves the Return on Capital area slightly negative overall. The capital spending itself is being done well (explicit 18-20% ROCE targets, roughly ₹19 billion deployed over two years with about ₹15 billion self-funded while net debt fell from ₹25 billion to ₹19 billion and borrowing costs came down to 7.48%), but it is a bet placed years ahead of the revenue. And the revenue arrives on a cycle, not a schedule: sales have compounded at about 18% a year but lumpily (-2 on persistence of growth), and Cyclicality is the single HIGH RISK in the review — the calls span a full swing from management's own 'sectoral up cycle' language to Q4 FY26 RevPAR falling 3% with a 7.7 percentage point occupancy drop on geopolitics and aviation. The office annuity portfolio is the partial answer here: 2.4 million square feet at an 83% margin, expected to throw off ₹3-4 billion of steady cash a year at stabilisation.

The second connected thread runs through the brand and the price point. Chalet is graded only PARTIAL on Brand Strength because the names guests actually book — Marriott, Westin, JW, Novotel, Taj, Hyatt, Ritz-Carlton — are licensed from others, so the heritage and marketing scale belong to the operators. That single fact is also the Stakeholder Concentration risk (WATCH), and the company's answer to it, its own Athiva brand launched at Khandala with Vashi converting and Aravali rebranded, is simultaneously what earns it a PARTIAL on Forward Integrators. The pricing story splits the same way: rate-led growth is real and repeated (16% ADR growth and 12% RevPAR in Q3 FY26, residential prices 14% above the prior year), but the screen calls Pricing Power only a LIKELY FIT because it looks more like a favourable supply-demand cycle than power the company owns — and the risk review finds the matching soft spot, rating Good Enough Goods ELEVATED after management flagged softening confined to the ₹25,000-40,000 band in Goa with no resistance at ₹15,000-20,000, and after Vashi's RevPAR fell 7% when similar-category rooms opened nearby. New Entrants is a WATCH for the same reason — competition arrives as new supply resetting a micro-market, not as a new format, with roughly 700 Marriott rooms already in Aerocity and more coming. Government Dependency is a WATCH too, and again it traces back to the moat: the irreplaceable Delhi asset exists because of a DIAL bid with the shell built by the airport operator, and it is now delayed by pollution-led work stoppages, while Airoli waited years for environmental clearance. The combined rating lands at 60 out of 100 — Decent, three stars — from 61 on business quality, 59 on multibagger fit and 58 on risk safety, with resilience judged SOUND. That is a fair reflection of a company with genuinely irreplaceable assets and honest, disciplined management, whose returns are held back by the weight of those assets and whose earnings ride a cycle it does not control.

The trade-off in one line: Cost to Replicate and Corporate Culture are the two things worth paying for here; the asset-heavy design, the licensed brands and the cycle are the three things that cap what you should pay.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 3 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Hotels are a demand-cycle business and the calls span a full swing: post-COVID recovery framed by management itself as a 'sectoral up cycle' and 'favourable demand-supply arbitrage', then Q4 FY26 RevPAR down 3% with a 7.7ppt occupancy drop on geopolitics/aviation disruption. Simultaneously the company is investing heavily into the upcycle (~INR19bn deployed over two years, INR30bn planned FY27-29, 1,655 keys in pipeline), the classic expansionary-period over-investment pattern; management explicitly acknowledges the risk by building annuity CRE against it.

> "a strategic move into alternate asset class designed to mitigate the cyclicality of the hotel industry through annuity income" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: CRE annuity portfolio (2.4mn sq ft, 83% EBITDA margin, INR3-4bn stable annual cash flow at stabilisation) plus residential cash flows; capex largely funded from internal accruals with net debt down from INR25bn to INR19bn*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±48 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from 2% to 43% (swinging ±13.3 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 56% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 16% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Good Enough Goods — ELEVATED
