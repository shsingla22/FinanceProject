# Fedbank Financial (FEDFINA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Fedbank Financial Services (FEDFINA) is an Indian non-bank lender (NBFC) that makes small, mostly collateral-backed loans to self-employed people and small business owners — the segment management calls "emerging India" — whose income is hard to document and who larger banks generally won't underwrite with standard models. It lends against whatever the customer can pledge: gold jewellery (gold loans) or property (loan against property, both small-ticket and medium-ticket), and it previously ran an unsecured business-loan book that it stopped originating and fully sold down, so the on-book portfolio is now over 99.5% secured. It earns money mainly from the spread between the ~15-18% yields it charges and its borrowing cost, supplemented by co-lending tie-ups with banks and by direct assignment (selling loan pools), though management has deliberately shrunk direct-assignment income in favour of core interest income. It operates a branch-led, physically intensive model across India — 607 branches in 17 states at listing in late 2023, growing to over 750 branches by FY26 — with total loan assets (AUM) rising from about Rs. 10,000 crore in Sept 2023 to Rs. 21,136 crore by June 2026; it listed on the NSE and BSE on 30 November 2023 and is majority-linked to Federal Bank, which it refers to as its parent.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Gold Loans** — Loans against pledged gold jewellery to small customers, including a fast-growing "doorstep" service where the loan is done at the customer's location; it is the company's largest and fastest-growing business, with AUM of Rs. 11,191 crore (about 53% of the book) by Q1 FY27 versus Rs. 3,969 crore in March 2024, and management calls it one of the two "twin engines."
- **Small Ticket LAP (ST LAP) / Small Ticket Mortgage** — Small loans against residential or commercial property to self-employed borrowers at high yields; it was the company's stated focus area at listing, then hit heavy delinquencies through FY24-FY25 requiring new leadership, rewritten credit policy and a system-driven rules engine, and it disbursed Rs. 904 crore in FY26 while management described it as "stabilized" and again a growth business (as of FY27 it is being run together with Home Loans).
- **Medium Ticket LAP (MT LAP)** — Larger loans against property, positioned as the lower-risk, lower-capital half of the mortgage book, run partly through direct assignment to conserve capital; it grew 44% in FY25 to Rs. 4,394 crore (28% of AUM) and disbursed Rs. 2,180 crore in FY26 with stable yields.
- **Business Loans (BL) — unsecured** — Unsecured loans to small businesses, which management deliberately wound down: originations were stopped during FY25 (Rs. 1,656 crore, under 10% of AUM at March 2025), and the remaining Rs. 886 crore portfolio was 100% assigned away in FY26 to free capital and shield the company from stress in unsecured lending.
- **Co-lending and Direct Assignment (DA)** — Rather than fund every loan itself, it partners with banks — a large private bank and a foreign bank were the first co-lending tie-ups, mainly for gold — and sells loan pools to investors; the gold co-lending book reached Rs. 2,127 crore by Q4 FY26, while DA income was deliberately cut 89% in FY26 to Rs. 7 crore and is now used as a capital-allocation tool rather than a profit source.
- **Branch network / "Vyapar" branches and collections** — The distribution and recovery machinery behind the lending: over 750 branches by FY26 (148 new gold branches added that year), with 70 small-ticket LAP branches folded into gold branch premises as combined "Vyapar" branches selling both products, plus a collections organisation that was verticalized by product and roughly doubled in headcount over FY26 to bring credit costs down to 0.8%.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Fedbank Financial comes out of all three reviews as the same company: a fast-growing, well-run, but structurally ordinary lender. The growth is real and mostly self-made — assets under management up 27% to Rs 20,153 crore, gold loans up 76% year-on-year to Rs 10,352 crore, the book now 99.5% secured after a deliberate exit from unsecured lending, credit cost down from 1.8% in FY25 to 0.8% in FY26, and return on assets of 2.6%. It is being built through owned distribution rather than bought growth: 750-plus branches with 148 new gold branches added in FY26, doorstep gold loans scaled from nothing to roughly Rs 1,787 crore and over 15% of the gold book, and third-party agent-sourced lending dropped because it did not earn its cost of capital. Assets per branch have kept rising (Rs 12.1 crore to Rs 16.5 crore to Rs 17.7 crore), so the branch-building is growth spending, not maintenance. Management scores highest of anything here — it publishes its promises at the start of the year and reports against each one, and openly owns the short-term LAP delinquency problem, the stubborn cost base and the difference between optical and real movements in yields and bad loans. That is why the multibagger screen credits Market Share Gainers, Forward Integrators, Innovation Dominance and Corporate Culture, but only as partial fits, and why the combined rating lands at 54 out of 100 — 'Decent' — from 49 on business quality and 64 on risk safety.

The reason nothing scores a full fit is that the growth has no protective wall around it. Return on capital scores worst of all the quality areas, competitive advantage and customer benefits score zero, and industry structure is negative, and the pattern screen agrees: Pricing Power, Cost to Replicate, Low Price Plus, Friendly Middleman and Recurring Revenue are all outright no-fits. The high yields — 15.7% to 17.5%, with gold above 19%, against roughly 8.7% funding cost — are payment for lending to under-served, hard-to-assess borrowers, not the reward of a brand or a franchise, and they are already drifting: yields fell 12 basis points year-on-year as the mix shifted toward lower-yield gold and medium-term LAP, and management shaded gold origination yields in the fourth quarter to win volume. The same fact shows up on the risk side as both 'Good Enough Goods' and 'New Entrants' watch items, because credit is an interchangeable product and management itself says competition simply has not arrived in its catchments yet rather than being kept out. What genuinely helps is the size of the opportunity rather than the strength of the position: management says only about a fifth of demand in the small self-employed and MSME secured-credit market is served, and that under-penetration, plus underwriting that requires branch-level judgement of borrowers with no formal financials, is the only real protection identified.

Several strengths and risks here are the same trait viewed from two sides. The owned branch-and-doorstep network that earns the Forward Integrators credit is also what makes the model asset-heavy and increasingly borrowed against: debt to equity has climbed from 3.98 to 4.89 and capital adequacy has fallen to 20.71%, with direct assignment and co-lending needed to keep relieving capital — which in turn creates the stakeholder-concentration watch item, since a granular base of borrowers (35,000 new gold customers in one quarter) sits on top of a small set of institutional partners, even with 42 lender relationships, new foreign lenders and a $250 million external borrowing. Gold, now more than half the book, is the engine behind the market-share pattern and the risk flagged under cyclicality: the 76% growth came largely on bullion price tailwinds against only 12% tonnage growth, and a 15% fall in domestic gold prices between January and June 2026 turned the same driver the other way, with the steady 10-12% tonnage growth the only price-independent part. Regulation is the one channel rated high risk outright and it feeds the competitive weakness directly — the November 2023 RBI risk-weight change lifted funding cost and dented capital adequacy, draft guidelines on bank-NBFC overlap with parent Federal Bank forced replanning, and a rule change now lets banks write higher loan-to-value income-generating gold loans against Fedfina. Fashion risk and technology disruption are both low, since the demand is functional, repeat and collateral-backed, and there is no government revenue dependency. Overall resilience is recorded as unknown, which fits a company whose execution is proven but whose economics depend on competition staying away.

**What the calls show:** Regulator action repeatedly and materially reshapes the P&L and the product itself: the Nov-2023 RBI risk-weight change raised cost of funds and dented capital adequacy, draft RBI guidelines on bank-NBFC overlap with parent Federal Bank left the company making representations and sitting at 'status quo' on its own structure, and the April-2026 LTV/bullet-loan rewrite forced a redesign of the core gold product with reported Stage 2 rising 2.2%-2.7% purely on the rule change while co-lending transactions broke down in transition.
