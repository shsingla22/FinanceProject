# Waaree Renewables (WAAREERTL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Waaree Renewable Technologies is an Indian solar power project contractor — it builds solar plants for other people under EPC (engineering, procurement and construction) contracts, describing itself as an end-to-end provider that can finance, construct, own and operate solar projects. It is a listed subsidiary of Waaree Energies, India's module-manufacturing group, and wins work both from commercial and industrial (C&I) customers and from government/PSU tenders for utility-scale projects, mostly in India with a growing pipeline of international enquiries. It makes money by executing megawatts from a contracted order book — revenue recognised as projects are built — with some contracts being 'turnkey' (Waaree also supplies the solar modules, usually sourced from Waaree Energies) and others 'pure EPC' where the customer supplies modules; module-inclusive jobs carry much higher revenue per megawatt. FY2026 revenue was about INR 3,331 crores with PAT of INR 479 crores, executing 2,727 MWp in the year against an unexecuted order book of 2.83 GWp.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Solar EPC** — The core business — designing and building ground-mounted and other solar plants for C&I clients and PSU/utility tenders; it drives essentially all of the reported revenue, with 2,727 MWp executed in FY26 and a 2.83 GWp unexecuted order book, and management calls itself a leader in India's solar EPC space.
- **Turnkey orders (EPC with module supply)** — A sub-set of EPC contracts where Waaree also procures and supplies the solar modules — mostly Waaree Energies' own modules unless a customer specifies otherwise — which lifted realisation to roughly INR 2 crore per MW in Q4 FY26, when management said about 50% of execution revenue came from module-inclusive orders.
- **O&M (operations and maintenance)** — Running and maintaining solar plants after they are built, giving recurring revenue; the portfolio stood at about 1,180 MWp (1.18 GWp) as of December 2025 and March 2026, and management calls it a growing driver as its installed base and hybrid/storage projects expand.
- **BESS EPC (battery energy storage)** — A new, still-emerging line — building battery storage systems as tenders increasingly bundle storage with solar; management says 5–6 GW of its tender pipeline includes BESS, but no revenue from it is separately reported yet.
- **Green hydrogen** — An early exploratory area mentioned in the FY23 call, where the company won a contract to set up a 1 MW green hydrogen plant, tiny relative to the solar EPC business.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Waaree Renewables builds solar projects for other people on a contract basis, and on the raw numbers it has grown extraordinarily fast: revenue went from Rs 351 crore in FY23 to Rs 3,331 crore in FY26, average sales growth of about 153% a year, with 2,727 MWp executed in FY26 against an order book of 817 MW back in June 2023. That growth has come without a matching build-up of capital — management says it is 'not seeing any other metrics go up in terms of capital requirement' — and the business turned every Rs 100 of shareholders' funds and borrowings into Rs 27 of operating cash this year, a 27% cash return on capital and the single strongest check in the whole review. The end market is genuinely expanding too: India's non-fossil capacity crossed 274 GW against a 500 GW-by-2030 target, solar drove 82% of FY26 renewable additions, and battery-storage contracting has opened as an adjacent line. This combination is why the screen marks 'Low Price Plus' as a likely fit and 'Market Share Gainers' as a partial one — the company has clearly grown far faster than an industry whose annual solar additions went from 23.8 GW to 44 GW.

The uncomfortable point is that the very trait producing those returns — owning almost nothing — is also the reason the company has no protection. Management states outright that it manufactures nothing and buys all equipment externally, with no R&D and no proprietary technology, so the quality framework scores technology advantage, switching costs, network effects and industry structure all at their worst level, and the screen returns outright 'no fit' on pricing power, innovation dominance and toll-road economics. Every project is a fresh fixed-price tender or bilateral award where the customer sets the scope, right down to whether modules are even included, so growth is pure megawatt volume and pass-through rather than better pricing. That same absence of a barrier shows up directly on the risk side: new entrants are rated elevated, with an analyst in April 2026 tying the fall in fourth-quarter margins from about 25% to about 19% to aggressive bidding by newcomers, and management conceding tender rates keep falling. It also explains the wild margin record — quarterly margins between roughly 10% and 48%, profit margins swinging by plus or minus 13.6 points — and the 'good enough goods' problem, where the only defence management offers is scale, execution and the ability to pre-qualify for tenders, which the review notes a funded rival can accumulate in a few years.

What partly offsets this is that management appears to know it. The 'Corporate Culture' pattern is a partial fit because promises have been kept — the roughly 15% margin floor set in November 2024 was beaten at 19.24% in FY26, growth guidance was met, and cash-flow discipline has been stated from the first call — and the company is deliberately building something more durable out of its own cash: an operations-and-maintenance book on 1.18 GWp of installed capacity, plus 54.8 MWp of self-owned generation with over 200 MWp under construction, all funded from internal accruals with no debt. That is the 'Recurring Revenue' and 'Forward Integrators' story, and it is exactly the silver lining cited against the high cyclicality risk — but it is still under 5% of revenue, with management saying more than 95% still comes from contracting. Two further dependencies sit underneath everything: essentially the entire demand narrative rests on government policy — the 500 GW target, ALMM, import duties, PM-KUSUM, PM Surya Ghar, PSU tenders at 15-20% of the order book — and the company sources modules from its parent Waaree Energies on a related-party basis, so both a key input and its pre-qualification credibility trace back to one relationship. Set against a business that is also the opposite of an overlooked niche, being a listed arm of a high-profile group covered by Goldman Sachs, Kotak and Axis, the combined rating lands at 34 out of 100 — Weak — from 44 on business quality, 18 on multibagger fit and 36 on risk safety, with underlying resilience judged sound.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**What the calls show:** Revenue is solar EPC sold into developers'/PSUs' capacity-expansion capex, so the top line swings with the customer capex cycle, and management repeatedly ties per-MW pricing and margins to module and commodity prices; margins have ranged from ~10% to ~48% quarterly and order book has oscillated (2.3GW → 1.7GW → 3.2GW → 2.8GW). > "Depending mainly on the module price and the commodity price, it is ranging... you see the volatility in the margin" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Growing O&M portfolio (1.18 GWp) and self-funded IPP assets building a recurring, operations-linked revenue stream; BOS-only contracts push module price risk back to the customer*
