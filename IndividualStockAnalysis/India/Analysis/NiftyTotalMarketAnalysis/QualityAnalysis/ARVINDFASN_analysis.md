# Arvind Fashions (ARVINDFASN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Arvind Fashions Limited (part of India's Lalbhai Group) is a branded clothing company that sells apparel and related products to Indian consumers under a portfolio of five brands it owns or licenses — U.S. Polo Assn., Tommy Hilfiger, Calvin Klein, Arrow and Flying Machine. It makes money by designing/sourcing the product (sourcing is predominantly India-based) and selling it at retail prices through its own stores (EBOs), its own websites/apps and online marketplaces, and by wholesaling to multi-brand outlets and department stores. The mix is mainly menswear — shirts, polos, denim, t-shirts, blazers — plus growing 'adjacent' categories like footwear, innerwear, accessories and kidswear, which management says are now about 24% of the business. Full-year net sales value was about INR 4,620 crores in FY25 and grew 14% in FY26 (Q4 FY26 alone was INR 1,365 crores), with the company operating in India and steadily shifting sales toward direct-to-consumer channels (62% of sales in Q1 FY27, targeting 65%).

**The parts of the business:**

- **U.S. Polo Assn. (USPA)** — A licensed American sportswear brand that management repeatedly calls its largest and 'marquee' brand — it 'led the pack' with the highest-ever growth in Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27, and its adjacent categories (footwear, innerwear, etc.) alone were over 25% of its revenue by FY24.
- **PVH brands — Tommy Hilfiger and Calvin Klein** — Two licensed super-premium international brands sold in India; management describes them as a 'stellar' double-digit growth and profit engine, with wide accessory ranges (watches, kidswear, innerwear, small leather goods) making up roughly 20% of their retail business.
- **Arrow** — A formal/premium menswear brand (with a premium '1851' line and wrinkle-free shirts) that reached a better stage of profitability in FY25, but was subdued in Q4 FY26 on a model change and weak wedding season; it is described as sitting mainly in the wholesale business.
- **Flying Machine** — A youth denim brand, now fully owned after buying the residual stake, repositioned as a unisex, on-trend Gen Z denim brand — it grew over 10% in Q4 FY26 with double-digit retail like-for-like and 70% online B2C growth, and gets a dedicated dotcom in H2 FY27.
- **Retail / EBO (exclusive brand outlets)** — The company's own branded stores — the single biggest channel at roughly INR 2,000 crores in FY25, growing via ~1.2–1.4 lakh sq ft of net new space a year, 50 stores added in Q4 FY26 alone, and like-for-like growth of about 8% in FY26 and 11.6% in Q1 FY27.
- **Online B2C (direct-to-consumer online)** — Sales through the company's own brand websites/apps plus omni and marketplace tie-ups, the fastest-growing channel at 38–40% growth, rising from 11% to 14% of revenue in FY26 and 18% by Q1 FY27, deliberately replacing lower-margin online B2B.
- **Wholesale / MBO / department stores (including online B2B)** — Selling to multi-brand outlets, department stores and online partners rather than end consumers — still delivering strong double-digit secondary sales growth, but shrinking as a share as management pivots toward direct channels (direct is now 62% of sales).
- **Adjacent categories** — Non-core-apparel lines — footwear, innerwear, accessories, watches, kidswear, small leather goods — that management now reports as about 24% of total business and a designated growth driver, growing around 20% in FY25.
- **Sephora (divested)** — A beauty retail business sold to Reliance Retail after Q2 FY24 at an enterprise value of INR 216 crores, which management called the completion of its portfolio rationalization, with proceeds used to retire debt and focus on the five remaining brands.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Arvind Fashions comes out of these three reviews as a genuinely improved business run by a management team the framework rates at its very best — the only area to score full marks. The record backs that up: promises made on early calls were kept on later ones, money from selling the Sephora business went to paying down debt (net debt from ₹476 crore to about ₹260 crore, with a further ₹75 crore cut in FY25), the guided 100 basis points of annual margin expansion was delivered, and return on capital went from slightly negative to 23%. It does this without heavy machinery: every ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.25 of sales, capex is roughly ₹100 crore against roughly ₹4,600 crore of revenue, and that spend goes into new selling space (1.2 lakh sq ft in FY25 rising to 1.4 lakh sq ft in FY26) rather than into keeping old assets alive. The single clearest pattern on the multibagger screen — selling directly to the shopper instead of through middlemen — is what ties this together: direct channels went from roughly half of sales to 56% in FY26 and 62% in the June 2027 quarter, with 65% the stated goal, and online consumer sales compounding 25-40% while lower-quality wholesale online business is deliberately shrunk. Owning the shelf is what lets the company cut discounts, push gross margin from 49.5% to 56.7%, and keep ₹54 of every ₹100 of sales after direct costs.

The uncomfortable part is that the very traits producing the good patterns are the same ones producing the elevated risks — the risk review scores lowest of the three, at 46 out of 100, and calls the company's resilience strained. First, brand spending: advertising above 4% of revenue for three straight years earns a top mark and underpins the Brand Strength pattern, but management itself concedes this spend must keep rising as a share of revenue to keep the brands top-of-mind. That is a permanent cost of staying in business, not an investment that finishes, and it sits directly on top of the fashion risk — two of the five brands needed rescuing inside the review window. Second, ownership: the long-held India rights to U.S. Polo Assn, Tommy Hilfiger and Calvin Klein are exactly what makes the business hard to copy, which is why the screen gives partial credit on cost to replicate — but the brands are licensed or held in a joint venture rather than owned, and that same fact makes stakeholder concentration an elevated risk. The moat and the dependency are the same asset. Third, the shift to owning stores and websites lifts margin and control, but it also means the company carries the inventory and the store estate; the framework flags a working capital cycle of about 129 days, money stuck between paying suppliers and getting paid by customers, as the one clear cost of the strategy.

What is missing is any built-in repeat revenue. The screen finds no fit at all on subscription-style income, no professional intermediary recommending the product, and no toll-booth position — every garment is a one-off discretionary purchase that has to be won again next season. That absence explains both the strongest and weakest numbers in the record: it is why brand spending can never be dialled back, and it is why growth has been lumpy despite averaging about 17% a year, the framework's single worst mark. Growth and industry structure are the two weakest quality areas, and the demand backdrop has swung from 'soft' and 'difficult' in FY24-25 to 'stable' in FY26, with a consumption slowdown flagged again for FY27 — so the recent acceleration from 8.5% to 14% to 15.5% is real but young. Management claims market share gains, but never against a named competitor with a number attached, so the screen can only rate that as a likely rather than proven fit — and the same goes for pricing power, where management itself describes price increases as selective and defensive against input cost inflation. Meanwhile digital-first labels with structurally lower costs are pressing on the online shelf where the company's fastest growth is happening. The combined rating of 64 out of 100 — a decent, three-star business — reads fairly: a well-run, improving, cash-generative company whose competitive protection is rented rather than owned and whose demand is cyclical.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Stakeholder Concentration, Fashion Risk. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ⚠️ Borrowings are now 1.4× shareholders' funds (up from 0.9×) — a leveraged balance sheet leaves little room when any of the risks above bites.
- ✅ About 74% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 3% to 17% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Discretionary apparel demand swings with the consumer cycle — the calls run from 'soft market condition' and 'difficult demand environment' in FY24-25 to 'demand environment remains stable' in FY26, with FY27 flagging consumption-slowdown risk from West Asia-driven inflation, cotton/forex pressure. But this is a flow product bought out of household operating spend, not customer capex, and AFL invested countercyclically through the downturn — 1.2-1.4 lakh sq ft added annually and marketing raised to 4%+ of sales while demand was weak — with margins rising every year regardless (EBITDA 12% to 13.4%, ROCE negative to 23%).

> "we do expect that there could be a potential risk of a consumption slowdown due to the inflationary pressures" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/repeat-purchase consumer demand; countercyclical store and marketing investment through the soft FY24-25 period with margins expanding ~100bps a year regardless*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±77 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between -0% and 14% (±4.3 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 2% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 4%).

### Stakeholder Concentration — ELEVATED
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but the only computed fingerprint does not confirm it — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** AFL's economics rest almost entirely on licensed/JV brands it does not own — U.S. Polo Assn (licensor USPA, whose Global CEO Michael Prince appears at AFL's own marketing events), Tommy Hilfiger and Calvin Klein (PVH JV, driving the minority interest line) and Arrow — and a single brand, USPA, is roughly half of group revenue by FY26 (~INR2,400-2,500 cr on ~INR5,000 cr), described as leading the pack every quarter. PVH's own global sourcing changes flowed straight into AFL's Q1 FY27 inventory commentary, and the Safilo pattern (licence loss to a stronger retail player) is the live tail risk; the only structural offset is the acquisition of the residual Flying Machine stake, one of the few brands they now fully control.

> "there was a change in PVH global sourcing in order to mitigate geopolitical risks" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Broadening of the revenue base — 5-brand portfolio, adjacencies at 24% of sales, and full ownership taken of Flying Machine*

- ✅ Customers pay in about 55 days (was 69) — no sign of weakening bargaining power in collection terms.

### Fashion Risk — ELEVATED
*"Cool today, uncool tomorrow"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** The entire price premium is brand desirability, and management concedes it must keep raising marketing as a share of revenue 'to keep our brand salient' — a permanent tax, not a one-off. Two of five brands needed rescue inside the window: Arrow went loss-making post-COVID and was still subdued on a 'onetime model change and a weak wedding calendar' in FY26, and Flying Machine required a wholesale repositioning to a 'unisex denim-anchored, on-trend youth brand' to recover.

> "We'll continue to invest strongly into marketing to keep the brand strong & salient" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Five-brand portfolio spanning formal to youth to super-premium rather than one bet, anchored in functional repeat-purchase categories — shirts, polos, denim, t-shirts, innerwear, footwear (24% adjacencies)*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±77 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ✅ Growth is holding up: 19% a year earlier, 15% recently — no structural stall.

### Government Dependency — WATCH
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Policy repeatedly moves the P&L at the margin without setting it: BIS certification held back footwear inventory and Stride store openings through FY25, a GST rate change on premium apparel knocked PVH brand growth for weeks in Q4 FY26, and management explicitly credits GST/income-tax/interest-rate measures for supporting demand. This is regulatory friction on a consumer business, not revenue set by a regulator or subsidy.

> "Retail growth was impacted by a transitory GST rate change impact on PVH brands for a few weeks" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: No tariff-, subsidy- or tender-driven revenue; demand is consumer-funded and the BIS/GST hits were transitory and absorbed within a quarter*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Analysts twice probe rising competition on online platforms, and management acknowledges 'the advent of certain B2C brands' — digital-first labels with structurally lower cost bases — plus a crowded marketplace field of 'a lot of labels'. The response is not discounting, however: discounts fell and gross margin rose to 56.7%, and AFL is building the new format itself rather than defending the old one.

### Shifting Consumer Preferences — WATCH
*"The customer is changing"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Two genuine migrations are reshaping the demand base — casualization away from formals (which is exactly where Arrow sits and where Arrow lags) and the shift of buying to online, where B2C has gone from a minor channel to 18% of sales growing 38-40%. AFL is on the right side of both rather than defending: it is pivoting the portfolio toward casual/denim/Gen Z and deliberately reallocating from online B2B to owned B2C.

> "The pivot towards casualization is also helping like-to-like growth of our brands" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Successful pivot toward the new preference — casualization aided LTL, D2C share taken to 62% with a stated 65% target, and each brand getting its own dotcom and app*

- ✅ Growth is holding up: 19% a year earlier, 15% recently — no structural stall.

### Good Enough Goods — WATCH
*"Cheaper rivals that are good enough"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Branded apparel sits directly in the path of private label and cheap online substitutes, and management is asked point-blank how it prices against the many labels on marketplaces. The affirmative defence is real but not absolute: gross margin rose 130bps in FY25 and a further 90bps to 56.7% in Q1 FY27 on lower discounting and higher full-price sell-through, and by selling 62% direct AFL removes the intermediary that decides for the customer — unlike the Nobel Biocare dentist. > "there has always been pressure and there has been always other brands on the online platform, and there are a lot of labels that sell over there" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Premium/global brand awareness sits with the end consumer, not an intermediary; D2C at 62% controls the shelf, and pricing power is demonstrated by falling discounts alongside rising gross margin*

- ✅ Operating margin (14%) remains near its historical peak (14%) — no collapse in the premium.

## What to watch

- Direct-to-shopper share climbing from 62% toward the stated 65% (Forward Integrators pattern) without the roughly 129-day working capital cycle getting longer — the Cost of Working Capital check is the price of that strategy
- Any change in the licence or joint-venture arrangements for U.S. Polo Assn, Tommy Hilfiger or Calvin Klein — the Stakeholder Concentration risk and the Cost to Replicate pattern rest on the same rented rights
- Advertising and promotion rising further as a share of revenue without like-for-like store growth responding — that would turn the top-scoring A&P check into the 'permanent tax' the Fashion Risk describes
- Gross margin holding near 56.7% and discounting staying down — a reversal would undercut the Pricing Power pattern and confirm the Good Enough Goods and New Entrants pressure from lower-cost online labels
- Growth staying near the recent 14-15.5% pace through a weaker consumer year — the Persistence of Growth check is the framework's worst mark and Cyclicality is rated elevated, so one soft year tests both at once
