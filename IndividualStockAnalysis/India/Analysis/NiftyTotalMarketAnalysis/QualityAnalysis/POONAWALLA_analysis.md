# Poonawalla Fin (POONAWALLA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Poonawalla Fincorp is an Indian non-bank lender (NBFC), listed on the BSE and NSE, that lends money to retail customers and small businesses across India. It runs roughly 12 lending products plus an instant consumer loan business — including loans against property, MSME/business loans, prime personal loans, gold loans, consumer durable (appliance EMI) loans, education loans, pre-owned car loans and commercial vehicle loans — sold through its own branch network (400+ dedicated gold branches operationalised by March 2026, 460 by June 2026, mostly in Tier 2/Tier 3 towns), tie-ups with 17,300+ retail outlets and channel partners, and increasingly through fully digital, straight-through journeys. It makes money on the spread between what it charges borrowers and what it pays for funds — new loans were being priced at around a 15.5% yield while borrowing cost fell to 7.65%, giving a net interest margin (including fees) of about 9.1% in Q1FY27 — plus fee and other income. Loans outstanding (AUM) grew from ₹30,984 crores in December 2024 to ₹67,054 crores by June 2026, with the book split roughly evenly between secured and unsecured (about 56% secured on-book), and quarterly profit of ₹308 crores at an ROA of 1.98%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Loans Against Property (LAP)** — Loans to individuals and businesses secured against their property; described as a core secured product at ₹6,795 crores of book in December 2024 (up 86% year-on-year, lent at a conservative 51% loan-to-value), and management groups it with gold, education and salary personal loans as products expected to make up 50–60% of the portfolio.
- **Business loans / MSME** — Unsecured-style lending to established small and mid-sized businesses with proven cash flows; ₹5,003 crores of book in December 2024, and management calls MSME a 'calibrated' business that lets it participate in the country's growth.
- **Prime Personal Loan (PL Prime)** — Personal loans to salaried professionals at large 'Category A' employers (70%+ with credit scores above 750 and take-home pay over ₹75,000), launched August 2024 and now one of the biggest new engines at about ₹537 crores of disbursement a month, with 38% of it fully digital, 24/7 straight-through processing.
- **Gold loans** — Small loans against pledged household gold, sold through a dedicated branch network built from scratch — 400 branches by March 2026 and 460 by June 2026, over 90% in Tier 2/Tier 3 towns — disbursing roughly ₹875 crores a quarter, with another ~400 branches planned in FY27 and the branches doubling as cross-sell hubs for other products.
- **Consumer durable loans (and the PFIN EMI card)** — Point-of-sale EMI financing for appliances and electronics through 17,300+ tied-up retail outlets in 339 locations, disbursing over 58,000 cases in a single month and ₹433 crores in Q1FY27; management calls it the 'anchor product' that brings middle-class households into the franchise and feeds data for underwriting, and aims to at least double its customer count in FY27.
- **Education loans** — Loans for студents' education, launched about a year before July 2026 and scaling fast — 55% quarter-on-quarter disbursement growth to about ₹144 crores a month, sold via 50 locations and a network of 600+ education consultants, with ~30% of sanctions done digitally.
- **Commercial vehicle loans** — Financing of trucks and last-mile logistics vehicles, running at roughly ₹104–125 crores of disbursement a month through 1,100+ channel partners across 70-plus locations in 30 states; a smaller but steadily growing line.
- **Pre-owned / used car loans** — Loans against used vehicles, an older product the new management deliberately re-shaped towards lower-risk profiles two quarters before early 2025 and expected to grow around 20%; grouped with MSME as a 'calibrated' business.
- **Short-term / instant consumer loans (STPL)** — Small, quick, unsecured consumer loans, deliberately shrunk and re-underwritten in late 2024 before being stepped back up; management treats it as the one product that by design carries a structurally higher credit cost, with the other ~12 products described as more granular and lower risk.
- **Six new business lines (as a group)** — The bundle of products launched from FY25-26 onward as part of the plan to go from about 4 products to around 10-13; they contributed 20% of disbursements in Q3FY26, 24% in Q4FY26 and 26% in Q1FY27, at a monthly run rate of roughly ₹950 crores, and are the main driver of the company's growth and margin improvement.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Poonawalla Fin comes out as a genuine share gainer with a real operating turnaround behind it. Its loan book grew from ₹30,984 crore in December 2024 to ₹67,054 crore by June 2026 — 69% to 78% a year against industry retail credit growth of around 16% — and the growth was not bought by loosening lending standards, with bad loans falling from 1.84% to 1.37%, the cleanest loan bucket rising to 97.6% and early delinquencies down to 0.64%. That is why 'Market Share Gainers' is the one pattern rated a strong fit, and it is supported across the quality checks: gain in market share, growth spending, convenience for customers, structural end-market growth and management follow-through all score at the top of the scale. Management earns particular credit here — the February 2025 promise of six new products, 400 gold branches and a full leadership bench was delivered on schedule, and the margin guidance was met a quarter early. Margins, funding costs and operating costs all moved the right way at the same time: net interest margin from 8.40% to 9.10%, cost of borrowing from 8.04% to 7.65% as bonds rose from 7% to 33% of borrowings, and operating cost against loan book from 4.76% to 4.06%, helped by measurable AI savings of 15% on pre-due and 27% on post-due collections.

The catch is that almost everything driving the growth is also what stops it from becoming a durable advantage, and the two show up as the same fact wearing two hats. The company built six lending businesses from scratch in roughly eighteen months by hiring proven leaders two levels deep and buying off-the-shelf software and AI tools. That capability is exactly what powered the share gain — but it is also the direct evidence behind the 'Cost to Replicate' pattern being marked no fit and the barriers-to-entry check scoring negative: if this company could assemble a lender that quickly, so can the next well-funded entrant. The same duality runs through distribution. The 1,100 commercial-vehicle partners, 600-plus education consultants, 17,300 dealer outlets and 38 digital partners give it reach and speed, and they are why 'Friendly Middleman' registers at all — but because those intermediaries steer the borrower and are sold on turnaround time rather than loyalty, they are also the core of the 'Good Enough Goods' risk flagged as watch. Lending here is close to a commodity, which is why the intangible-benefits check scores negative and 'Brand Strength' and 'Pricing Power' land only as partial fits; the improving yields come from choosing better products and customer segments, not from charging existing borrowers more. This is the arithmetic behind the rating of 52 out of 100, or Decent: business quality scores a middling 56 and risk safety a high 88, but multibagger pattern fit scores only 15, so a strong operator sits inside a weak structure.

The risk review is mostly reassuring and points back to the same source. Five of eight channels are low risk — technology, stakeholder concentration, shifting consumer preferences and fashion risk — precisely because the breadth that undermines the moat also removes single points of failure: products went from about four to thirteen, channel partners and consultants were broadened repeatedly, funding sources were diversified, and on the one preference shift visible in the record, borrowers moving to instant digital credit, the company is on the winning side with straight-through processing rising from 28% to 38%. The three watch items are structural rather than company-specific. Cyclicality is the big one, and it is the direct shadow of the growth story: a loan book that grew 77.6% and 69.4% year on year is young and unseasoned, so its true loss behaviour has not yet been tested through a downturn — the counterweight being deliberate spread across gold, loan-against-property, education and salaried personal loans with gold described as counter-cyclical. Regulatory dependence is second-order but real, since provisioning rules feed straight into credit costs even though pricing itself is market-set. Underlying all of it is the return-on-capital area scoring the worst of any category at -1.0: this is a balance-sheet-heavy lender earning a spread on ₹67,054 crore of borrowed money at a return on assets of just 1.98% even after the turnaround, with no protection around those assets. Overall resilience is recorded as unknown, which fits a business that has proved it can execute but has not yet proved it can absorb a bad cycle.

## What to watch

- Credit quality as the book seasons — the Cyclicality risk rests on an unseasoned loan book growing 69-78% a year; if bad loans reverse from 1.37% or early delinquencies rise from 0.64%, the 'Market Share Gainers' strong fit weakens immediately.
- Whether yields hold without a moat — the 'Good Enough Goods' watch and the negative intangible-benefits check mean the ~40-50bps yield gains and 9.10% margin must survive competition; a reversal would confirm pricing is set by rivals, not by the company.
- Return on assets trend — the return-on-capital area scores -1.0 with ROA at 1.98%; whether the rising trend from 0.78% in Q4FY25 continues is the single test of whether the growth spending check (+2) is actually creating value.
- Delivery on the second gold branch wave — management's credibility under the 'Corporate Culture' pattern and the independent-and-tenacious check rests on kept promises; the ~400 branches planned for FY27, after 460 built, is the next verifiable commitment.
- New entrants in the same products — the 'Cost to Replicate' no-fit and negative barriers-to-entry check imply rivals can copy the build; visible new competition in gold, education or prime personal loans would compress the share gain that the whole case depends on.
