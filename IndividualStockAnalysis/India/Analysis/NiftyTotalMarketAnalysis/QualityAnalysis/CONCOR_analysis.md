# Container Corporation of India Ltd. (CONCOR) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: SERVICES*

CONCOR (Container Corporation of India Ltd.) is a government-owned ("Navratna CPSE") multi-modal logistics company that moves freight packed in containers around India, mainly by rail. It buys haulage on the rail network and runs its own fleet of trains and containers — 423 high-speed rakes and about 57,746 owned containers as of FY26 — between seaports such as JNPT/Nhava Sheva, Mundra, Pipavav, Kandla, Chennai and Vishakhapatnam and its own network of inland terminals/ICDs (Dadri, Khatuwas, Nagpur, Raipur, Sonipat, Hyderabad and others), 26 of which sit on Indian Railways land for which it pays a land license fee of roughly Rs. 450 crores a year. Its customers are shipping lines (long-term volume-discount deals with the likes of Maersk and PSA), exporters and importers of goods like garments, rice, meat, auto parts, solar panel parts and polymers, and domestic shippers of cement, tiles and gunny bales. It earns money on rail freight plus terminal handling, warehousing and road pickup/delivery, and its volumes are measured in TEUs — an all-time-high 5.58 million TEUs in FY26, up 9.6% — with profitability driven by rail freight margin (27.16% in FY26), running double-stack trains, and cutting empty container/rake running.

**The parts of the business:**

- **EXIM** — Carries import and export containers between seaports and inland terminals for shipping lines and traders; it is by far the bigger half — 4.21 million of the 5.58 million TEUs handled in FY26, and the first time revenue from this stream crossed Rs. 6,000 crores.
- **Domestic** — Moves containerized cargo within India (cement, tiles, gunny bales, food and other goods, roughly 14-15 million tonnes of loading a year), growing faster in percentage terms — 14.6% in FY26 — but a much smaller share of total volumes than EXIM.
- **Terminals / ICDs (Projects & Services)** — Owns and operates the inland container depots and terminals where containers are handled, stored and transferred between rail and road, including 26 built on Indian Railways land; new terminals and rakes are funded from an annual capex budget of about Rs. 945-1,085 crores.
- **Double-stack and high-speed rake operations** — Runs trains with containers stacked two high on routes like Khatuwas and NCR to Mundra, Pipavav and (from June 2026, over the Western Dedicated Freight Corridor) JNPT — management calls this a main driver of both faster transit times and better margins, with 6,396 double-stack trains run in FY26.
- **Tank containers / bulk cement (new domestic product)** — A newly launched service hauling bulk cement and liquids in tank containers; still small — a fleet of about 500 tanks growing by 200 a month, with 3,000 more approved — and management targets at least 1 million tonnes of loading from it.
- **Warehousing and first-mile/last-mile (FMLM) road services** — Road pickup and delivery plus warehousing bolted onto the rail leg so customers get an end-to-end "total logistics solution"; described as a focus area rather than quantified, and supported by assets like 230 LNG trucks and electric handling equipment.
- **Direct Port Delivery (DPD) and cabotage services** — Schemes that let containers move straight between the port and the customer under liberalized rules; a liberalized policy lifted DPD volumes 38% in FY26 and is flagged as a continuing growth driver.
- **Bharat Container Shipping Line (30% stake)** — A shipping-line joint venture CONCOR signed an MOU for and holds 30% of, under which it has begun issuing its own bills of lading — about 700 containers moved to the Middle East before conflict halted the service.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

CONCOR's whole story starts with one asset: a national rail-logistics network that no rival can match. The quality framework gives its distribution reach the highest score in the whole review — 423 high-speed rakes, roughly 57,746 owned containers and hub terminals at Khatuwas and Dadri, reach so complete that CONCOR was asked to clear JNPT's congestion by rail. That same footprint is what makes the 'Cost to Replicate' pattern a partial fit: rakes, containers, double-stack-capable terminals on the dedicated freight corridor and over Rs.1,000 crore of capex a year are genuinely slow and expensive for anyone to copy. The financial housekeeping around it is tidy — about 20 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, roughly Rs.21 of operating profit on every Rs.100 of sales with unusually steady margins, no debt and no plans to raise any, and a dividend that has climbed from 105% of par in the first half of FY25 to 172% for FY26. Capital spending is clearly aimed at growth rather than repair — new terminals at Mandalgarh, Kadakola, Jajpur, Salawas and Chharodi, new port links at Kandla and Paradip, and Nepal points at Birgunj, Raxaul and Biratnagar — with the FY26 budget lifted 23% mid-year to Rs.1,060 crore and Rs.1,085 crore actually spent. This financial sturdiness is why the review still calls the business sound even while rating it Mixed, and it is why the trade cycle registers only as something to watch: revenue comes from goods that keep moving, not from customers' own investment plans, and management spends counter-cyclically.

The catch is that almost every strength has a matching weakness attached to the same fact. The terminals that form the moat sit on Indian Railways land licensed at 6% of notified value with a compulsory 7% annual increase — around Rs.450 crore a year — and the same counterparty owns the track, sets the haulage price, grants axle-load permissions and controls project timelines for essentially the entire core business. So the landlord is also the supplier, the price-setter and the regulator, which is exactly why government dependency is rated high risk and reliance on a single supplier is rated elevated, with management repeatedly pointing timing questions back at that counterparty. The scale advantage cuts both ways too: real cost savings from double-stacking, cutting empty running by 10.5% and shrinking headcount through digitalisation make 'Low Price Plus' a partial fit — but those savings are deliberately handed back in lower tariffs to win traffic off the roads, which is precisely why pricing power is judged absent. The evidence is blunt: rebate and free-empty schemes in November 2022, a deliberate rate cut in November 2024 to win back domestic volume, volume discounts to shipping lines, and FY26 revenue growing only 2.2% while volumes grew 9.6%. Because the service itself is largely undifferentiated, cheaper private operators and road hauliers keep taking business on price — the 'good enough goods' risk — and management would rather forgo low-margin work than match rates.

The result is a share story that shows up in four places at once as a single fact: total share slipped from 56.05% to 54.35% over nine months of FY26 and from around 75% fifteen years ago, with Mundra down 232 basis points. That one trend explains the negative mark on market-share gain, the failure of both the 'Market Share Gainers' and 'Brand Strength' patterns, and the elevated new-entrant risk, since private container train operators have permanently reset the economics and are building their own facilities inside CONCOR's catchment. The company's answer is to move closer to the end customer — first- and last-mile road services, warehousing, end-to-end Delhi–Kolkata trains and, by FY26, issuing its own bills of lading — plus a steady cadence of new services such as Retail Express and Aushadhi Express with Maersk, bulk cement in tank containers and higher-capacity rakes. Yet volumes are still largely routed by shipping lines and forwarders rather than by the cargo owner, which is both a limit on the 'Friendly Middleman' pattern and, read the other way, the reason customer concentration is not a worry: three-to-five-year tie-ups with Maersk, MSC, CMA-CGM, Hapag-Lloyd and a PSA agreement spread the base wide. Management deserves credit for candour — it openly owns the share loss, the missed 20% domestic growth guidance, the tax settlement charge and the depreciation distortion — but capital allocation is the weak link: the only inorganic move is a 30% stake in a government-vision shipping line, capital-hungry and unrelated, even though it doubles as one of the few ways to loosen the grip of the railway landlord. Add a company that is the opposite of undiscovered — a listed Navratna covered by Goldman, JPMorgan, Citi and BNP with a politically visible profile — and the picture is a solid, cash-generating, well-run infrastructure business scoring only 10 out of 100 on the multibagger patterns, which is how a sound company lands at 43 out of 100 and a Mixed, two-star rating.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration, New Entrants. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.1× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 78% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital moderated from about 16% to 13% — lower, but still well clear of the deep slide this check looks for.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** CONCOR is a Navratna CPSE whose landlord, sole track owner, tariff-setter and infrastructure timeline-owner is the government: land licence fee is fixed by railway policy at 6% of notified land value with a mandatory 7% annual escalation (Rs.450 crore/year, swinging PAT via reversals/corrections), haulage charges and 'reforms for container sector' are set by Indian Railways, and the single biggest growth driver across all 14 calls — DFC connectivity to JNPT — slipped from March 2025 to Dec 2025 to June 2026 entirely outside management's control.

> "the land license fees for the 26 terminals which we are operating on Indian Railways land is 6% of the market value of the land" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Partial self-help only — surrendering railway terminals for own logistics parks to save LLF, and diversification into own shipping line (Bharat Container Shipping Line) and FMLM/warehousing*

### Stakeholder Concentration — ELEVATED
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but the only computed fingerprint does not confirm it — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** One supplier — Indian Railways/DFCCIL — controls the track, the haulage price, the axle-load permissions, the land and the project timelines for essentially 100% of the core business; management repeatedly deflects timing questions to that counterparty ('ask the dedicated freight corridor management'), and the Q3 FY26 PAT was hit simply because last year's LLF reversals normalised.

> "LLF also in this quarter, LLF is showing increase of around 23% ... which is the actual LLF we will be paying to railways" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Customer side is broad and broadening — 5-year tie-ups with Maersk, MSC, CMA-CGM, Hapag-Lloyd, PSA MOU, plus multi-commodity domestic base (cement, ethane/propane, food grains, Nepal traffic)*

- ✅ Customers pay in about 17 days (was 4) — no sign of weakening bargaining power in collection terms.

## What to watch

- Market share: whether the 54.35% total share stabilises or keeps sliding (the 'Gain in market share' check and the failed 'Market Share Gainers' pattern), with Mundra's 232 basis point drop the near-term tell.
- Price versus volume: the FY26 gap between 2.2% revenue growth and 9.6% volume growth narrowing would start to contradict the 'Pricing Power' no-fit verdict; widening confirms it.
