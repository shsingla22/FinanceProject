# Avalon Technologies (AVALON) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Avalon Technologies is an Indian electronics manufacturing services (EMS) company — it does not sell its own branded products, but builds electronic and engineered products under contract for other companies. Its pitch is "high-mix, flexible-volume" work: complex, mission-critical, low-to-medium-volume products with heavy engineering content, rather than cheap mass-market goods. Because it is vertically integrated (PCB design, circuit-board assembly, cables, sheet metal, magnetics, plastics, testing and logistics all in-house), it can deliver a finished "box build" product rather than just a component — box build was 56% of revenue in Q4 FY26, up from 44% four years earlier. It runs 13+ plants in India and the United States, serving customers in rail, aerospace, industrial, clean energy, medical and communications; FY26 revenue was INR1,603 crores (up 46%), with 62% of revenue from US customers and 38% from India, while 77% of manufacturing is done in India.

**The parts of the business:**

- **India manufacturing operations** — The core plants (largely Chennai/Bengaluru) that build for both Indian and global customers — about 77-78% of total revenue and where nearly all the profit sits, at roughly 16.7% EBITDA margin and 12% PAT margin in FY26.
- **US manufacturing operations** — Plants in the United States serving American customers, about 22-23% of revenue; it has been loss-making (a INR14.2 crore loss in Q1 FY25, narrowed to about INR5 crores in Q4 FY26), and management has shifted 45-50% of that production to India while targeting breakeven in late FY27.
- **Box build (vertically integrated end-product assembly)** — The flagship offering — taking a customer's product from PCB design through to a fully assembled, tested and shipped finished unit; management calls this its main differentiator and it has grown from 44% of revenue four years ago to 56% in Q4 FY26.
- **PCB design and assembly** — Design and analysis of printed circuit boards (one of India's largest such teams, based in Bengaluru) plus manufacture of complex multi-layer board assemblies for mission-critical uses like railway signalling, vehicle tracking and medical equipment.
- **Cable assemblies and wire harnesses** — Complex cabling and harness manufacturing feeding into box builds; management says it is on the cusp of winning cable work with a major aerospace customer, an area of growing interest.
- **Sheet metal and precision machining** — Precision-engineered metal parts and enclosures for mission-critical applications, carrying NADCAP approval for fabrication and welding — the base for newer bids in aerospace cockpit assemblies and landing gear components.
- **Magnetics** — Custom-designed transformers of various types, made largely for clean energy and similar products.
- **Plastics** — A small but growing injection-moulding division that supplies plastic parts into the integrated box-build offering.
- **Industrial vertical** — The largest end-market at roughly 34-35% of FY26 revenue, growing about 65% year-on-year, covering established industrial equipment makers plus newer wins like semiconductor manufacturing equipment and power systems.
- **Mobility (rail and aerospace)** — About 28% of FY26 revenue growing ~50%, split between rail at 16% (signalling, locomotive engine subsystems, the Kavach anti-collision system) and aerospace at 9% (cabin subassemblies, work for a large aerospace conglomerate including a factory transfer to India).
- **Clean energy** — Around 20% of FY26 revenue growing ~45%, covering solar, hydrogen, EV and especially energy storage systems — including a US customer's home electrification/storage-and-grid product now ramping in production.
- **Communications** — About 8% of FY26 revenue growing ~58%, including control units for satellite antenna systems where prototypes are done and volume orders are expected from FY27.
- **Order book and long-term contracts** — Firm purchase orders stood at INR2,196 crores as of March 2026 (up ~25%, executed over about 14 months on average), plus a separate INR1,245 crores of longer-term contracts running 15-36 months — the visibility behind management's guidance of 24-27% growth in FY27 and roughly INR3,200 crores of revenue by FY29.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Avalon builds complex, mission-critical electronic assemblies — railway signalling, aerospace, medical, industrial and clean-energy equipment — and the quality work scores it well on the things that matter most in that business. It grew 46% in FY26 while the wider market grew far more slowly, it earns about ₹19 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital employed, it keeps roughly ₹11 of operating profit out of every ₹100 of sales with very little year-to-year drift, and it does all this without much machinery: every ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.27 of sales, capex runs about ₹50 crore a year, and net debt to equity is just 0.06. Management scores at the top of the sheet on candour, discipline and patience — it pre-announced the US downturn before it arrived, disclosed the loss in exact rupee terms during the worst quarter, grew entirely without acquisitions, and delivered a promised three-year revenue doubling a year early. That track record is the one place the multibagger screen gives an unqualified yes: Corporate Culture is the single STRONG FIT out of eleven patterns, and it is essentially a scorecard of promises kept.

Beyond culture, the screen is mostly half-marks, and the reason is that the same traits that create an advantage also cap it. Certification is the clearest example: NADCAP approval for sheet metal and welding, RDSO clearance for the Kavach rail programme, and a six-to-twelve month qualification that means a programme 'once it comes into production, normally stays' — that is real lock-in, and it is why Toll Roads and Cost to Replicate both register as PARTIAL rather than absent, and why the cheaper-competitor risk is rated LOW with gross margin sitting in a 33-35% band across all fifteen calls. But the assembly is a large part of the customer's cost rather than a small one, and contract manufacturing capacity itself is not scarce, so the lock-in stops short of a true toll gate. Pricing power reads the same way: customers absorbed 99% of a tariff shock with no volume loss, which proves they are buying certainty rather than the lowest price, yet across fifteen calls there is no instance of pushing price above cost inflation. Five patterns get no fit at all — the 'recurring revenue' management refers to is repeat production orders on long-life programmes, not subscriptions or renewals, and the business is working-capital heavy rather than customer-funded, with about 160 days of cash stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. That single trait is the worst-scoring check in the whole framework and the direct reason the Recurring Revenue pattern fails, and it sits alongside a Capital Allocation area score of zero.

The risk review turns those same facts around and shows their downside. Four of the eight channels are on watch, and resilience overall is judged STRESSED. Concentrated growth from a handful of named accounts is what produced the market-share gains, and it is also what produced the FY25 collapse: revenue fell 15.2% in one quarter, operating margin went from 15.1% to 2.2%, profit turned negative, and the cause was a few large US customers running down inventory, including a medical customer down 80%. So Stakeholder Concentration and Cyclicality are two readings of one dependence. Policy cuts both ways too — tariffs that were passed through successfully swung from 50% to 18%, RDSO approval gates the rail programme that is 16% of revenue, and a government allotment decides the C-DAC server work where the company is 'one out of three' — meaning the qualification barrier that keeps rivals out is partly written by regulators. Even the capital discipline has a mirror image: refusing subsidised greenfield component manufacturing protects returns and keeps the balance sheet clean, but it means new subsidy-funded entrants are building the capacity Avalon is choosing not to. The offsets are genuine — a capex-light model that survived the downturn, ₹1,245 crore of long-term contracts running 15 to 36 months, a ₹2,196 crore order book, India now 38% of revenue, and deliberate expansion into Southeast Asia and Europe. Netted out, the rating is 56 out of 100, or Decent: strong business quality at 72, solid risk safety at 64, and a low 25 on multibagger pattern fit, which is the honest bottleneck.

## Section 2 — Does it look like a long-term winner? (11 multibagger patterns)

*"A culture that compounds"*

**What the calls show:** The 15-call record shows promises made and then delivered: the FY23 pledge to double revenue in three years was hit a year early, working capital was guided to improve 10-15 days and came in at 112 vs a 120-130 guide, and guidance was raised then beaten in FY26 — while bad news (US destocking, Q1 FY25 PAT loss, US manufacturing losses) was flagged in advance and tracked openly, with a conservative balance sheet (net debt/equity 0.06) and ROCE as the stated yardstick (10% to 20.6%).

*"Small cost, can't skip it"*

> "we were able to recover 99% of the applicable tariffs from our customers" — *management, on an earnings call*

## What to watch

- Whether the 160-day working-capital cycle actually shortens — the 112 days delivered against a 120-130 day guide was a promise kept, but the Cost of Working Capital check remains the framework's worst score and the reason Recurring Revenue is NO FIT.
- Customer concentration: watch whether the three new US industrial and defence accounts plus India at 38% of revenue genuinely broaden the base, or whether results still hinge on a few named customers as they did in the FY25 destocking quarter (Stakeholder Concentration, WATCH).
- Any repeat of a stocking-cycle downturn — a second episode of revenue falling and margin dropping toward the 2.2% low would confirm the Cyclicality risk is structural rather than a one-off, and would test whether the capex-light model really is the shock absorber management describes.
- Evidence of price being raised above cost inflation, not just cost being passed through — that is the specific gap keeping Pricing Power at PARTIAL despite the 99% tariff pass-through and a gross margin held at the top of the 33-35% band.
- Policy moves on either side: tariff rates, RDSO clearance timing for Kavach, and the C-DAC allotment where Avalon is one of three — each can swing results without any change in operating performance (Government Dependency, WATCH).
- Whether subsidy-funded entrants under schemes like ECMS start showing up as pricing pressure — Avalon currently reports none, and its refusal to chase greenfield capacity is scored as discipline; that reading changes if margins in complex box build begin to slip (New Entrants, WATCH).
