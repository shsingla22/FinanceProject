**Checks that improved:**

- **Pricing, Mix and Volume** (Growth): Poor → Weak. Now: Growth is capacity/volume-led while pricing softened (cells 13-13.5c/W, modules 22-22.5c/W) and mix shifted toward structurally lower-margin modules, compressing EBITDA margin quarter by quarter. | The long view had said: Growth is purely volume/capacity-led while both pricing and mix worked against the company — cell realisation fell from ~14 to 13-13.5 cents and the mix shifted toward structurally lower-margin modules.
- **Oligopoly (vs duopoly)** (Industry Structure): Weak → Neutral. Now: ALMM/DCR rules create a temporarily narrow field of domestic cell suppliers with strong pricing, but management itself points to a large pipeline of announced capacity that will erode that structure. | The long view had said: Far from a stable oligopoly — a wave of new domestic cell capacity is being announced, and customers (module manufacturers and pump players) retain pricing power, as the realisation decline shows.
- **Geographical expansion** (Growth): Weak → Neutral. Now: No geographic market expansion — the business is deliberately confined to the Indian DCR market, and the Andhra Pradesh project is a domestic plant location, not a new market. | The long view had said: Zero export exposure and no credible near-term international plan; Europe is only aspirational talk and the US is written off on tariffs.
- **Security by obscurity** (Industry Structure): Weak → Neutral. Now: A small-cap that stayed quiet for decades and only just began investor calls, but the solar manufacturing niche is now firmly on institutional radar, with Kotak, DAM Capital and Singularity on the line. | The long view had said: Indian solar manufacturing is one of the most capital- and attention-saturated sectors in the market, the opposite of an ignored niche, even if Websol itself is a smaller name.

**Checks that regressed:**

- **People matter (talent development)** (Management): Good → Neutral. Now: There is a 30-year CTO anchoring technology and a stated phased succession plan with a dedicated execution team, but the pipeline is thin and personality-dependent, with the MD joking about staying another 50 years. | The long view had said: There is a structured in-house training and succession pipeline — a dedicated project team under the CTO, cross-training junior staff for existing lines, sending engineers to equipment suppliers, and PhD recruitment — though succession at the MD level remains loosely defined.
- **Assurance benefits** (Customer Benefits): Good → Neutral. Now: Thirty years of operating experience and demonstrated ramp-up capability offer some reliability value to buyers, but nothing in the calls shows customers paying a premium for that assurance. | The long view had said: Buyers do pay for reliability of supply and efficiency — ALMM-listed, 23.4-23.6% efficiency, low breakage and repeat orders from module makers — a modest trust-based benefit in an otherwise commoditised product.
- **Disciplined stewards** (Management): Excellent → Good. Now: Phase-2 was self-funded, the company turned net cash with debt/equity down to 0.19x, growth is wholly organic and the AP project was deliberately slowed for better sequencing — offset by over 80% of promoter shares still pledged to IREDA. | The long view had said: Phase-2 was self-funded, the company turned net cash surplus with D/E falling to 0.19x, no M&A has been attempted, and management deliberately slowed the AP announcement to get sequencing and funding right.
- **Technology advantage** (Competitive Advantage): Good → Neutral. Now: Execution on standard industry technology is competent — 23.6% peak PERC efficiency, TOPCon upgrade targeting >24.5% — but the technology is bought-in equipment (Linton MOU for ingot/wafer) and the upgrade merely keeps pace with peers already on TOPCon. | The long view had said: There is a genuine operating-technology edge — 23.6% peak PERC efficiency, industry-low breakage, 25% silver reduction, a TOPCon upgrade targeting 24.5%+, and space reserved for back-contact — but it rests on bought-in equipment and a single product line.
- **Partial monopoly / broken competition / switching costs** (Industry Structure): Neutral → Weak. Now: Customers are module manufacturers and solar-pump players buying a fungible product with no switching costs or captive aftermarket; Websol does not even hold the end-customer relationship. | The long view had said: ALMM/DCR rules create a regulatory ring-fence that excludes Chinese cells and confers a temporary partial protection, but it is policy-granted and open to every domestic entrant, with no customer switching costs.

### Bucket 2 — Multibagger patterns (11 patterns)

The long view matches 2 of the 11 winning patterns; the one-year view matches 2. Genuinely re-tested pattern verdicts: 0 strengthened, 3 weakened, 8 unchanged.

*Foundation test: UNKNOWN on the long view, UNKNOWN on the one-year view.*

**That weakened:**

- **Toll Roads**: PARTIAL → NO FIT. Why now: Cells and modules are the dominant cost element of the customer's system, not a sub-1% critical input, and the regulatory anchoring (ALMM List 2, DCR mandate) is a policy shelter Websol benefits from rather than a standard it owns; management concedes customers deferred offtake when prices fell. (The long view had said: There is genuine regulatory/standards anchoring — ALMM List 1/2 approvals and the DCR mandate protect the niche, and management leans on it for demand visibility — but the core test fails: a solar cell is the dominant cost component of a module, not 1-2%, and customers negotiate on cents-per-watt.)
- **Low Price Plus**: PARTIAL → NO FIT. Why now: The FY26 story is the opposite of low-price-high-turns: 41% EBITDA margin, 28.6% PAT margin and an explicit claim to run the highest margins in the industry, with no itemised cost-culture or savings-passed-to-price language on either call. (The long view had said: Real cost discipline is visible — 25% cut in silver consumption, upgrading the PERC line rather than scrapping it, Cell Line-2 built entirely from internal accruals — but the company positions on the highest margins in the industry, not on low price passed to customers.)
- **Forward Integrators**: PARTIAL → NO FIT. Why now: The stated integration strategy runs backward toward ingots and wafers, not forward to the customer; there are no own stores, franchising, D2C or direct end-customer channels mentioned, and management explicitly says it does not tender directly for end orders. (The long view had said: Websol is moving down the chain from cells into modules sold to end customers, and future capacity is planned as integrated cell+module, but this is manufacturing integration only — no own stores, franchising, D2C or channel ownership.)

## How this comparison was built

Both sides ran the same three analysis engines (BusinessAnalysis, MultibaggerPattern, QualityRisks) through their respective orchestrators:

- **Long view (full AnalystSkill):** statements over ~a decade, the whole call timeline — engines ran with_calls.
- **Last one year (RecentAnalystSkill):** latest fiscal year (prior year as baseline only), last 12 months of calls — engines ran 0 items carried from the full-history analysis, with_calls.

Comparison rules: only verdicts assessed on both views are counted; one-year-lens silences are listed but never scored as regressions; every movement line carries both sides' evidence so the WHY of the change is visible, not just the direction. Research tooling; not investment advice.
