# Ellenbarrie Indl.Gas (ELLEN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Ellenbarrie Industrial Gases makes and sells basic industrial gases — mainly oxygen, nitrogen and argon separated out of air, plus hydrogen and medical gases — to Indian industrial and healthcare customers, with steel and metals the biggest end market and chemicals, pharma, healthcare, solar and electronics described as growing ones. It earns money three ways: piping gas directly to a single large customer from a plant built at that customer's site under long-term take-or-pay contracts (so revenue is contracted from day one), producing liquid gas at its own 'merchant' plants and trucking it to many buyers in the surrounding region, and filling and distributing cylinders locally. The company owns and runs the plants, so it is capital-heavy — power is its single largest cost — and profitability turns on plant utilisation, power cost and the price of argon. It has historically operated in eastern and southern India (plants at Uluberia in West Bengal and Kurnool, a cylinder-filling operation in Bangalore), holds mid-single-digit share of an industry it sizes at about Rs 15,000 crore, and is building out to north India and west-central India to become pan-India; capacity was 1,370 tonnes per day at end-FY25, planned at 1,910 TPD by end-FY26 and 2,130 TPD in FY27.

**The parts of the business:**

- **On-site gases** — Plants built at a large customer's own premises supplying gas under take-or-pay contracts — management says capacity utilisation is 'irrelevant' here because revenue is guaranteed from day one, and these carry a higher EBITDA margin; examples include the Tata Steel Metaliks plant already running and a 320 TPD East India on-site plant due around Q1 FY27.
- **Merchant gases (bulk liquid)** — Company-owned plants producing liquid oxygen, nitrogen and argon sold to many customers across a region; each new merchant plant takes roughly 18–24 months to ramp to about 85% utilisation, and recent examples are the Kurnool plant (60–65% loaded in Q1 FY26) and the 220 TPD Uluberia 2 plant in West Bengal commissioned in Q4 FY26 at about Rs 160 crore capex with roughly Rs 100–110 crore of revenue potential.
- **Packaged / cylinder gases** — Local cylinder filling and secondary distribution so customers who want gas in cylinders rather than bulk liquid can be served — the small Rs 5.5 crore acquisition of cylinder-filling assets in Bangalore is the example given, valued mainly because it adds value to Kurnool's output and lets the company serve nearby medical-oxygen customers who need a supplier close at hand.
- **Argon** — The highest value-added product, a by-product of oxygen production sold from the same plants; it rose from about 7% of revenue a year earlier to roughly 9–10% and management targets 15%-plus, and its price swings matter a lot — a more than 25% drop in Q3 FY26, blamed on weak steel and oversupply from steel makers' captive plants, pushed group EBITDA margin from 38% down to 31% that quarter before recovering in Q4.
- **High-purity and speciality gases (planned)** — A future product capability to be built alongside the planned west-India plant, targeted for FY28, aimed at customers such as solar cell manufacturers; management says it is still in discussions with no signed contracts to disclose.

### The story in depth

Ellenbarrie sells industrial gases into what the records describe as a roughly ₹15,000 crore Indian market growing about 10% a year, with new demand pools opening in solar, electronics, pharma and healthcare. The strongest scores in the quality framework cluster around the customer and the people running the business: the entire proposition is reliability and certainty of supply — most starkly in medical oxygen, where proximity and dependability rather than price decide the sale — sold to an entirely corporate and industrial customer base on long-dated take-or-pay contracts and in-plant pipelines. Management scores well on candour (a weak quarter volunteered without spin, each one-off itemised with amounts, a project delay owned) and on stewardship: ₹210 crore of borrowings repaid immediately after the IPO, leaving net cash of ₹3,550 million, growth entirely organic and greenfield, no large acquisitions. Those same traits produce the strongest pattern reading in the screen — Toll Roads — where 15-year on-site contracts sitting inside customer premises and take-or-pay economics kept revenue steady even when steel customers cut how much gas they actually took. The combined rating lands at 56 out of 100, a 'Decent' three-star verdict, built from 67 on business quality, only 34 on multibagger fit, and 64 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound.

The uncomfortable part of the story is that the very structure creating the toll-road quality is also what drags the returns. This is an explicitly asset-heavy model: every ₹1 of assets produces ₹0.26 of sales, and management's own arithmetic is about ₹160 crore of capex for a 220 tonne-per-day plant yielding roughly ₹100–110 crore of revenue at full run rate. That is why Return on Capital is the one negative quality area (-0.67) while Customer Benefits sits at 1.5. The heavy asset base and the physical embedding of plants in customer premises are simultaneously the barrier that makes the business hard to replicate — long equipment lead times, greenfield-only expansion because existing plants cannot be debottlenecked, a 500km delivery radius that makes local density defensible — and the reason capital works hard for a modest sales return. The same duality runs through the contract book: roughly 700 of about 1,600 tonnes per day sits in on-site plants dedicated to single named customers such as Tata Steel Metaliks, which is what makes the annuity revenue real and what makes stakeholder concentration a watch-level risk, illustrated by a ₹15 million one-time settlement with an on-site customer over a plant start-up date that hit the fourth quarter of FY26. The ongoing capex programme — around ₹450 crore, taking capacity from 1,370 to 1,910 to 2,130 tonnes per day across three new plants, funded from a net-cash balance sheet at segment margins of 38–40% — is scored as genuine growth spending rather than maintenance, but it deepens the asset intensity rather than relieving it.

Where the story is weakest is price. The screen returns an outright 'no fit' on Pricing Power: argon realisations fell more than 25% in the third quarter of FY26 on soft steel demand and surplus supply from steel producers' own captive plants, had still not recovered to first-half levels by the fourth quarter, and management frames pricing as something to wait out rather than something it sets. That single fact ties three findings together — the commodity nature of oxygen, nitrogen and argon (the 'good enough goods' watch risk, where switching costs come from pipelines and contracts rather than any brand premium), the cost-indifferent competing supply from captive plants (the new-entrants watch risk), and cyclicality, the only high-rated risk in the review, where the same quarter showed revenue down 9% sequentially, EBITDA down 25% and margins falling from 38% to 31%. The take-or-pay book cushions this but does not remove it, since on-site customers were lifting below their committed volumes. Two other patterns that look encouraging on the surface are thinner on inspection: the market-share case rests on a genuinely large runway — mid-single-digit share against Linde and Inox at roughly 25% each, with share to be taken from the unorganised sector and from new geographies in North and West India — but share was about 4% in August 2025 and still mid-single digits by February 2026, so no measurable gain has yet been demonstrated; and the innovation claim rests on in-house plant design and engineering versus peers who license multinational technology, with no research spending disclosed and no named new product. Cost work is real but defensive — more power-efficient new plants, buying power on exchanges, a renewable energy contract — and matters because power is both the largest input and a regulated tariff item, which is what keeps government dependency on the watch list after a state tariff increase in the South drove the FY23 cost spike. Net: a well-run, honestly-reported, structurally advantaged business whose economics are capital-hungry and whose selling price it does not control.
