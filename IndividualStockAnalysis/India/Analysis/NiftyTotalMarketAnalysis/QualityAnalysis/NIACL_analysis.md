# The New India Assurance Company Ltd. (NIACL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

The New India Assurance Company is a 106-year-old, government-owned general (non-life) insurance company founded by Sir Dorabji Tata in 1919 and nationalized in 1973, which sells policies that cover things other than life — health and personal accident cover, motor insurance, fire/property, marine, crop, liability and aviation — to retail customers, SMEs and large corporates, and it is also the designated state insurer for Gujarat and Lakshadweep. It collects premiums through a mix of brokers (~37-39%), its own direct offices (~31-33%), agents (~22-24%), motor dealers (~6-7%) and banks (~0.5%), operating from roughly 1,660-1,840 offices across India plus operations in 24-25 countries. It is the largest non-life insurer in India by gross direct premium, with about 12.8%-15.5% market share and gross written premium of roughly Rs 42,000 crore in FY24 and Rs 35,555 crore in the first nine months of FY26. Notably, it does not make money on insurance itself — its combined ratio runs well above 100% (116%-124%), meaning claims and expenses exceed premiums (a 9M FY26 underwriting loss of Rs 7,046 crore); profit comes from investing the ~Rs 1 lakh crore float of premiums, which generated Rs 2,280 crore of investment income in one quarter and turned that underwriting loss into a Rs 372 crore quarterly profit after tax.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Health and Personal Accident (PA)** — Medical and accident cover for individuals and groups; it is by far the biggest line at roughly 45%-50% of the book and management notes it has overtaken motor as the dominant business since COVID, though rising health claims have pushed up the claims ratio.
- **Fire** — Property/fire cover, largely for corporates and industrial risks; the second-largest line at about 15%-17% of premium, and a segment where management says it has consistently been market leader (Rs 2,272 crore of premium in Q1 FY26, up ~20%).
- **Motor Third Party (Motor TP)** — Compulsory cover for injury or damage a vehicle causes to others, about 10.6%-14% of the book, sold heavily alongside new-vehicle packages (3-year TP on cars, 5-year on two-wheelers) with an incurred claims ratio described as running around 84-86%.
- **Motor Own Damage (Motor OD)** — Cover for damage to the policyholder's own vehicle, about 11%-12% of premium, sold mainly through car dealer tie-ups; management says it runs above a 100% claims ratio because of heavy price discounting, and is written largely to capture the more profitable third-party premium bundled with it.
- **Marine** — Cargo and marine risk cover, a small line at roughly 2.4%-3% of the book, but one where the company says it holds market leadership.
- **Crop** — Agricultural crop insurance, the smallest named line at under 1% of premium (0.94% in Q1 FY26, falling to 0.37% by 9M FY26).
- **Other lines (liability, aviation, engineering)** — A residual bucket of roughly 8%-10% of premium covering liability, aviation and engineering risks, where the company claims leadership in aviation and engineering; aviation is lumpy — a single Air India claim pushed the group claims ratio to 99.76% in Q1 FY26.
- **Overseas operations** — Insurance business written in 24-25 countries, adding roughly Rs 1,000 crore a quarter on top of Indian premium (Rs 13,334 crore global vs Rs 12,299 crore Indian in Q1 FY26); it carries currency risk, as shown by a Rs 110 crore impairment on the Nigerian business when the naira devalued.
- **Investments / investment income** — The company invests the premium float — assets under management of about Rs 1,00,802 crore — in fixed income and equities, and the resulting interest, dividends, rent and capital gains (Rs 2,280 crore in Q3 FY26) are what actually produce the company's profit, since underwriting itself loses money.
- **Claims settlement / Motor TP claims management** — A core operating activity rather than a revenue line: management describes actively settling pending motor third-party court claims through Lok Adalat compromises (7,000-8,000 in one sitting) and 30-day Detailed Accident Report processes to cut interest costs, litigation and claim backlogs.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

The single fact that explains most of this company is that it sells insurance below what the claims cost. The 34-check framework scores it worst on Return on Capital (-1.67) and Industry Structure (-1.0): every ₹100 of capital produced ₹-16 of operating cash this year, and only ₹1 of every ₹100 of sales is operating profit, with margins swinging about ±11.7 points a year. The multibagger screen finds the same thing from the other direction. The one pattern that genuinely fits, Market Share Gainers, is real and repeated — share up from 12.78% in FY24 to 15.51% in Q1 FY26, growing 15.27% against an industry at 8.84%, and 13.40% for 9M FY26 on 13.71% domestic growth against 8.69% — but that share was bought at a combined ratio of 116-124%, meaning the more business it writes, the more it loses on underwriting. So the strength and the weakness here are not two separate findings; they are the same act seen twice. This is why the checks on Pricing, Mix and Volume, Profit margin, and Barriers to entry all score -2 while the growth number looks impressive.

What keeps the company standing is float, and that too shows up in all three analyses at once. The Cost of working capital check is the report's single best score (+2): customers pay premiums upfront, so they effectively fund the business. The Recurring Revenue pattern picks this up as a partial fit — technical reserves grew to ₹56,745 crore and assets under management to ₹1,00,890 crore by Q3 FY26, with investment income of ₹2,280 crore in the quarter being the sole reason the company earns anything at all. But that same float is why the Asset turn check scores -1: each ₹1 of assets generates only ₹0.45 of sales, because this is a float-heavy rather than an asset-light business. Meanwhile the things that would normally let an insurer charge more are absent. Network effects score -2 because distribution is rented, not owned — brokers alone are 37-38.8% of premium and the pricing power sits with them. That single fact drives the Friendly Middleman pattern to only a partial fit and simultaneously feeds the Good Enough Goods risk, where management repeatedly describes losing profitable business because private players pay agents and dealers more. The Brand Strength pattern captures the frustration precisely: a 1919 founding by Sir Dorabji Tata, 106 years of history, market leadership since 1973, CRISIL AAA and an AM Best outlook raised to positive in December 2025 — and none of it converts into the ability to charge a higher price.

The third thread is that much of what happens to this company is decided by someone else. Government Dependency is the only HIGH RISK finding, and it is the root of several other scores: motor third-party prices are fixed by the Ministry and have not been raised since 2018, which is exactly why the Pricing Power pattern is a flat NO FIT and why loss ratios sit at 116% in motor own-damage, 105% in motor third-party and 109% in health. Being a Government of India undertaking also means being CAG-audited and unable to match private commission structures — the New Entrants risk (ELEVATED) — and being a regulator-designated systemically important insurer, which is why the Security by obscurity check scores -2: this is the most visible target in the sector, not a quiet compounder. Against this, management scores a neutral 0.0 and the Corporate Culture pattern is a NO FIT for a specific reason: the May 2024 commitment to a 110% combined ratio and double-digit return on equity was followed by a 124% combined ratio and 4.95% return on equity for 9M FY26, and the FY26 pledge to cut the combined ratio by 3% moved the wrong way. There are genuine efforts underway — offices cut from about 2,100 to 1,660, the operating expense ratio down to 7.86% in Q1 FY26, a deliberate exit from crop insurance now at 0.37% of the mix, and a pivot toward health and personal accident from 45% of the mix in FY24 to 48-50% by FY26 — and Capital Allocation is the one positive area at +0.25 with resilience judged SOUND. But the combined rating of 34 out of 100 (Weak) reflects that these are repairs at the edges of a business whose central economics are set by regulators, intermediaries and competitors rather than by the company itself.

*"The expert sells it for you"*

> "How these private companies are paying 50%? ... We have some limitation to pay some fixed amount, 2.5% we can pay ... But, we cannot match." — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ Operating margin of 1% — too thin for a protected franchise.

**What the calls show:** NIACL is a Government of India undertaking whose economics are set by policy at almost every layer: motor TP prices are fixed by MoRTH and have not been raised since 2018, IRDAI's EOM/commission, product, Bima Sugam/Bima Vistaar and rural-obligation regulations reshaped the P&L, CAG audit caps what it can pay intermediaries, crop premium arrives via government schemes, and a Central Government-approved wage revision forced INR 1,877 cr of provisions in 9M FY26 that pushed the combined ratio to 124%.
