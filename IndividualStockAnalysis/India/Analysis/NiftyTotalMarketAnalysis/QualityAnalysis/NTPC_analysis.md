# NTPC Ltd. (NTPC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

NTPC is a Government of India-owned company that generates electricity and sells it in bulk, mainly to state distribution companies (DISCOMs) across India under long-term arrangements, at tariffs set under CERC regulations — a cost-plus style framework that management says supports steady profitability, cash flows and dividends. Most of its power comes from large coal-fired stations, supplemented by gas, renewables, hydro/pumped storage and, in future, nuclear; it also digs its own coal from captive mines to secure fuel and trades power through a subsidiary. It is the country's largest power producer, meeting roughly 20-25% of India's electricity supply, with group installed capacity of about 89-90 GW as of March 2026 and generation of 432.2 billion units in FY26. Operations are spread across India (plus a power project in Bangladesh through a joint venture), and the company earns money by getting paid for the capacity it keeps available and the electricity it dispatches, with dues from DISCOMs collected at essentially 100%+ and receivable days down to 15 days.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Coal-based thermal generation** — The core business — large coal-fired power stations that provide reliable baseload and grid-balancing power; NTPC's coal fleet ran at 72.04% PLF in FY26 versus a 63.20% rest-of-India average, and the company is executing about 16.5 GW of new coal capacity with a further ~4.6 GW in the pipeline against a national requirement of 86 GW by 2036.
- **Gas-based generation** — A small fleet of gas-fired plants; management describes dependence on gas as 'limited', which it says insulates NTPC from swings in global gas prices.
- **Renewable energy (NTPC Green Energy Limited / NGEL)** — The listed green arm building solar and wind; group renewable capacity reached 12,068 MW after adding 4,738 MW in FY26 (NGEL alone 4,225 MW), with generation up 114% to 14.6 billion units and a target of 60 GW of renewables by 2032, backed by a government investment approval limit of INR 20,000 crore for the renewable subsidiaries.
- **Coal mining (captive mines / NTPC Mining Limited)** — NTPC mines its own coal to feed its plants — 47.88 million tonnes produced in FY26, up 8.5%, covering about 18% of its coal needs; the mining business was fully transferred into wholly-owned NTPC Mining Limited effective 1 April 2026.
- **Nuclear (NTPC Parmanu Urja Nigam Limited, plus NPCIL JV)** — A newer growth leg intended to supply baseload power for decades — the Mahi Banswara project in Rajasthan is a JV with NPCIL (NPCIL 51%, NTPC 49%) that has received regulatory excavation consent, and NTPC has set up a wholly-owned nuclear company and is running site studies in several states.
- **Hydro and pumped storage** — Hydro assets including the Tehri pumped storage project, whose final 250 MW unit was declared commercial in April 2026, making the plant fully operational and useful for balancing renewable supply.
- **Battery energy storage (BESS)** — Storage being added to firm up renewables and meet peak demand — 5 GWh being built at existing NTPC thermal stations on a cost-plus basis plus 320 MWh under NGEL, now covered by a CERC tariff framework for co-located storage.
- **Power trading (NVVN)** — A trading subsidiary that buys and sells power; management reported 13% growth in trading volumes in FY26.
- **Green chemicals, hydrogen and carbon capture** — Early-stage emerging-technology businesses the CMD highlighted (green molecules, hydrogen, carbon capture, circular economy), with dedicated executives but no size disclosed in the calls.
- **Joint ventures and subsidiaries (including international)** — A large share of growth now comes through JVs and subsidiaries — they contributed 7,795 MW of the record 9,618 MW added in FY26, including the acquisition of the 1,350 MW Sinnar thermal station with MAHAGENCO, and NTPC also built the 1,320 MW Maitree project in Bangladesh through a JV.

### The story in depth

NTPC's whole story starts with the same fact: it owns an enormous pile of physical plant — ₹4,70,618 crore of gross property, plant and equipment against group income of ₹1,89,799 crore — and sells regulated electricity off it. That single trait cuts both ways. It is what makes the 'Cost to Replicate' pattern a STRONG FIT: the ₹4.71 lakh crore of plant, roughly 36 GW under construction, captive coal blocks and pithead sites, a sovereign-level credit rating with 5.98% funding cost, and nuclear licensing through ASHVINI/NPCIL are simply not things a challenger can assemble. It is also why the quality framework marks Asset turn at its worst score: every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.34 of sales. The barriers are real (capital, land, coal linkage, environmental and AERB clearances) and profit margins have barely moved, about ±2.8 points, with ₹28 of operating profit on every ₹100 of sales — but the price of that fortress is a business that will always need heavy money in before money comes out, with group growth capex of ₹49,068 crore in FY26 and a ₹16.8 lakh crore ten-year plan ahead.

The second thread is regulation, and it explains most of the contradictions in the record. Because CERC sets tariffs on a cost-plus, regulated-equity basis under new five-year norms effective April 2024, earnings are unusually stable: Cyclicality scores LOW, and FY26 proved it — standalone income fell 2.69% on weaker demand yet profit still rose 18%. That same stability funds a dividend paid and raised for 33 consecutive years, ₹9 per share in FY26, maintained right through the heavy building programme. But the identical mechanism is why three of the multibagger patterns come back NO FIT. There is no Pricing Power, because management talks about protecting profitability within regulation and about 'affordable' power, never about raising prices. There is no Brand Strength, because the product is a commodity sold to captive state buyers. Toll Roads does not fit either, since electricity is a large, actively negotiated line item for distribution companies and the calls show backing down and curtailment — 314 MUs at NGEL in FY26 — rather than unconditional offtake. On the quality side the same trait produces a worst-score on Pricing, Mix and Volume: growth is purely capacity-led with no pricing lever. And the risk review names the flip side plainly — Government Dependency is the one HIGH RISK, with tariffs set by the regulator, the capacity plan handed down by the Ministry of Power, state distribution companies as customers, and the government as owner. The genuine cost advantage that shows up as 'Low Price Plus' — pithead siting, captive coal ramped from 14 MMT in FY22 to 47.88 MMT in FY26, borrowing cost cut from 6.61% to 5.98% — only rates PARTIAL for the same reason: inside cost-plus, savings do not straightforwardly become shareholder profit.

The third thread is the transition, and it is where the strongest tailwind and the least-protected ground meet. The end market is in clear multi-decade expansion — India's electricity requirement projected from 1,708 BU in FY26 to 8,100 BU by FY50, peak demand from 245 GW to 750 GW — and NTPC runs its assets harder than the industry, producing 24% of national generation from 17% of installed capacity at 72% utilisation against 63.2% for the rest of India, while capacity went from 69 GW in August 2022 to over 90 GW by mid-2026. But the profit core is being demoted by design: coal plants are backed down, utilisation has slipped from above 80% five or six years ago, and management itself projects non-fossil generation rising toward 78%. That is the Technological Innovation WATCH risk, and the pressure from investors and society on coal is the Shifting Consumer Preferences WATCH — with a credible answer in the record, since MSCI moved the rating from CCC to BB by FY26 and roughly 60% of FY26's record capacity addition was renewable. The catch is that the segment NTPC is pivoting into does not carry the moat the old one did: renewables are competitively bid and crowded with private producers, which is the New Entrants WATCH, and NGEL already absorbs grid curtailment. This is why the framework scores Competitive Advantage negative overall — no network effects, no obscurity, since NTPC is India's largest power producer and central to national policy — while Management and people score well, with every functional director having risen from executive trainee inside the company and a solid promise-to-delivery trail on coal, capacity, receivables and dividends. Netting it out, the combined rating is 51 out of 100, or Decent, built from 52 on business quality, 44 on multibagger fit and 58 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged SOUND: a durable, well-run, hard-to-copy asset base whose returns are capped by the same rules that make it safe.

## Section 2 — Does it look like a long-term winner? (11 multibagger patterns)

### Cost to Replicate — STRONG FIT
*"Could a newcomer copy this?"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** The replication cost is concrete and enormous: INR 4.71 lakh crore of gross plant at March 2026, ~36 GW under construction, captive coal blocks and pithead sites, sovereign-level credit rating and 5.98% funding cost, plus nuclear licensing via ASHVINI/NPCIL — a well-funded newcomer could not rebuild this in a decade, though regulated returns cap what the moat earns.

### Innovation Dominance — LIKELY FIT

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 1.3× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** NTPC is literally 'A Government of India Enterprise' whose returns are set by CERC tariff regulations, whose capacity plan is handed down by the Ministry of Power (80 GW thermal, of which 'NTPC will invest in 26 gigawatt'), whose customers are state DISCOMs, and whose new growth legs (nuclear via the SHANTI Act, captive/commercial coal policy, INR20,000 crore government-approved investment limit for RE subsidiaries) all depend on policy enablement — the textbook fixed-infrastructure utility.

> "the new CERC norms for the next 5 years have come in effect... will be able to help to maintain our profitability and cash flows" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Regulated cost-plus returns are also a stabiliser, and diversification across coal, RE, hydro, nuclear, storage and green molecules broadens the policy base — but every one of those legs is still policy-set.*

### Stakeholder Concentration — ELEVATED
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Structurally NTPC sells to a captive set of state DISCOMs and historically bought coal from essentially one supplier (Coal India), with recurring management attention to fuel supply and to realisation from distribution companies; both are single-counterparty-type exposures even though neither is a single named customer.

> "with nearly 18% of our coal requirement being met through captive mines, fuel security for our stations is more assured than ever before" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Deliberate de-concentration on both sides — captive coal to 47.88 MMT (target ~25% of need), commercial-mine sourcing 'through an open and transparent bidding process' to eliminate imports, pan-India customer/geographic spread, and receivable days down to 15 with 100% realisation.*

- ⚠️ Customers now take about 69 days to pay, up from 41 — lengthening collection terms are how weak bargaining power against concentrated customers shows up in the numbers.

## What to watch

- Whether the renewables build (New Entrants, WATCH) earns real returns — NGEL's auction-based tariffs and its 314 MUs of FY26 curtailment are the test of whether the pivot reproduces the old moat or just adds capacity.
- Any change to the CERC cost-plus framework or the five-year norms effective April 2024 — this single rule underpins the LOW Cyclicality score, the steady 28% margins and the 33-year dividend, and also caps them (Pricing Power, NO FIT).
- Thermal utilisation and the pace of the fossil-to-non-fossil shift (Technological Innovation, WATCH) — utilisation at 72-77% versus above 80% five or six years ago, against management's own path toward a 78% non-fossil generation share.
- Whether the ₹16.8 lakh crore ten-year plan and ₹49,068 crore of FY26 group capex improve or further dilute Asset turn (currently ₹0.34 of sales per ₹1 of assets) — heavier assets without matching sales would worsen the framework's weakest check.
- Policy and ownership decisions flowing from Government Dependency (HIGH RISK) — the Ministry of Power capacity mandate, including the 26 GW thermal share, and realisation from state distribution companies, where receivables have already been pulled from 45 days to 15.
