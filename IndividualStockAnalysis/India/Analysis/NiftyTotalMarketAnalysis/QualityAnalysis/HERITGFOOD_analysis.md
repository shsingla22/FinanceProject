# Heritage Foods (HERITGFOOD) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Heritage Foods is an Indian dairy company, founded in 1992 and headquartered in Hyderabad, that buys raw milk directly from farmers and sells it to consumers as packaged liquid milk and as branded milk products such as curd, paneer, ghee, butter, buttermilk, lassi, ice cream and Indian sweets like gulab jamun and rasgulla. It makes money on the spread between what it pays farmers per litre (₹46.61/litre in Q1 FY27) and what it charges consumers (₹58.68/litre average milk selling price), with fatter margins on the value-added products; it describes this as an 'integrated farm-to-consumer model'. Selling is through consumer channels — its own distribution, general trade, and organized/national retail customers — rather than bulk institutional orders, which management says it does not do. Its milk and milk products are sold across Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Odisha, NCR Delhi, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand; consolidated quarterly revenue was about ₹1,338 crore in Q1 FY27, up 18% year-on-year, with EBITDA margin of 4.6% and PAT margin of 1.9%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Dairy — liquid milk** — The core business of procuring raw milk from farmers (18.10 lakh litres a day in Q1 FY27, up 2%) and selling packaged milk; it is the volume backbone and the largest single revenue line, though management says milk sales volume has been roughly flat lately and profitability swings with procurement costs.
- **Value-Added Products (VAP)** — Curd, paneer, buttermilk, lassi, ice cream, sweets and similar branded products — the stated 'primary growth engine', at ₹5,636 million in Q1 FY27 (up 40%) and a record 44% of consolidated revenue, or 49% (₹6,322 million) if consumer-pack ghee and butter are included, versus about 35% back in Q1 FY23.
- **Ice cream (Alpenvie and Get-A-Way)** — A fast-growing premium ice cream and nutrition-led frozen dessert business that crossed ₹550 million in Q1 FY27 (up 65%), made at a newly commissioned dedicated factory running at roughly 40% utilisation, with Get-A-Way revenue up 196% and near breakeven.
- **Heritage Nutrivet Limited (cattle feed / animal nutrition)** — A wholly owned subsidiary selling cattle feed and animal nutrition products, largely to the farmer base that supplies milk; small relative to dairy at ₹728 million revenue in Q1 FY27 (up 37%) with ₹20 million profit before tax.
- **Renewable energy** — 10.50 MW of solar and wind generation capacity used captively to power the company's own dairy factories rather than sold outside — described as a business division but essentially a cost-saving support activity, with no separate revenue discussed on the calls.
- **Heritage Novandie Foods (Livo)** — A high-protein yogurt business under the Livo brand that became a wholly owned subsidiary in Q1 FY27; small and early-stage, described as performing well in organized retail.
- **Peanut Butter and Jelly Limited** — A 'better-for-you' premium nutrition foods venture in which Heritage raised its stake to 71% in Q1 FY27; an early-stage growth platform, with no revenue figures given on the calls.
- **New/premium brand launches** — Nourish+ (including a high-protein paneer), Sampurna A2 curd, chef's choice curd and organic milk — recently introduced premium lines management says are gaining acceptance, but which are not yet sized in the calls.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Heritage Foods comes out of all three reviews as a well-run, honestly managed dairy business that is genuinely winning volume, but without a deep moat around it. The quality framework scores management, capital allocation and customer benefits at the top of the range: every ₹1 of assets produces ₹2.32 of sales, only about 18 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and the business earned ₹16 of operating cash for every ₹100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings. Management also volunteers unflattering facts — calling bulk fat a loss-making proposition, flagging the value-added slowdown itself, and pointing out it was cycling a weak base rather than taking credit for a 40% print — which makes the rest of its disclosures more believable. The promised spending was delivered too: a ₹200 crore-plus greenfield ice cream plant announced in October 2024 was commissioned and running at roughly 40% utilisation by Q1 FY27, with ice cream revenue up 65%. Where the framework marks the company down is precisely where the pattern screen and the risk review also converge: competitive advantage and industry structure both score negative, and the combined rating lands at 55 out of 100, or Decent — 63 for business quality, only 26 for multibagger fit, and 76 for risk safety.

The single strongest thread running through all three records is share gain, and it is also where the same trait creates both the opportunity and the exposure. The company is taking business from a large, structurally weak unorganised sector, from weaker branded players in organised channels, and from category exits such as HUL's ice cream restructuring, with named gains in curd, paneer and buttermilk and value-added products rising from about 27-28% of revenue to a record 44%, against 36% a year earlier. But the screen is blunt about how that share is being won: 80% of the 35% value-added volume growth came from opening new distribution points in existing markets, not from customers seeking out the brand. That is why 'Market Share Gainers' rates a likely fit while 'Brand Strength' only rates partial, and it is the same fact that makes the 'Good Enough Goods' risk elevated — liquid milk and curd are largely undifferentiated against loose milk and cheaper co-operative alternatives, so the price premium has to be defended continuously rather than resting on brand pull. Distribution reach is a real asset, but it is a reach that has to keep being extended.

The second shared thread is raw milk, which is at once the company's hardest-to-copy asset and its biggest swing factor. A village-level procurement network built over 30-plus years — farmer engagement, vet services, cattle nutrition, timely payments — secured 18.1 lakh litres a day even in a shortage year, and that is the barrier a funded newcomer cannot assemble quickly, which is why 'Cost to Replicate' registers at all. The same dependence is why 'Cyclicality' is flagged elevated: management frames four years of results around flush and lean seasons, weather and El Nino, and margins move violently with them — one quarter posted a 24-quarter revenue high alongside a 24-quarter margin low, and EBITDA margin fell to 4.6% in Q1 FY27 after running at 8.16% earlier. Pricing follows costs with a lag rather than leading them: prices were flat for fifteen months to October 2024 even as milk realisation fell, before a 5.12% weighted increase came through in Q1 FY27. That lag is exactly why 'Pricing Power' is only partial and why 'Toll Roads' is no fit at all — this is a visible, price-sensitive household purchase, not an invisible critical input. Alongside those two elevated risks, five of the eight risk channels rate low and overall resilience is judged sound, but the pattern screen finds no recurring or subscription revenue, no professional recommender driving demand, and no quantified own-parlour, franchise or direct-to-consumer sales — which is the main reason multibagger fit scores just 26 even though the underlying business is solid.

The innovation record is what keeps the shifting-preferences risk low rather than high. There is a named launch every year with disclosed revenue impact — Gulab Jamun, Rasgulla, buttermilk cups and spout-pack ghee in 2022, then Alpenvie, Get-A-Way, Livo yogurt, Nourish+ high-protein paneer and Sampurna A2 curd, plus making Novandie a wholly owned subsidiary and taking a 71% stake in Peanut Butter and Jelly. Rather than losing its customer to protein and premium trends, the company is re-pointing its portfolio at them, and the end markets themselves are expanding as organised dairy takes share from unorganised and per-capita ice cream and premium dairy penetration stay very low, evidenced by four straight years of high-teens value-added growth. There is also a real cost programme behind the scenes — eight named cost-of-production projects, electric vehicle conversion, truck-load utilisation lifted from 50% to 70-80% — though management explicitly rejects a low-price positioning and no durable, hard-to-copy cost edge is visible. One internal tension is worth noting plainly: the framework calls the business asset-light on the strength of its ₹2.32 asset turn, while the calls describe a company that keeps adding plants and village collection infrastructure. Both can be true, but it means future growth will keep consuming capital, and the returns on that capital will keep depending on a milk cycle nobody at the company controls.

**What the calls show:** Share gain is claimed consistently from Aug 2022 ('gain market share') to Jul 2026, where management names gains in curd, paneer and buttermilk in measurable organised channels against branded peers, alongside VAP rising from ~28% to a record 44% of consolidated revenue and 18% revenue growth in a shortage year.

### Pricing Power — PARTIAL
*"Raises prices, keeps the customers"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (0 of 2 checks supporting).

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but none of the 3 computed fingerprints confirms it in the numbers yet — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across all four years management frames results around the raw-milk procurement cycle (flush vs lean season, weather, El Nino), and margins swing violently with it — Q1FY23 revenue at a 24-quarter high with margins at a 24-quarter low, EBITDA margin 8.16% in Q2FY25, then 4.6% in Q1FY27 despite 18% revenue growth. It is an input-commodity cycle rather than a customer-capex cycle, and demand is a daily consumable, which bounds but does not remove the damage.

> "elevated milk procurement costs continue to weigh on the dairy industry... EBITDA margin of 4.6%" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/consumable product with stable daily demand rather than customer capex; countercyclical investment in procurement network, ice cream plant and brands while input costs squeeze the industry; VAP mix rising to 44-49% of revenue*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 2 of the last 11 years with swings of ±13 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between -14% and 11% (±7.6 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 4% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 8%).

### Good Enough Goods — ELEVATED
*"Cheaper rivals that are good enough"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Liquid milk and curd are largely undifferentiated products sold against loose milk and cheaper co-operative/unorganised alternatives, so the premium must be defended continuously; management explicitly refuses deep discounting and instead relies on brand and quality, and so far has sustained a large gap between procurement (INR46.61/l) and realisation (INR58.68/l) while pushing through price hikes.

> "we do not go for deep discounts kind of strategy because our product... high quality products and all this growth we have got with just purely by acquiring more and more consumers" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Demonstrated pricing power — 4% milk and ~5% VAP price increases passed through with volumes still growing and share gained; premiumisation into A2, organic and high-protein niches where scale-based good-enough substitutes are harder*

## What to watch

- Cyclicality risk: whether the 5.12% value-added price increase and 4% milk realisation gain hold through the next lean season, or margins slide back toward the 4.6% EBITDA level of Q1 FY27.
- Market Share Gainers pattern and the convenience-benefits check: 80% of value-added volume growth came from new distribution points — watch whether growth continues once the easy runway of new points in existing markets thins out.
- Capex-for-growth check: the ₹200 crore-plus ice cream plant is only at about 40% utilisation, so watch whether the 65% ice cream growth and the Alpenvie refresh actually fill it.
- Good Enough Goods risk: value-added products at a record 44% of revenue versus 36% a year earlier, with curd still about 69% of that mix — management has already flagged a value-added slowdown once, so watch whether the mix shift stalls against cheaper unorganised alternatives.
