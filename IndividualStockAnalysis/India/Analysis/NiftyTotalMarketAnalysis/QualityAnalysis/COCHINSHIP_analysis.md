# Cochin Shipyard Ltd. (COCHINSHIP) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

Cochin Shipyard Limited is an Indian shipyard company that builds new ships and repairs existing ones, working to order under long contracts rather than selling off the shelf. Its customers are mainly the Indian Navy and other government arms (it built and delivered the indigenous aircraft carrier INS Vikrant, is building 8 anti-submarine warfare shallow water craft worth about INR 6,300 crores, and builds electric ferries for the Kochi Water Metro and the Inland Waterways Authority), plus commercial buyers in India and Europe such as German and Norwegian owners, tug operators like Ocean Sparkle and Polestar, and Dredging Corporation of India; it has also signed a Master Ship Repair Agreement letting it repair US Navy vessels. Money comes in as a contract is executed over several years — roughly a year of design, engineering and procurement first, then steel fabrication, outfitting, trials and delivery — with repair work billed on refits, drydocking and equipment upgrades. It operates from its main yard in Kochi (including a new 310-metre dry dock and an International Ship Repair Facility commissioned in January 2024 at a capex of about INR 2,769 crores), from ship repair units in Mumbai, the Andaman & Nicobar Islands and Kolkata, and through subsidiaries at Udupi and Hooghly; in Q1 FY26 it reported turnover of INR 1,068.59 crores and an order book of about INR 21,100 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Shipbuilding** — Designs and constructs new vessels — naval ships, ferries, dredgers, cargo ships and offshore wind service vessels — and is the larger half of the business, at 72% of operating income in FY24 and about INR 19,600 crores of the INR 21,100 crore order book across 75 vessels.
- **Ship Repair** — Drydocks, refits, repairs and upgrades ships (including mid-life upgrades and repowering of naval platforms and maintenance on INS Vikrant), contributing 28% of FY24 operating income, crossing INR 1,000 crores of turnover for the first time that year, with an order book of roughly INR 1,500 crores.
- **Indigenous Aircraft Carrier (IAC) work** — The INS Vikrant programme, delivered and commissioned in 2022, which still generated 46% of FY24 shipbuilding revenue and now also feeds repair and maintenance contracts, such as an INR 488.25 crore deal signed in December 2023.
- **ASW-SWC corvettes for the Indian Navy** — A series of 8 anti-submarine warfare shallow water craft valued at about INR 6,300 crores — management calls it the biggest ongoing project after the carrier — with 3 launched in November 2023 and the rest in keel-laying or fabrication stages.
- **Kochi Water Metro / inland passenger vessels** — Hybrid electric catamaran ferries for Kochi Metro Rail (23 under contract, 19 delivered by mid-2025) plus a follow-on 8-vessel order from the Inland Waterways Authority of India; small in rupee terms but described as high-visibility work that is being replicated in other regions.
- **Commercial / export shipbuilding** — Merchant and specialised vessels for overseas owners — 8 numbers of 7,000 deadweight multi-purpose vessels for European clients, autonomous electric barges for ASKO in Norway, and Hybrid Service Operation Vessels for the European offshore wind market worth roughly INR 1,000–1,200 crores for two.
- **Udupi Cochin Shipyard Limited (UCSL)** — A wholly-owned subsidiary yard acquired through insolvency proceedings that builds tugs and small dry cargo ships for clients like Ocean Sparkle, Polestar and Wilson of Norway; it turned profitable in FY24 with INR 186 crores of revenue and about INR 800 crores of orders.
- **Hooghly Cochin Shipyard Limited (HCSL)** — The other wholly-owned subsidiary yard, newer and much smaller, building ASD tugs and a luxury river cruise vessel, with an order book of roughly INR 150 crores as of FY24.
- **Regional ship repair units (CMSRU, CANSRU, CKSRU)** — Satellite repair yards in Mumbai, the Andaman & Nicobar Islands and Kolkata that supplement the Kochi yard, with Mumbai billing over INR 150 crores and Andaman about INR 100 crores in FY24.
- **Green and new-technology vessels** — A small but strategically emphasised line covering India's first indigenously built hydrogen fuel cell vessel, hybrid-electric ferries and an autonomous underwater vehicle — management says these are not yet financially material but position the yard for future green shipbuilding work.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Cochin Shipyard comes out of these three reviews as a well-run company that has not yet been paid back for what it built. The people side is the standout: Management scores a perfect 1.0 among the quality areas, with top marks for candor and for disciplined stewardship, and the multibagger screen turns that same evidence into its only STRONG FIT, Corporate Culture. The proof point in both records is the same episode — management opening a call unprompted to explain a ₹275-280 crore revenue reversal in Q4 FY23 and framing it as deliberate conservatism, alongside repeated warnings that margins are not sustainable. Stewardship matches the talk: the company funded its largest-ever capital programme straight through the weak FY23 period while redeeming ₹100 crore of bonds to become practically debt-free, paid ₹256.5 crore of dividends in FY24 (32% of profit), and kept acquisitions to one small distressed bolt-on, UCSL, which moved from a ₹9 crore loss to ₹1 crore profit with a roughly ₹800 crore order book of its own.

The single fact that most shapes this company cuts both ways. Roughly ₹2,769-3,250 crore was spent over seven years on a 310-metre stepped dry dock and the ship repair facility (ISRF), and that spending is simultaneously the source of two patterns and two of the worst quality scores. On the pattern side it underpins Cost to Replicate — years of money and a finite coastal site that a rival cannot quickly copy — and Market Share Gainers, since the new facilities add about a quarter of India's ship repair capacity by vessel numbers and helped ship repair cross ₹1,000 crore for the first time in FY24. On the quality side the same assets explain why every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.35 of sales, why ISRF is expected to yield only ₹600-plus crore of revenue against that outlay, and why the Return on Capital area scores the worst possible -1.0: this year the business generated minus ₹16 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital employed, with about 250 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Management says revenue can double to FY31 without much further spending, so the capital is in place — the open question is whether it converts to cash. The green and technical work runs on the same logic: India's first hydrogen fuel-cell vessel, autonomous electric barges for ASKO Norway, hybrid vessels and zero-emission feeder ships earn a LIKELY FIT on Innovation Dominance and are precisely why two risk channels, Technological Innovation and Shifting Consumer Preferences, come back LOW — the industry's shift toward cleaner ships is arriving as orders rather than as a threat.

The risks are largely the mirror image of the strengths, which is why the combined rating lands at Mixed (47 out of 100) and resilience reads STRAINED. Government Dependency is the one HIGH RISK: about ₹16,064 crore of the ₹21,500 crore order book is defence, order flow waits on Navy tender timing, and a January 2023 ministry decision to indigenise two sensors shows how one outside call moves the business. That same concentration shows up again as ELEVATED Stakeholder Concentration — 46% of FY24 shipbuilding revenue came from the Indigenous Aircraft Carrier alone — and as ELEVATED Cyclicality, where FY23 turnover collapsed because no FY22 contracts had reached steel-cutting and FY24 then jumped 62% with profit before tax up 156%, matching the quality framework's finding that growth averages about 10% a year but is lumpy. The customer side also explains why five of the eleven patterns are outright NO FIT: with fixed-price, long-dated contracts won through competitive tenders against other Indian yards, the company absorbs currency and cost-estimation risk itself, so Pricing Power fails — the very reversal that demonstrated management's honesty is also the evidence that it cannot reprice. Shipbuilding margin guidance has drifted to 10-12%, and the Good Enough Goods channel flags lower-cost Korean and Chinese yards as the benchmark, with the company itself conceding it needs Korean help (the HD KSOE tie-up) to build merchant ships in competitive time. The counterweights run through every risk channel and point the same way: roughly ₹2,688 crore of European export orders, US Navy and Maersk arrangements, and ship repair acting as an aftermarket annuity on ships the yard itself built — including a ₹488 crore contract to repair INS Vikrant — which is what earns the PARTIAL on Recurring Revenue and cushions the cycle.

## Section 2 — Does it look like a long-term winner? (11 multibagger patterns)

### Corporate Culture — STRONG FIT
*"A culture that compounds"*

> "we felt that we are recognizing slightly more than what is required for the hull part. So we thought we'll make that correction in the Q4 so as to make it more prudent and in a more conservative basis" — *management, on an earnings call*

### Innovation Dominance — LIKELY FIT
*"Out-inventing the competition"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits and every one of the 2 computable checks supports the pattern.

**What the calls show:** A consistent record of technical firsts — India's first hydrogen fuel-cell vessel, the world's first autonomous electric barges for ASKO Norway, hybrid SOVs and zero-emission feeder container ships, the world's first stepped dry dock — and these green credentials directly converted into European orders. But R&D spend is never disclosed, gross margins are ordinary, and management explicitly declines a full smart-shipyard programme.

> "we are not currently talking about moving entirely into a smart shipyard kind of a configuration" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ High retained margin (56% of sales) alongside 10% yearly growth — the profitable-innovation flywheel: margins fund the spending that drives the growth.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** The company is a government-owned yard whose fortunes turn on MoD/Navy ordering: about Rs.16,064 crores of the Rs.21,500 crore order book was defence, order flow depends on Navy RFI/RFP timing and AON, and a January 2023 MoD decision to indigenise two sensors forced a re-estimation that hit Q4 FY23 revenue. Growth narrative rests on Maritime India Vision 2030/2047, the standard tug design policy and the Rs.70,000 crore Maritime Development Fund.

> "decisions have been taken that all these sensors will now be made in India. And this decision has been taken in January 2023" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Genuine diversification underway — ~Rs.2,688 crores of European commercial export orders, US Navy MSRA, Maersk and Drydocks World MoUs, and a growing commercial ship repair franchise.*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but none of the 3 computed fingerprints confirms it in the numbers yet — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Revenue is customer-capex (new vessels) and swings violently with order inflow and execution stage: FY23 turnover collapsed because no contracts secured in FY22 reached the steel-cutting stage, then FY24 turnover jumped 62% and PBT 156%, and management repeatedly warns current margins are not sustainable (ship repair margin falls away once the two aircraft carriers leave the dock). Revenue recognition itself is lumpy enough that Rs.275-280 crores had to be shifted between hull and outfit stages in one quarter.

> "for revenues to flow in during FY '23, company should have secured orders well prior to the close of FY '22... the ordering was not happening during the latter part of FY '22" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Ship repair is an operations-linked flow business (28% of FY24 revenue, order book up to ~Rs.1,500 crores) and the Rs.21,100 crore multi-year order book smooths the shipbuilding cycle; countercyclical Rs.3,250 crore capex (new dry dock, ISRF) completed and now debt-free.*
