# Honasa Consumer (HONASA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Honasa Consumer is an Indian beauty and personal care (BPC) company that sells everyday skin care, hair care and personal care products — face washes, sunscreens, serums, moisturizers, hair colour and similar items — to Indian consumers under a family of its own consumer brands, the best known being Mamaearth. Management describes it as "India's largest digital-first beauty and personal care company": it started life selling mostly online (over 60% of sales came from online/digital channels as of FY24) and has since pushed hard into physical retail, appointing its own distributors and selling through chemists, modern trade, general trade and fast-growing quick-commerce apps. It makes money the way a normal consumer-goods company does — buying/making product cheaply and selling it at a much higher price, running gross margins of about 70-71%, and spending a large slice of that on advertising and brand building; as marketing and overheads grow slower than sales, the leftover profit margin rises (EBITDA went from roughly 1.9% in H1 FY23 to 9.3% for full-year FY26, with PAT of about ₹200 crore in FY26 on quarterly revenue of ₹682 crore in Q4 FY26). It operates in India, run from a Gurugram head office with a New Delhi registered office, and has repeatedly bought small brands to fill gaps in its range; it runs on negative working capital, generates cash, and paid its first dividend (₹3 per share) for FY26.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Mamaearth** — The founding brand, launched in 2016, selling "clean-label, toxin-free, natural" personal care built around India-specific ingredients like onion, ubtan and multani mitti; it is the company's core and largest brand — the fastest brand in India to reach ₹1,000 crore and a "Top 15 BPC" brand — which stumbled during a distribution overhaul in FY25 and was back to mid-teens growth by FY26.
- **The Derma Co** — Launched in 2020 as a science-led, active-ingredients skincare brand (serums, sunscreens, face cleansers, and newer peptide/stem-cell hair care); management repeatedly calls it the "star" of the portfolio — strong growth, a face-cleanser business that nearly doubled, and the only brand they specifically flag as having reached double-digit EBITDA margins on its own.
- **Aqualogica** — A hydration-focused skincare brand aimed at Gen Z, launched in-house, now being extended into moisturizers; counted among the "young brands" that drive above-average growth.
- **Dr. Sheth's** — An acquired brand (bought in 2022) positioned at the premium end, especially premium serums, used to show the company can grow brands it buys, not just ones it creates.
- **BBLUNT** — The other 2022 acquisition, a hair brand; the calls flag hair colour as one of the categories the young brands are winning in, but give little separate detail on its size.
- **Ayuga** — An Ayurveda and natural-ingredients "masstige" brand targeted at Indian millennials; mentioned as part of the six-brand portfolio in FY24 but barely discussed in later calls.
- **Staze** — A newer brand within the young-brand group that had crossed roughly ₹50 crore annualised revenue (ARR) as of Q3 FY26 and was still growing well.
- **Reginald Men** — A men's skincare brand acquired around FY26 to play the men's grooming trend — described as the most-searched men's sunscreen brand — which doubled revenue year-on-year, crossed ₹100 crore ARR to become the sixth brand in the group at that scale, and also brought the company a Hyderabad-based team to strengthen it in South India.
- **BTM Ventures** — A recent acquisition whose numbers are included in the Q4 FY26 results; the calls mention it only as part of reported growth and give no description of what it sells.
- **"Young brands" (the non-Mamaearth portfolio as a group)** — Management tracks everything other than Mamaearth as one growth engine: it was over 40% of company revenue by Q3 FY25 and grew 25-40% year-on-year through FY26, versus Mamaearth's slower mid-teens pace.
- **Online channels (e-commerce and quick commerce)** — The company's original and still largest route to market — more than 60% of sales in FY24, growing 20%+ in FY26 — within which quick commerce was the fastest-growing piece, rising from 4-5% to 7-8% of sales within a single quarter of FY25.
- **Offline distribution (general trade and modern trade)** — Physical retail — chemists, modern trade, and under 500 beauty-advisor counters (less than 20% of general-trade sales) — which the company rebuilt from FY25 by appointing 150+ new distributors across the top 50 cities; by FY26 direct distribution was about 80% of revenue, stock levels were normalised at 25-30 days, and general/modern trade were growing 25%+.
- **Services business (discontinued)** — A small non-core services line the company was deliberately scaling down in FY24, mentioned only as the reason reported growth looked lower than "like-for-like" growth.

### The story in depth

Honasa's core strength is a business that costs very little to run and turns cash quickly. It keeps about ₹70 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs, every ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.14 of sales, and customers pay roughly 78 days before suppliers have to be paid, so the business is effectively funded by its own customers rather than by capital. That cash engine pays for the two things the company actually competes with: advertising and product innovation. Both show up in the multibagger screen as its strongest results. Innovation Dominance is a strong fit because the high gross margin funds a named new-product cadence every year — Multani Mitti, rosemary and the sunscreen stick in FY24, Tinosorb M filters and peptide/stem-cell haircare in FY26 — with innovation measured at 13% of H1 FY24 revenue and one-year-old launches contributing 7-8% of FY26 growth. Market Share Gainers is also a strong fit, with outside-sourced evidence: shampoo up 50bps and face wash up 100bps in FY24, Nielsen share gains even through the FY25 downturn, Mamaearth into the top-15 beauty and personal care brands, and Derma Co ranked the No.1 sunscreen brand. The runway is real too — 200,000 outlets reached against a 500,000 potential, in a category described as the fastest-growing part of FMCG.

The uncomfortable part is that the same trait producing those wins also produces the biggest risk. Growth is repeatedly credited to trending ingredients and viral moments — Onion, Ubtan, Multani Mitti, rosemary, actives, peptides — and to a Gen-Z content and influencer cadence. That is exactly what makes the innovation pattern work, and it is exactly why Fashion Risk is rated ELEVATED, the only risk above 'watch' in the entire eight-channel review. This is not hypothetical: the flagship Mamaearth lost its aspirational pull and declined for four-plus quarters through FY25, needing reformulation and repositioning, while actives and hydration brands grew 30%+. That same episode explains why three other patterns land only as 'partial' rather than strong. Brand Strength has genuine assets — most-searched beauty brand in India, Kantar Brand Power Index up three straight years, six brands past ₹100 crore annual run-rate — but under a decade of heritage that visibly failed to hold the flagship up. Pricing Power is only a likely fit: gross margins are stable and expanding modestly (71.4%, up 70bps in Q4 FY26), but management credits growth to volume rather than price and never discusses actual price increases. And the quality framework flags the arithmetic behind all of it — sales have grown about 39% a year but lumpily, operating margin sits at about ₹10 per ₹100 of sales, and margins swing by roughly ±120 points a year, which is what a business looks like when its results depend on whether the current trend is working.

Underneath both the strengths and the fashion risk sits the structural problem: this industry has almost no walls. The framework scores barriers to entry and rationality at -2, the worst check in the set, and industry structure is the weakest quality area at -0.83. The pattern screen agrees from three directions — Cost to Replicate is no fit because everything management names as an advantage (content engines, digital-first DNA, formulation playbooks, distributor appointments) can be rebuilt by a funded entrant, and Honasa's own history of building a top-15 brand from scratch proves it; Toll Roads is no fit because nothing here is a critical input or standard; Low Price Plus is no fit because the strategy is deliberately premium. That same openness drives the watch-list risks: New Entrants, with quick commerce resetting metro distribution and legacy players discounting online for six-plus quarters, and Good Enough Goods, since masstige formulations sit in a channel full of cheaper substitutes. Concentration is the other live exposure — a single Flipkart accounting change moved reported revenue by ₹28 crore for several quarters, and Mamaearth at roughly 60% of FY24 revenue dragged the whole company down on its own. Against that, management has done what it said: the FY25 promises to fix general trade and add about 100bps of EBITDA a year were delivered, EBITDA tripled to 9.3%, and a maiden dividend followed. The combined verdict reflects both sides — 54 on business quality, 48 on multibagger fit, 74 on risk safety, for 57 out of 100, a 'Decent' three-star rating with resilience judged sound. It is a well-run, cash-efficient company competing in a category that will not let it build a lasting moat.

*"Out-inventing the competition"*

### Market Share Gainers — STRONG FIT

## What to watch
