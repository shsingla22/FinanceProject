# Campus Active (CAMPUS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Campus Activewear is an Indian branded footwear company — it designs, makes and sells sports and athleisure shoes (sneakers, running/closed shoes and lower-priced open footwear like sandals) for men, women and kids, positioned as premium-looking, design-led product at affordable prices. It makes money by selling pairs of shoes: FY26 revenue was about INR 1,774 crore on roughly 26 million pairs at an average selling price of INR 683, with gross margin ~53%, EBITDA margin ~17.5% and PAT of INR 150 crore. It sells almost entirely in India, through a wide network of distributors and around 23,000-29,000 retail touchpoints, its own ~300 exclusive brand outlets, large-format store chains, and online (Flipkart, Amazon, Myntra, Snapdeal and its own brand.com). Manufacturing is largely in-house — all assembly is done internally, over 90% of raw materials are sourced locally, and it runs shoe-upper plants at Paonta Sahib and Pant Nagar; in January 2026 it extended the brand into athleisure apparel.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Trade Distribution** — Sells to distributors and super stockists who supply tens of thousands of multi-brand retail shops across India (23,100+ retailers in FY25, ~29,000 touchpoints by FY26); management calls this its volume-growth channel and it is the largest route to market, growing ~10.5% in FY26.
- **D2C Online (marketplaces and brand.com)** — Sells directly to shoppers on e-commerce platforms — Flipkart is the biggest partner, plus Amazon, Snapdeal, Myntra and the company's own website — and was the fastest-growing channel in FY26 at ~19% in Q4, helped by performance marketing.
- **D2C Offline / Exclusive Brand Outlets (EBOs)** — Company-owned and franchise Campus stores (about 275 in FY25, steady at 300 by FY26) that, along with brand.com, are used to push higher-priced, premium product rather than pure volume.
- **B2B online / O2O** — Bulk online business the company deliberately shrank; it fell more than 40% at one point and was a drag on the online numbers through FY25 before the base effect washed out.
- **Large Format Stores (LFS)** — Sales into big retail chain accounts, added selectively (one new account in Q1 FY25) as an extra way to widen consumer reach.
- **Closed footwear (including Sneakers)** — The core, higher-priced part of the range that the company says it dominates; sneakers are the newest and most premium piece — entered roughly 1-1.5 years before FY26, doubling volume nearly every quarter, at an ASP of about INR 900-910.
- **Open footwear** — Sandals and similar summer product at lower prices — a smaller portfolio (around 18-22% of sales in the June quarter) that spikes in hot months and pulls the average selling price down.
- **Women's and kids' category** — A deliberate expansion beyond the men's-dominated base, supported by the 'You Go, Girl' campaign with actor Kriti Sanon; the mix moved from 16% of revenue in FY22 to about 21-22% by FY26.
- **In-house manufacturing (uppers and assembly)** — Owned plants — Paonta Sahib now stabilised and Pant Nagar started in January 2026, together making about 2 lakh uppers a month with plans to double by end-FY27 — which management credits for an 80-100 day time-to-market and ~250 new SKUs launched in FY26.
- **Athleisure apparel** — A new clothing line launched in January 2026 to sell more to existing customers and lift store productivity; described as an early-stage extension of the brand rather than a material revenue contributor yet.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Campus Active comes out as a decent but uneven business — 50 out of 100, or three stars, built from a business-quality score of 52, a multibagger-pattern fit of only 34, and a risk-safety score of 64, with overall resilience judged sound. The clearest strengths sit on the demand side. It sells into what the calls describe as the fastest-growing footwear category in India for six straight years, driven by casualisation and low penetration, and it has been pivoting into that shift rather than being caught by it: sneaker volumes roughly doubled year on year, and the women's and kids' mix rose from 16% in FY22 to 22%. FY26 revenue growth of 11.4% came mostly from a 7% rise in average selling price on premium sneakers at around Rs.910, with volumes adding only low single digits. After direct input costs the company keeps about Rs.50 of every Rs.100 of sales, and gross margin is reported at 53.5%. That combination is why the screen marks Pricing Power, Innovation Dominance and Market Share Gainers as likely fits, and why the risk of shifting consumer preferences is rated low.

The awkward part is that the same traits that create those strengths also create the biggest risks. The high gross margin on a product that is not technically complex is exactly what invites cheaper copies — the Good Enough Goods risk — and the record shows that pressure arriving in FY24-25, when the sub-Rs.1,000 economy mix rose to 30% while the Rs.1,500-plus premium mix fell from 46% to 40%, and management chose to absorb wage inflation rather than raise prices. So the pricing power is real but conditional on the cycle, not permanent. Likewise, the design engine that supports the innovation pattern — roughly 250 new SKUs in FY26, 78 new designs in a single quarter, an 80-100 day time to market — is fashion-led SKU refresh with no disclosed R&D spend, and management openly calls itself a fashion-forward organisation crediting recent growth to a celebrity campaign and a trending sneaker shape. That is why Fashion Risk is rated elevated. The advertising spend that built the brand and demonstrably lifted the women's mix from 18.7% to 22% has also been rising much faster than sales, and the brand did not prevent the FY24-25 volume stall. On the moat itself, the record is honest: the barriers that exist — a retailer network of 23,100-plus built over decades, in-house assembly with over 90% local sourcing, and BIS certification that has squeezed cheap Chinese imports — are described as suppressing import competition through enforcement rather than through anything durable the company owns, which is why competitive advantage and industry structure are the two weakest quality areas and the New Entrants risk stays on watch. Profit margins have swung by more than 30 points, a sign of price wars or cost shocks rather than a settled industry.

The financial picture carries a similar tension between building and earning. The company is putting genuine growth money into backward-integrated upper and sneaker manufacturing, with Paonta Sahib stabilised and Pantnagar commissioned in January 2026 targeting 8-9 lakh sneaker pairs a month, and it has integrated forward with exclusive stores rising from about 140 in FY23 to 275 in FY25 to 300 in FY26, plus a deliberate three-year shift to marketplace selling on Amazon and Flipkart for more control. But the payoff is unproven: about 139 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and on the headline measure the business generated only about Rs.3 of operating cash for every Rs.100 of capital employed this year, even as the accounting return on capital is quoted at 22.4% in FY26. Growth has also been lumpy year to year rather than steady, and store expansion was halted during the downturn. Several of the patterns simply do not apply: there is no subscription or renewal stream, demand is created by consumer advertising rather than any professional prescriber, and the product is a discretionary purchase the buyer is visibly price-sensitive about — MRPs were cut 5-6% when GST fell, and management flagged resistance to the April 2026 price increase. The balance sheet is conservative and management has been candid about the FY24-25 shortfall, though an August 2022 promise to hold the growth and margin trajectory was not delivered. Taken together: a genuinely good end market and a capable product operation, sitting on a thin moat, with the returns on the new capital still to be demonstrated.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Fashion Risk. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 2 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.7× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 49% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 2 numeric fingerprints reinforce it.

**What the calls show:** Demand is consumer-discretionary and highly seasonal/weather-driven rather than commodity- or customer-capex-driven: Q1 FY25 revenue fell on heatwaves and lower footfalls, FY24-25 saw a 'challenging market environment' and channel destocking, and an analyst in Feb 2026 flagged 'quite a volatility in terms of the quarterly performances'. Offsetting this, footwear is a repeat-purchase flow product with no tie to customer capex programmes, and management kept marketing spend up through the downturn.

> "we have never compromised over the last 20-25 years... we have continued to invest in marketing no matter how the markets have behaved" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Repeat-purchase everyday footwear (operations-linked, not capex-linked demand); countercyclical continuation of ad spend and capacity build through the FY24-25 slowdown*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±11303 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from -57% to 20% (swinging ±30.4 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 8% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 15%).

## What to watch

- Whether the FY26 mix improvement holds — if the sub-Rs.1,000 share climbs back toward 30% and the Rs.1,500-plus share slips again, the Pricing Power fit and the Good Enough Goods risk both turn against the company.
- Whether cash returns on the new Pantnagar and Paonta Sahib capacity actually show up — the Return on Capital check is currently 3% of operating cash per Rs.100 employed, and the Capex for Growth strength depends entirely on that closing.
- The 139-day working capital cycle and channel inventory levels — any further stretch would deepen the Cost of Working Capital weakness and worsen the cash return.
- How the April 2026 price increase lands given management's own flag about customer resistance — a rollback would confirm the Toll Roads no-fit and the conditional nature of pricing power.
- Any softening of BIS enforcement or renewed cheap imports — the New Entrants risk and the Cost to Replicate pattern both currently rest on that regulation rather than on a company-owned barrier.
- Concentration in the online channel — Flipkart is called the biggest partner and one B2B platform's collapse already caused 40%-plus de-growth, so the Stakeholder Concentration risk could escalate from watch.
