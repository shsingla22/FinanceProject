# Anand Rathi Wea. (ANANDRATHI) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Anand Rathi Wealth is an Indian wealth-management firm (a "non-bank wealth solution company") that helps rich families — high-net-worth and ultra-high-net-worth individuals with roughly ₹5 crore to ₹50 crore to invest — put their money into financial products and manage it over the long term. It does not manufacture most of what it sells: it is an AMFI-registered mutual fund distributor that places clients into regular-plan equity and debt mutual funds and into structured products/market-linked debentures (sourced both from group NBFC Anand Rathi Global Finance and from third parties), earning distribution commissions and recurring trail fees on the assets it looks after. The work is done face-to-face by a large in-house team of relationship managers (about 360 as of mid-2024), and management stresses very low client and RM attrition as the engine of growth. It operates mainly in India, with total assets under management crossing ₹1 lakh crore (₹1,06,300 crore as of June 2026) across ~13,900 client families, FY26 revenue of ₹1,198 crore and profit of ₹386 crore; it has recently started operations in the UK.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Private Wealth / Wealth Management (flagship business)** — The core business: relationship managers advise HNI and ultra-HNI families and place their money in equity and debt mutual funds and structured products, and it is overwhelmingly the largest part — it accounts for essentially all of the ~₹1 lakh crore AUM and ~13,941 client families, with mutual fund distribution alone bringing in ₹494 crore of FY26 revenue.
- **Digital Wealth Management (DWM), a B2B2C business** — A technology-led offering for the smaller "mass affluent" segment, delivering wealth solutions through a mix of technology and human contact; it is small — ₹2,526 crore of AUM and 7,320 clients as of June 2026, with revenue of only about ₹7 crore in a quarter.
- **Omni Financial Advisors (OFA), a SaaS platform** — A subscription software platform sold to independent mutual fund distributors so they can service their own retail clients; it had 6,890 subscribers and ₹1.66 lakh crore of assets sitting on the platform as of June 2026, but is tiny financially (revenue of about ₹1.84 crore in a quarter).
- **Platinum and Gold client segments** — An internal way of grading clients by size, with Platinum being the largest accounts — launched about two and a half years ago and grown from 40–45 clients to roughly 230, with management aiming for 400–500.
- **Stake in Anand Rathi Global Finance Limited (ARGFL)** — A roughly 8% shareholding in the group's unlisted NBFC, which issues the market-linked debentures the wealth business distributes; it is a passive holding rather than an operating line, though revaluing it produced fair-value gains in FY26.
- **UK operations** — A newly started overseas operation announced in the July 2026 call, with no revenue contribution yet.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Anand Rathi Wealth sells and services investments for wealthy Indian families, and it does so with almost no capital tied up in the business. Every ₹1 of assets produces ₹0.94 of sales, ₹47 of every ₹100 of sales is operating profit, and the business turned ₹26 of operating cash for every ₹100 of shareholders' money and borrowings in it. Returns on shareholders' funds ran at 42.3% in FY23 and 46.74% in FY26. The growth behind those returns has been real rather than promised: client assets went from ₹32,142 crore in June 2022 to ₹1,06,300 crore in June 2026, and the number of client families went from 7,400 to 13,941. The end market is still early — equity mutual funds are only about 1.8% of Indian household savings against 28% sitting in gold — and the company's own ambition is a mere 3-4% share of equity mutual funds, so the runway is long. Management has also been straight with investors: it flagged that the ₹1 lakh crore asset target was missed by the 30 March date, disclosed relationship-manager departures with the exact assets retained, and shows both reported and adjusted profit figures. On the framework's own scoring, the business is strongest exactly where you would expect — return on capital and customer benefit — and weakest on industry structure and durable competitive advantage.

The multibagger screen picks up the same engine from a different angle, and four patterns fit strongly. Growing annuity-style trail income on an installed asset base is a stated goal that is working (trail revenue up 70% year-on-year in Q1FY25, with a 50:50 trail-versus-upfront target), alongside a software subscription business that grew from 6,064 subscribers in FY25. Market-share gain is evident in the asset and client numbers above, and it was not bought by cutting price — returns stayed near 47%. Culture fits because promises made on old calls were delivered on later ones: FY26 profit of ₹386 crore against ₹375 crore guided, and 18-19 straight quarters of over 20% profit growth. Cost to replicate fits because management can say precisely why a well-funded newcomer cannot copy it — relationship managers cannot be hired in bulk, 78% of new ones are trained from colleges, and there are 86 team leaders and 19 unit heads all grown from the relationship-manager ranks. Pricing power is a likely rather than a strong fit, and the distinction matters: the company defends its roughly 1.09% fee on mutual funds and 1.17-1.18% on structured products, it does not raise them. Three patterns are only partial (the middleman here is an employee, not an independent party; the fee is a small toll but one set inside a regulated cap; the brand is earned through trust rather than advertising) and three do not fit at all — the company refuses to compete on price, refuses to innovate for its own sake (the same two structured-product designs have run for 10-12 years, with no new fund offers), and is moving toward manufacturing rather than away from it, having sought board consent for an asset-management licence in FY27.

The uncomfortable part is that several of the traits driving those patterns are the very same traits driving the risks. The asset-light, fee-on-client-assets model produces the exceptional returns, but it also ties revenue directly to Indian equity markets — the only risk rated elevated. Asset mix already moved on market values (the debt share fell from 11% to 7%, reasonably attributed to mark-to-market), the structured-product engine explicitly needs 7-8% interest rates and 16-17 realised volatility to work, and operating margins have swung about 6.9 percentage points a year, which the quality check reads as key costs sitting outside management's control. The refusal to offer cheaper direct plans — even turning away a ₹2,000 crore mandate — is what protects the fee and underpins the pricing and middleman patterns, and it is simultaneously the good-enough-goods and shifting-preference risk, because direct plans and passive funds deliver the same underlying product without the fee and management itself concedes the industry is seeing a rising share of direct plans. The stable fee that makes the business look toll-like is set by the regulator, not the company, with a new fee structure effective 1 April FY27 already being probed on the April 2026 call. The internally-grown relationship-manager base is both the reason newcomers struggle and the thing they attack, with rivals offering double salaries; the defence has held, with 80-90% of assets retained when managers left and no regret departures for several quarters. Structured-product supply leans heavily on the group's own finance company (₹1,875 crore of issuance from group companies against ₹312 crore from outsiders in Q1FY27), which an investor asked about directly. Offsetting all this: client attrition is near nil (0.09-0.54%) against rivals losing 4-10%, competitors are weak enough to donate share (portfolios elsewhere returning under 11% on complicated products, two structured-product issuers gone bust), demand is repeat and functional rather than fashion-driven, and the firm went to an all-trail model in 2016, two years before the regulator required it. The combined rating is 70 out of 100 — Strong, four stars — from 68 on business quality, 78 on multibagger fit and 66 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Revenue is AUM-linked and therefore levered to Indian equity markets — AUM swung on mark-to-market (debt mix falling 11%→7% 'reasonably attributed to mark-to-market'), and the structured-product engine explicitly needs 7-8% interest rates and 16-17 realised volatility to work, while FY26 net flows grew only 7% in a weak market. Against that, the flow-like trail/commission base and client behaviour have made results largely market-agnostic: 18-19 straight quarters of >20% PAT growth with a 4.5-4.8% standard deviation of PAT growth, and net flows that rose in the bad June-2022 and Q1FY27 quarters.

> "structured product requires high interest rates... you need a certain degree of volatility" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Trail/operations-linked AUM revenue rather than customer capex; flows rise in volatile markets ('clients... generous to give us more money during bad times'); low measured PAT-growth volatility*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 1 of the last 7 years with swings of ±22 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 28% and 47% (±6.9 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 18% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 4% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

## What to watch

- The new regulator-set fee structure effective 1 April FY27 — the Government Dependency risk and the Pricing Power pattern hinge on the same 1.09% yield, and management has already conceded a possible 2-4 hundredths of a percentage point squeeze.
- Share of client money in direct plans versus regular plans — the Good Enough Goods and Shifting Consumer Preferences risks would break the Friendly Middleman and Pricing Power patterns if the firm's own clients start migrating rather than just the industry.
- Interest rates and market volatility falling below the 7-8% and 16-17 levels the structured-product engine explicitly needs — the Cyclicality risk is the one rated elevated, and it is the flip side of the asset-light, market-linked model behind the Return on Capital score.
- Concentration of structured-product supply in the group's own finance company (₹1,875 crore versus ₹312 crore third-party in Q1FY27) — the Stakeholder Concentration risk; watch whether third-party issuance grows as a share.
- Relationship-manager departures and the assets retained with them — the Cost to Replicate pattern and the New Entrants risk both rest on the 80-90% retention figure and the record of no regret attrition.
- The move toward manufacturing via the asset-management licence sought in FY27 — this is where the Forward Integrators no-fit and the Competitive Advantage score of 0.33 could both change, in either direction.
