# Dynamatic Tech (DYNAMATECH) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Dynamatic Technologies is a Bangalore-headquartered company that designs and manufactures highly engineered mechanical products, chiefly hydraulic gear pumps and complex aerostructures (metal parts and assemblies for aircraft). It sells to tractor and equipment makers on the hydraulics side, and to aircraft makers and defence customers on the aerospace side — Airbus for commercial work, Boeing for defence work, plus Indian programmes with HAL and DRDO such as the Sukhoi, the Tejas front fuselage and the Lakshya drone. It makes money by building parts and assemblies to customer specification under long-term supply positions, often as the sole source, with management calling the pump business a 'door opener' to selling higher-value systems (a Rs 1,800 single pump becoming a Rs 4,500 double pump, and Rs 25,000-30,000 power lift systems) — a shift they describe as going from market share to wallet share. Operations run out of Bangalore and Coimbatore in India plus significant manufacturing in the UK (Swindon and a plant near Heathrow) and Germany, with a very small facility in North America; the deliberate model is handcrafted assembly and engineering in India combined with robotic machining in the UK.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Hydraulics** — Makes hydraulic gear pumps for tractors — management says roughly one in three tractors made anywhere in the world has a Dynamatic pump inside, spanning the world's smallest to biggest tractors — and is now extending into power lift systems and steering; it employs about a thousand workers, has plants in Peenya (Bangalore) and a large UK plant bought from a competitor, and sells UK-made units at around £2,000 (about Rs 1.8-2 lakh) each.
- **Aerospace** — Precision manufacturer of complex aerostructures for both commercial and military aircraft — sole supplier of the flap track beam assembly for the Airbus A318/A319/A320/A321 family, in production on A220 emergency hatches, and recently awarded what management calls the largest order in India's history covering all A220 door variants (passenger, service, over-wing exit and cargo); it is the growth engine management is pointing to ('blue sky above us'), and the aerospace site alone gathers 3,000-4,000 people with families.
- **Defence / military work** — Supplies defence programmes as a deliberate counterweight to commercial aviation — hydraulics for Indian battle tanks and the Arjun, structures from the Lakshya target drone up to about one-sixth of the Sukhoi airframe and the Tejas front fuselage, and defence work for Boeing including the F-15EX; management says this kept the company running when commercial jetliner suppliers went bankrupt during the pandemic, and it holds 90 acres at Sulur Air Force base for further military expansion.
- **Automotive (residual)** — Described as 'the vestiges' of the old automotive business — the company still owns what it calls one of the world's best automotive foundries, located in Germany, and also provides material science capability, after selling its Chennai aluminium, steel and iron foundries and machine shops (which supplied Hyundai, Tata, Renault and Daimler India) because that work had become a commodity.
- **Wind farm (monetised)** — A captive wind farm that supplied the company with non-carbon electricity for 18 years, now largely sold with the proceeds used to pay down debt, though management says a strategic piece has been retained.

### The story in depth

Dynamatic Tech earns its top marks on the parts of the business that are genuinely hard to copy. It has been the sole source on the A320-family flap track beam for 17 years, holds NADCAP aerospace certification, and is described as the only single firm able to cover fighters, commercial aircraft and helicopters end to end. That combination is what drives the strongest pattern in the screen, Cost to Replicate, rated a strong fit, and it is the same fact set behind the perfect scores on mini-monopolies, switching costs, barriers to entry and assurance benefits. Customers are buying certified reliability on safety-critical parts, evidenced by supplier-of-the-year recognition from Boeing, Airbus and Bell and by doors that must cycle 30 to 40 thousand times without failing. The Toll Roads pattern follows from the same economics: an Rs 1,800 pump inside a tractor or a flap track on an A320 is a trivial share of the customer's cost but impossible to fail on. Underneath sits a forty-year founder-led arc, a management team grown from within over decades, and a company-run training school drawing from 55 villages.

The trouble is that the very thing creating the moat also creates the biggest single risk. Being designed in as sole source on specific ship-sets means results are described almost entirely through a handful of named customers - Airbus, Boeing, Bell, Dassault and HAL - and the flagship growth event is one order from one customer. That is the Stakeholder Concentration risk, rated high, and it is the flip side of the same certification lock-in that scores full marks as a competitive advantage. A similar mirroring runs through the growth story. Management describes growth as climbing a value ladder, from an Rs 1,800 pump to a double pump to an Rs 25-30,000 power lift and an integrated UK pump at around 2,000 pounds, which scores well as a mix-driven growth check. But the screen reads that same climb as wallet-share expansion rather than price realisation, so the Pricing Power pattern is only a partial fit, and the risk review reads it as the defensive response to the Good Enough Goods risk, since the company has already exited its Indian automotive machining and foundry businesses once because they commoditised. Cyclicality is rated elevated because both end markets - tractor build rates and aircraft and defence programme rates - are cyclical and management itself flags an unusually synchronised peak plus a demand pause in the UK pump line. Government dependency is on watch given the DRDO origins, HAL, Tejas and Sukhoi workshare, defence orders and even a regulatory mandate credited with lifting pump volumes.

Put together, the picture is a genuinely defensible niche business whose weaker scores sit in the financial and structural areas rather than in the operations. Return on capital scores zero and capital allocation nearly zero across the quality framework, and management scores low despite the strong talent-development evidence, partly because the culture pattern record is mixed with turnover at the managing director level. On the multibagger screen, only one pattern is a strong fit and one more a likely fit; recurring revenue, low price plus, pricing power, brand strength, innovation dominance and culture are all partial for want of quantification, and two patterns - friendly middleman and forward integrators - do not apply at all, since sales go direct to the aircraft makers and tractor makers with no channel of its own. That is why business quality scores 69, multibagger fit only 41, and risk safety 62, blending to 59 out of 100, a Decent three-star rating with resilience judged sound. The story is coherent: hard-won certification and single-sourcing buy durability and stability of margins, but they also concentrate the customer base, and the company has not yet shown the pricing, returns or capital-allocation record that would turn durability into outsized compounding.
