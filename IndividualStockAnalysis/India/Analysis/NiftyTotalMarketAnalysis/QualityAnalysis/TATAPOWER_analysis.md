# Tata Power Co. Ltd. (TATAPOWER) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

Tata Power is an Indian electricity company that generates power, moves it over transmission lines, and sells it to end customers through distribution utilities — the calls describe generation, transmission, distribution and renewables as its core businesses. It runs coal-fired plants (notably the imported-coal Mundra plant, which sells to Gujarat and four other state buyers under long-term power purchase agreements), hydro plants, and a growing fleet of solar and wind farms, while its distribution arms supply households and businesses in Mumbai, Delhi and four Odisha DISCOMs. Around this it has built a green-energy chain: building large solar projects for itself and for third-party customers, installing rooftop solar for homes and businesses, and manufacturing its own solar cells and modules at a 4 GW plant. It makes money mainly from regulated or contracted tariffs on generation, transmission and distribution assets, plus project/EPC revenue, equipment manufacturing margins and, in earlier years, profits from its coal mines and shipping. Management reported FY26 EBITDA of about INR16,090 crore and full-year profit of over INR5,000 crore, with net debt near INR56,000 crore and annual capex in the INR13,000–20,000 crore range.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Generation (thermal and hydro)** — Coal-fired and hydro power stations selling electricity to state buyers, with the 'Mundra' imported-coal plant the key asset — it ran under a government 'Section 11' cost pass-through, was idle for nine months in FY26, and is now covered by supplementary PPAs with Gujarat and four other states expected to keep it running to 2038.
- **Transmission** — Owns and builds high-voltage lines and substations — regulated Mumbai transmission (about INR10,000 crore of investment planned over five years), competitively bid TBCB projects such as the Jalpura-Khurja and Bikaner-Neemrana lines, an intra-state 765 kV line in Odisha, and stressed transmission assets bought through its 'Resurgent' platform; management cites 45% revenue CAGR over four years.
- **Distribution (DISCOMs)** — Supplies and bills electricity to consumers in Mumbai, Delhi and four Odisha distribution companies; the Odisha DISCOMs have been turned around from INR439 crore to INR809 crore of annual profit by cutting technical and AT&C losses, and Delhi has recently benefited from favourable prior-period regulatory orders.
- **Utility-scale renewables (solar and wind)** — Builds and operates large solar and wind farms, both for its own books and as group-captive supply to industrial customers like Tata Steel; renewable capacity was about 6.7 GW in mid-2026 with roughly 2.5 GW being added this year to cross 9 GW, and around 5 GW under implementation.
- **Solar EPC / third-party projects (TPSSL)** — Engineering and construction of solar plants for outside customers on contract — an order book that at one point ran to roughly 3.6 GW worth about INR15,000 crore; margins were squeezed in 2022 by high module and currency costs.
- **Rooftop solar** — Sells and installs rooftop solar (and now rooftop battery storage) to homes and businesses through channel partners, riding the government's PM Surya Ghar scheme; installations doubled in FY26 for roughly INR499 crore of profit, with revenue up about 100% year-on-year and management targeting another 60-70% growth.
- **Solar cell and module manufacturing** — A 4 GW solar cell and 4 GW module factory in Tirunelveli, Tamil Nadu (about INR3,000 crore) that supplies its own projects and outside buyers — now at full capacity with FY26 profit of INR857 crore, more than double the prior year — plus a planned 10 GW wafer and ingot plant to meet a domestic-content rule from June 2028.
- **Coal mines and shipping** — Stakes in coal mining (and associated shipping) that supply fuel and generated 'very high profit' when coal prices spiked in FY23, but which dragged in FY26 when Mundra was not running.
- **New-age / EV charging (Tata Power EZ Charge)** — Runs India's largest EV charging network — around 2,400 public charge points and 240 e-bus points across 437 cities as of 2022 — built out through tie-ups with vehicle makers and property developers; small relative to the utility businesses.
- **Storage and new hydro** — Developing about 2,800 MW of pumped-hydro storage (including a project at Bhivpuri, targeted to start up in early calendar 2029) and a 600 MW hydro plant in Bhutan with Druk Green Power costing about INR6,900 crore, in which Tata Power holds 40% equity and handles construction, operations and sale of the power into India.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Tata Power comes out of the three reviews as a solid, protected, but capital-hungry utility rather than a compounding machine: 57 out of 100 on business quality, 34 on multibagger fit and 58 on risk safety blend to an overall 50 — a "Decent" three-star rating, with overall resilience judged SOUND. The strongest marks sit in two places. First, protection: distribution licences are explicit regional monopolies with near-total switching costs for consumers, backed by a regulated return framework and a court-monitored six-year regulatory asset amortisation. Second, demand: renewables, rooftop solar under PM Surya Ghar (targeting 1 crore homes), pumped hydro, EV charging and domestic solar manufacturing are all in durable, policy-backed expansion. Management also earns a top mark for patience — four years of persistent negotiation turned the Mundra under-recovery into a signed supplementary PPA running to 2038, alongside a patiently built 4GW cell-module plant and 40-year hydro PPAs. Capital allocation and customer benefits score full marks, growth scores well, and the cash cycle is short and healthy at about 26 days. But return on capital scores deeply negative — every ₹100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings produced only ₹5 of operating cash this year — and competitive advantage, industry structure and management as scored areas all sit near zero.

The pattern screen tells the same story from the other side, and the crucial point is that the traits earning credit and the traits failing are frequently the same trait. Moving toward the end customer is the clearest genuine fit: the company owns four Odisha DISCOMs plus Mumbai and Delhi distribution, has done more than one lakh direct rooftop installations by 2024 (doubling again in FY26) and runs India's largest public EV charging network at roughly 2,400 points across 437 cities. The Tata name plainly helps at that consumer end, with management claiming 50-60% share of the quality-seeking rooftop segment, and rooftop growth is credited to channel partners, supply-chain tie-ups and after-sales support rather than direct end-customer marketing. Yet the very licences and regulated returns that create the monopoly also mean prices are set by regulators and auctions, not by the company — Mundra ran at under-recovery until a pass-through was granted, and renewable auction tariffs and falling module prices are set outside the business — so pricing power is a clear non-fit. Likewise the licensed distribution areas, transmission concessions, 110-year-old hydro sites and 40-year Bhutan and pumped-hydro PPAs are things a newcomer genuinely cannot rebuild, but the fastest-growing pieces are not, which keeps cost-to-replicate only partial. Recurring revenue is the opposite of a fit: there is no attach-rate, maintenance-contract or renewal funding anywhere, and instead ₹13,000 crore of capex in FY26 with ₹25,000 crore guided for FY27, and net debt climbing from about ₹42,000 crore in 2022 to about ₹56,000 crore. Leadership stability across all 17 calls and disclosure of bad news, such as the FY26 capex shortfall, support culture only partially, and the company is the reverse of an overlooked idea — a heavily covered Tata group flagship, which the quality framework marks down sharply.

The risk review then names the price of those same strengths. The single highest risk is government dependency, rated HIGH: economics are set by government action across all four years — Mundra ran for years on a Ministry of Power Section 11 dispensation with regulator-set pass-through tariffs and needed state cabinet approvals, while Delhi and Odisha returns hinge on regulatory orders. That is the direct cost of the regulated monopoly that scores best on quality, and diversification across generation, transmission, distribution, EPC, manufacturing and rooftop softens it only so far because nearly all of it sits under the same policy umbrella. Cyclicality is ELEVATED for the same asset-heavy reason the return-on-capital and asset-turn checks fail: FY23 profits were flattered by high coal-mine prices, cell, module and currency inflation hit EPC margins, and legacy losses recurred — with regulated annuity revenue from networks and long-dated hydro PPAs as the offset. Three risks sit on WATCH and each pairs with a pattern. Backward integration into 4GW cells and modules and now 10GW wafer and ingot is what supports the partial low-cost story, but it also commits heavy capital into fast-moving technology alongside pumped hydro competing with falling-cost batteries and exploratory small modular reactors, and it exposes the company to commoditised solar where the defence is a 15% customs arbitrage plus domestic-content protection rather than product difference. Mundra's dependence on five named procurers led by Gujarat left the plant idle for nine months of FY26 pending resolution — the same episode the quality framework praises as evidence of tenacity. The lower risks are genuinely low: demand is functional and repeat-purchase, growing 11% in Q1 FY25 and 8.5% in Q1 FY27, competition in rooftop comes from fragmented small installers, and the one real preference shift in the sector, thermal to clean energy, is one the company has already pivoted into.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Cyclicality. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 8% to 11% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Across all four years the company's economics are set by government action: Mundra ran for years on a Ministry of Power Section 11 dispensation with regulator-determined pass-through tariffs and needed state cabinet approvals for the SPPA; Delhi/Odisha DISCOM profits swing on prior-period regulatory orders (INR783 crore in FY26 vs INR333 crore in FY25) and a Supreme Court-monitored regulatory asset wind-down; rooftop growth is driven by the PM Surya Ghar scheme; solar manufacturing economics rest on BCD/DCR duty structure and a mandated Indian-wafer requirement from 1 June 2028; and the Indonesian coal arm faces changing royalties, corporate tax and possible export taxes.

> "the tariff has to be determined by the regulator... under Section 11 it's a compulsory generation that has been done" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Diversification across generation, transmission, distribution, renewables EPC, manufacturing and rooftop reduces single-policy dependence, but nearly all of it remains policy- or regulator-anchored*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Commodity and capex cycles visibly move results — FY23 profits were flattered by 'very high profit from our coal mines due to higher coal prices' while solar cell/module and forex inflation hit EPC margins, and Tata Projects booked repeated losses on legacy fixed-price contracts through Jul 2026; RE auction volumes peaked in FY25 and 'came off in FY26'. Offsetting this is a large regulated, operations-linked base (transmission, Mumbai/Delhi/Odisha distribution, long-term PPAs of 25-40 years) where demand is a flow product tracking consumption, up 8.5% in Q1 FY27.

> "Due to higher commodity prices, including solar cells and modules and foreign exchange movements, we saw a hit on profitability of the EPC business" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Regulated annuity revenue from T&D and 40-year hydro/pumped-storage PPAs; electricity as an operations-linked flow product; sustained countercyclical capex (INR25,000 crore FY27) with leverage held at 3.4x EBITDA*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 5 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 7% and 23% (±4.3 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 12% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 22%).

## What to watch

- Regulatory and government outcomes — the HIGH-RISK government dependency channel: whether Delhi and Odisha orders and the Mundra supplementary PPA keep delivering the pass-throughs the monopoly-and-switching-costs strength is built on.
- Cash return on capital — the headline return check scored only ₹5 of operating cash per ₹100 of capital; a rising figure as the growth capex commissions would be the strongest single upgrade signal.
- Capex delivery versus guidance and leverage — FY26 came in at ₹13,000 crore against ~₹22,000 crore, with ₹25,000 crore guided for FY27 and net debt at about ₹56,000 crore and 3.3x EBITDA; repeated shortfalls or leverage drift would weaken the partial Corporate Culture read.
