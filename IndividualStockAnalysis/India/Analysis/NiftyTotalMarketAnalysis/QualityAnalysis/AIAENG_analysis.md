# AIA Engineering Ltd. (AIAENG) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

AIA Engineering makes high-chrome wear parts — mainly grinding media (the metal balls that crush rock and raw material inside grinding mills) and mill linings (including the liners at the discharge end of the mill) — cast at its plants in Ahmedabad, Gujarat, India. Its main customers are mining companies abroad (copper, gold and other ores, with South America and the Americas repeatedly named) plus cement and other industrial users, and it exports the bulk of what it makes, so ocean freight, container availability and import duties are a constant part of the story. It earns money on tonnage sold at a per-kilogram realization — roughly Rs. 150/kg in FY23 rising to about Rs. 165/kg for FY26 — on a cost-plus basis where raw material and freight are passed through, which is how it protects margins. Full-year FY26 was about 258,000 tonnes sold, Rs. 4,355 crores of revenue, Rs. 1,744 crores EBITDA and Rs. 1,270 crores profit after tax, with roughly Rs. 4,300 crores of net cash and installed capacity of about 436,000 tonnes.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Mining** — Sells high-chrome grinding media and mill liners to copper, gold and other ore miners, pitched as a switch from conventional forged steel to chrome; it is the larger and strategically central part, about 45,000 of 70,000 tonnes in the March 2026 quarter and roughly 160,000 of 258,000 tonnes for FY26, and management sizes the addressable gold-and-copper market at 800,000 to 1 million tonnes.
- **Non-mining (cement and other industries)** — Supplies the same type of wear parts to cement plants and other industrial grinding users; it is the smaller, steadier and somewhat seasonal part, around 22,000-25,000 tonnes a quarter and roughly 98,000 tonnes in FY26.
- **Mill lining (metal, and now rubber/composite)** — The liners that protect the inside of a grinding mill, sold as an engineered solution meant to raise throughput and cut power use rather than just resist wear; management added a 50,000-tonne metal mill-lining plant in 2022 and a Rs. 65 crore rubber-and-composite liner line (about 20,000 tonnes) in 2024 so it can offer the full suite, and in May 2026 reported its first major discharge-system win at a marquee South American customer.
- **Grinding media capacity build-out** — The core product line, expanded through an 80,000-tonne plant costing about Rs. 250 crores announced in 2022; after closing the 24,000-tonne Welcast Steels subsidiary plant in Bangalore, grinding media capacity stands at 314,000 tonnes of a total 436,000 tonnes, and the company is running well below full utilisation at roughly 250,000-260,000 tonnes a year.
- **Castings (non-grinding-media)** — A separate 120,000-tonne casting capacity whose sales mix moves the blended realization — a heavier weighting of castings was one reason the March 2026 quarter's realization jumped to Rs. 178/kg.
- **Treasury and other income** — Not an operating business but a meaningful earnings contributor: the large net cash pile generates treasury income (Rs. 67-83 crores a quarter recently), alongside export incentives such as RoDTEP and duty drawback and foreign-exchange gains on export invoicing.
- **Planned overseas plants (Ghana and China)** — Proposed manufacturing outside India as a response to rising duties, protectionism and shipping risk; as of May 2026 both were still in paperwork and approval stages with no capex estimate given.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

AIA Engineering comes out as a high-quality but slow-compounding business, rated Strong at 67 out of 100 (76 on business quality, 43 on multibagger fit, 78 on risk safety), with overall resilience judged sound. The foundation is a niche it appears to own: management claims, consistently over years, to be the only supplier that can sell a full design-and-solution package for ball mills, competing on throughput, power savings and recovery rather than on price. The quality framework scores this a full 2 on mini-monopolies, and the pattern screen independently confirms it as the one clear Cost to Replicate fit — the barrier is mine-specific engineering, pattern-making and metallurgy built over decades, plus per-customer trial cycles measured in years, such as the discharge-system win in May 2026 that followed more than two years of work with a single customer. Demand behind it is structural rather than cyclical: falling ore grades force miners to process more tonnage per unit of metal, which mechanically raises grinding media consumption, and the wear-consumable nature of the product means Cyclicality is scored a low risk, with volumes holding in a narrow band (255,000 tons in FY25 against 258,000 tons in FY26) and margins actually rising. Around 28 rupees of every 100 rupees of sales is operating profit, with year-to-year swings of about three points.

The same traits that create the strengths also create most of the risks, and this is the core tension in the file. Because AIA sells a higher-cost chrome product against cheaper forged media and asks the mine to pay a delta for process benefits, the Low Price Plus pattern is a clear non-fit and the Good Enough Goods risk is the single elevated item in the review — the premium has to survive a freight cost that management does not control, roughly $300 per ton against $100 before COVID, which lands directly on top of that delta. The same logic caps the Pricing Power read at partial: realisations did rise from about Rs 120 to Rs 150-plus in FY23 and Rs 165 by FY26, but management itself attributes the move to raw material and freight pass-through, product mix and rupee depreciation rather than to pure pricing strength. The long, custom trial cycle that makes the business hard to copy is also what makes growth lumpy and slow: displacing local forged producers is explicitly the entire investment thesis and scores a full 2 on share donators, yet the Market Share Gainers pattern is only a partial fit because delivery has badly lagged the guidance given in August 2022, and the Growth quality area scores just 0.67. It also concentrates the future in a few names — Stakeholder Concentration is flagged as a watch item because, despite a broad installed base across roughly 140 countries and non-mining cement and utility tonnage of about 25,000 tons a quarter, the growth narrative now rests on a handful of very large mines, including one 15,000-ton South American order.

Management and balance sheet are the strongest part of the record and the reason the risk safety score is high, but they carry their own cost. Candor, disciplined stewardship, out-of-limelight behaviour and long-term tenacity all score a full 2, and the pattern screen rates Corporate Culture a strong fit for the same reasons: the same two executives run every call in plain, unpromotional language, they withdrew volume guidance rather than repeat a number they could not meet when logistics broke, they pointed out the Rs 65 crore currency benefit and the Rs 25-30 crore tax one-off sitting inside record numbers, and they kept a major customer win anonymous and caveated. Capital is handled the same way — purely organic growth, no acquisitions, net cash of about Rs 4,300 crore with no borrowing, capacity built two years ahead of demand and capital spending tapped down when conversion slowed. The flip side shows up in the scorecard: Capital Allocation scores only 0.25 and Return on Capital 0.33, and the cost of working capital is scored minus 2, with roughly 320 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Building capacity early and running a long cash cycle is prudent, but it parks a lot of capital against volumes that have barely moved. Two further items sit in the background: trade policy repeatedly shapes market access through US antidumping and countervailing sunset reviews and Brazilian dumping proceedings in an increasingly protectionist world, though selling into about 140 countries means no single regulator controls the business; and technological disruption is rated low because AIA is the disruptor, not the incumbent, with a self-funded cadence of in-house development covering the mill-lining plant, composite and rubber liners at Rs 65 crore, and the new-generation discharge system. The pattern screen scores only 43 largely because Brand Strength, Friendly Middleman and Low Price Plus do not apply to this model at all, and Recurring Revenue is only partial — repeat consumable offtake rather than any contracted maintenance or licence stream.

## Section 2 — Does it look like a long-term winner? (11 multibagger patterns)

### Corporate Culture — STRONG FIT
*"A culture that compounds"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** The same two managers have run every call since 2022 and repeatedly volunteer bad news rather than spin it — admitting the logistics problem was "far more worrisome than what it appeared", withdrawing guidance instead of repeating an unmeetable number, shutting the loss-making Welcast plant, and refusing to extrapolate the FY26 win. > "I will be very honest, we are not going to hazard any gas... It's wrong to say that, no, there is no problem." — *management, on an earnings call*

### Cost to Replicate — STRONG FIT
*"Could a newcomer copy this?"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** The articulable barrier is mine-specific solution engineering that cannot be bought: design, pattern-making and metallurgy built over decades, plus per-customer trial cycles that take years (the May 2026 discharge-system win came after "over 2 years, we work with that customer... doing multiple rounds of supply"), on top of 436,000 tons of high-chrome capacity. A funded copycat would need both the India-based ecosystem and the accumulated per-mill process data; the caveat is that freight and antidumping duties, not competitor capability, are what actually cap the advantage.

> "We do not think there is anybody else today who can offer the same or similar solution... not the design and not the solution." — *management, on an earnings call*

### Recurring Revenue (Product Upfront) — PARTIAL
*"Sells once, earns forever"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (2 of 3 checks supporting).

**What the calls show:** The economics are consumable-replacement rather than contractual: AIA wins a mine on the liner/discharge-system solution and then rides a repeat grinding-media offtake, as the May 2026 call spells out. But across all 16 calls there is no mention of AMC/licence fees, renewal rates, attach rates or deferred revenue, and working capital is a heavy user of cash (120 days in FY23, RM stock 31→52 days in Q1 FY25), not negative.

> "the strategy is to talk about the solution based on this lining and the discharge system... And then as a corollary, automatically also talk about grinding media, which is a high-volume business." — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ About 320 days of cash is tied up in the trade cycle — customers are not funding the business.
- ✅ Capital spending is about 2% of sales — growth needs little capital.
- ✅ Operating cash was positive in 11 of 12 years — the reliable cash engine recurring-revenue businesses show.

### Toll Roads — PARTIAL
*"Small cost, can't skip it"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (1 of 2 checks supporting).

**What the calls show:** The product is critical to mine throughput and recovery and management insists price is not the axis of competition (Aug 2024: "I do not think it's going to be a price discussion at all"), which is toll-road-like. But it fails the "customers pay without negotiating" test: cost-plus pricing with full freight pass-through, and by Aug 2024 customers were deferring conversions purely because shipping cost and delivery time had become unpredictable.
