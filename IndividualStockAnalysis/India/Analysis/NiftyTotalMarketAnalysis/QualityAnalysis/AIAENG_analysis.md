# AIA Engineering Ltd. (AIAENG) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

AIA Engineering is an Ahmedabad (Gujarat, India) based manufacturer of high-chrome wear parts used inside the huge grinding mills that crush rock and other hard material — mainly 'grinding media' (the hard-metal balls that do the grinding) and 'mill linings' (the replaceable plates that protect the inside of the mill), plus related castings and newer 'discharge system' designs. Its customers are mostly global mining companies (especially copper and gold mines) and, in the non-mining part, cement plants and similar industries, with most output exported from India to markets such as North and South America; management notes the plants are in India but has announced plans for facilities in Ghana and China. It earns money by selling consumables that wear out and must be repeatedly replaced, priced largely on a cost-plus basis so raw material and ocean freight are passed through to the customer — realisation ran about INR165 per kg in FY26 on 258,000 tons of sales, giving roughly INR4,355 crores of revenue and INR1,270 crores of profit. Growth comes from persuading mines to switch from cheaper forged steel balls to AIA's high-chrome solution by showing hard savings — more throughput, better ore recovery, lower power and reagent costs — and the company also earns sizeable non-operating income from a large cash pile (about INR4,300 crores net cash), export incentives and rupee-related foreign exchange gains.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Mining** — Supplies high-chrome grinding media and liners to metal mines (chiefly copper and gold); this is the bigger and strategically central half — roughly 160,000 of 258,000 tons sold in FY26 — and management sizes the addressable gold-and-copper market at 800,000 to 1 million tons.
- **Non-mining** — The same wear parts sold to cement and other industrial users, around 98,000 tons in FY26 (about 25,000 of the 70,000-ton March-2026 quarter); management describes it as steady but cyclical quarter to quarter, with no structural change.
- **High chrome grinding media** — The core product — the grinding balls themselves — accounting for the largest share of installed capacity (314,000 tons of the 436,000-ton total after the Bangalore subsidiary Welcast Steels was shut, removing 24,000 tons); an 80,000-ton new grinding media plant was built out over FY23–FY25.
- **Mill lining (metal, and composite/rubber)** — Replaceable mill liners, expanded via a 50,000-ton metal liner plant commissioned in 2022 and a roughly INR65 crore, 20,000-ton composite and rubber liner line at Kerala GIDC, so AIA can offer a full suite rather than just metal; management calls it the key lever for winning larger combined orders and reported a first big lining 'proof of concept' win at a marquee South American mine in 2026.
- **New generation discharge system** — The newest piece of the lining solution — redesigned linings at the discharge end of the mill that lifted throughput and cut power for a large South American customer after two years of trials; small in revenue today but presented as the proof that AIA sells engineered solutions, not just castings.
- **Castings / other non-grinding media** — Other cast wear products making up the remaining 120,000 tons of capacity; a heavier casting mix in the March 2026 quarter was one reason realisation jumped to about INR178 per kg that quarter.
- **Treasury and other income** — Not a business line as such, but a meaningful earnings contributor — roughly INR474 crores in FY26 from interest on the cash pile (about INR67 crores in Q4), export benefits like RoDTEP and duty drawback, and foreign exchange gains from a weakening rupee.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

AIA Engineering comes out of all three reviews as the same kind of business: a supplier of wear parts that get used up inside a mine's grinding mills, sold not on price but on what they do for the customer's operation. The quality framework's strongest marks sit in customer benefits and industry structure — assurance benefits scored a full 2 because customers are buying certainty about throughput, power savings and ore recovery, and management says the sale turns on trust rather than price. That single trait is what makes the two strongest multibagger patterns fit: Toll Roads is a strong fit because across all four sampled calls the product is framed as a small consumable line item that unlocks a much larger operating gain, and Cost to Replicate is a strong fit because the barrier is not the casting itself but the design, metallurgy, pattern-making and service chain wrapped around it. The economics line up with that story — ₹62 of every ₹100 of sales is left after direct input costs, ₹28 of every ₹100 is operating profit, and margins have moved only about ±3 points a year, which the framework reads as rational pricing with few new competitors. Management scores highest of any area (1.2), with full marks for candor (problems named plainly, guidance withdrawn rather than dressed up, one-off currency and tax gains separated out unprompted) and for stewardship (organic growth only, no acquisitions, roughly ₹4,300cr of net cash, capex and renewables continued through the freight downturn, and a loss-making subsidiary plant closed rather than carried).

The interesting part is that the same traits that create the strengths also create the main risks. Because the chrome product carries a cost premium plus freight over cheaper local forged media, the strategy is deliberately to move customers off price altogether — that is why the Low Price Plus pattern is a clear no-fit, and it is also exactly why Good Enough Goods is the one elevated risk in the review: management has spent four years explaining that the freight-plus-premium gap is what stalls conversions. Likewise, the two-year, mine-by-mine technical trial cycle is precisely what makes the business hard to copy, and it is also what makes growth slow and lumpy. Market Share Gainers is only a partial fit for that reason: converting mines from forged to chrome is the entire stated thesis and management guided 25,000–35,000 extra tonnes a year from Aug-22, but the guidance was withdrawn in Aug-24 on logistics and FY25 volumes came in at 255,000 tonnes. The same lumpiness shows up on the risk side as stakeholder concentration — the installed base is genuinely wide (140 countries, India under 5% of mining tonnage, no single client disclosed), but the growth story now leans on a handful of very large mines, including a 15,000-tonne first chrome order in South America. Corporate Culture and Innovation Dominance are partial for related reasons: the culture evidence is strong (the same two executives across all 16 calls, net cash rising from ₹2,065cr in 2022, buybacks, blunt disclosure) but is set against repeatedly missed volume promises, and the in-house development record is real (composite and rubber liners pilot-trialled internally, then a new-generation discharge system refined over two years) but R&D spend is never disclosed. Pricing Power is partial too — realisation rose from about 120 to 150-plus in FY23 and 165–178 by FY26 with reported margins widening from roughly 25% to 40%, but management itself credits pass-through of raw material and freight, product mix and a weaker rupee rather than pricing.

Where the record is weakest is on how much cash the business ties up to earn its profits. Roughly 320 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, scoring the worst mark in the framework, and for every ₹100 of capital in the business only ₹7 of operating cash was generated this year. Part of that traces straight back to a pattern rather than to sloppiness: selling direct to mines through its own overseas subsidiaries that carry local inventory in Brazil, Canada and South Africa is what earns the Forward Integrators partial fit and what removes any middleman skimming the margin — the Friendly Middleman pattern is a clear no-fit because conversions are won directly with the customer's own metallurgists — but stocking product close to distant customers is also what lengthens the cash cycle. The building of owned plants in Ghana and China extends the same logic, and it runs into the government-dependency watch item, where the Ghana plant is stalled awaiting clearance alongside US and Brazil trade cases and a broader protectionism theme. Set against that, the risk review is the most reassuring of the three: overall resilience is sound, with cyclicality, technological innovation, new entrants and fashion risk all rated low, because the product is consumed in the customer's daily operations rather than bought out of mining capex, and AIA is the one doing the displacing rather than being displaced. That split is what the combined rating captures — risk safety scores 82, business quality 64, and multibagger fit only 43, blending to 62 out of 100 and a Decent, three-star verdict. In plain terms: a genuinely well-protected, honestly run, debt-free business whose returns and growth simply have not yet followed the strength of its story.

### Toll Roads — STRONG FIT
*"Small cost, can't skip it"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence and the numbers agree (1 of 2 checks computable supporting).

**What the calls show:** Across all four sampled calls the pitch is that high-chrome media/liners are a small consumable line item that unlocks throughput, power, recovery and ore-grade problems for the mine, so the discussion is explicitly not about price; management repeatedly frames itself as the niche solution provider in a market where the alternative is commodity forged media.

> "the whole cost and pricing becomes moot because the benefits which we are offering are in a very, very different realm... surely not a price" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ Operating margin 28%, swings of ±3.0 points, median return on capital 21% — good but not the near-monopoly profile of a toll road.
- ✅ Operating margin of 28% moving only ±3.0 points a year — the steady, protected profitability these patterns produce.

### Cost to Replicate — STRONG FIT
*"Could a newcomer copy this?"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** There is a concrete replication barrier repeatedly articulated: not the castings themselves (many can make metal, rubber or composite) but the design/metallurgy/pattern-making/service value chain built on the Indian ecosystem, plus 2-year mine-level trial cycles that a funded newcomer cannot compress; the May-26 discharge-system claim of being the only ball-mill solution of its kind is consistent with a rational two-to-three-player global niche.

> "Material competitors could be offering... composite and rubber, but not the design and not the solution" — *management, on an earnings call*

### Recurring Revenue (Product Upfront) — PARTIAL
*"Sells once, earns forever"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (2 of 3 checks supporting).

## What to watch
