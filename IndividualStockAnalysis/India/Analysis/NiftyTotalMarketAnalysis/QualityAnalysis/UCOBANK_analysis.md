# UCO Bank (UCOBANK) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

UCO Bank is an Indian public sector (government-majority-owned) commercial bank, founded in 1943 and headquartered in Kolkata, that takes deposits from the public and lends the money out. It serves a very large retail base (about 4 crore customers) plus farmers, small businesses and large corporates through a pan-India branch network — 3,412 branches as of March 2026, about 61% of them in rural and semi-urban areas — along with two overseas branches in Hong Kong and Singapore and a representative office in Iran. It earns mainly from the spread between what it pays on deposits (cost of funds around 4.5%) and what it charges on loans (yield on advances around 8%), which management tracks as net interest income and net interest margin (~3%), plus fee-based income and returns on its government-securities/treasury book. It is listed on the NSE and BSE, with the Government of India holding roughly 95% of the shares, and reported full-year FY26 net profit of Rs. 2,768 crore with a 44 paisa dividend.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Deposits (CASA and term deposits)** — The bank's funding side — current and savings accounts (CASA), which management deliberately keeps at 37–38% of deposits because they are cheap money, plus retail and bulk term deposits, with deposits growing about 10–12% a year and management steering away from costly bulk deposits.
- **RAM segment (Retail, Agriculture, MSME)** — The main lending focus and the fastest-growing part — grew about 24–26% in FY26 and now makes up roughly 65–66% of the total loan book, up from around 61–62% in FY25.
- **Retail loans** — Loans to individuals, chiefly home loans (growing ~19%) and vehicle/car loans (growing 70%+ in recent quarters), the largest single driver within RAM at 26–28% growth.
- **Agriculture loans** — Farm-sector lending growing about 25%, driven mainly by gold/jewel loans and self-help group (SHG) lending.
- **MSME loans** — Loans to micro, small and medium enterprises, growing consistently at roughly 19–23% over recent quarters.
- **Corporate credit** — Large-company lending, now about 35% of the loan book (down from 38–39%), which management deliberately pruned where yields were too low and which contributes almost none of the bank's bad-loan slippages.
- **Treasury and investments** — The bank's government-securities (G-Sec) portfolio and market operations, used both for income and as collateral to raise cheap short-term funds in the TREPS market rather than paying up for bulk deposits; a new treasury system (Murex) went live in FY26.
- **International banking** — Two overseas branches in Hong Kong and Singapore plus a representative office in Iran — a small piece of the bank, but used to raise overseas deposits cheaply as a funding source.
- **Fee-based income** — Non-interest earnings from charges and services, a smaller but fast-growing line — up about 32% in FY26 to Rs. 516 crore.
- **Digital banking (Project Parivartan)** — The bank's digital channels — mobile app (153 lakh registered users), WhatsApp banking (20 lakh customers, 49 services in 14 languages), tab-based account opening, and end-to-end digital loan journeys — which had built a digital business book of over Rs. 25,000 crore by FY26, backed by an IT budget of over Rs. 1,000 crore a year.
- **Recovery and asset quality management** — A standing effort to collect on bad loans, which management describes as a core performance driver — recoveries and upgrades of Rs. 2,944 crore in FY26 exceeded fresh slippages, cutting gross NPAs from 7.89% (March 2022) to 2.17% (March 2026).

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

## The verdict: Decent — 52 out of 100 ★★★☆☆

In one breath: the quality framework finds an average business today (52/100); it strongly fits 1 of the 11 patterns long-term winners share; the risk review found 1 high risk (Government Dependency). Weighing those together gives 52 out of 100 — decent.

### The story in depth

UCO Bank scores 52 out of 100 — a "Decent" three-star rating built from 52 on business quality, 43 on multibagger fit and 64 on risk safety — and the single thread running through all three analyses is that this is a well-run bank rather than a specially advantaged one. The strongest marks are all about the people in charge: Management scores 1.17, the highest of any area, with top marks for candor (the bank publishes a guidance-versus-actual scorecard on eleven metrics, discloses stressed accounts above Rs1 crore when peers stop at Rs5 crore, and volunteers unflattering facts such as the negative operating profit in FY23) and for disciplined stewardship (no acquisitions at all, roughly Rs1,900 crore of provisions set aside against an expected-loss requirement of Rs2,500-3,000 crore that is not due until June 2027, and a refusal to write back old provisions). That behaviour is exactly what earns the only STRONG FIT in the multibagger screen — Corporate Culture — where four straight years of beaten guidance and publicly owned bad news are the evidence. The operating results follow the same pattern of steady self-improvement: bad loans down from 7.89% to roughly the low-2% range, credit cost of 0.62%, cost-to-income down from over 60% to 52.66%, and margin lifted from 2.84% to around 3.03-3.10% by shifting the loan book toward retail, agriculture and MSME lending from 61% to 65-66%.

The trouble is that none of this hardens into an advantage a competitor cannot copy, and the screen says so three times over: Toll Roads, Pricing Power and Cost to Replicate are all NO FIT. Deposits are a commodity, the technology stack (Project Parivartan, Murex, WhatsApp banking, the chatbot) is bought in from vendors and openly benchmarked against peers, and the one genuinely unrepeatable asset is the sovereign owner. That shows up in the quality framework as a -1 on returns on capital and a -1 on asset turn — a balance-sheet-heavy bank earning 0.83% on assets with nothing protecting that return — and as negative scores on competitive advantage and industry structure. Crucially, the same facts that make the strengths real also create the risks. Management's refusal to write underpriced corporate loans is what improved the mix and lifted margin (feeding the PARTIAL fits on Low Price Plus and Market Share Gainers), and it is simultaneously the proof behind the Good Enough Goods risk: the managing director conceded that corporates dictate rates to banks, and public-sector exposure shrank by about Rs6,000 crore purely on pricing. The rising technology budget (Rs576 crore, then Rs642 crore, then Rs899 crore) is counted as evidence of innovation and, in the risk review, as a permanent cost of catching up from behind — in mid-2023 the bank was adding only 500-600 daily active mobile users. And 90.95% government ownership is at once the reason a rival cannot replicate the franchise, the ceiling on how independently management can act, and the single HIGH RISK in the review.

Set against that, the risk review is more reassuring than the competitive picture. Four of the eight channels come back LOW: new entrants (across fourteen calls management never once discusses fintechs, small finance banks or payment platforms defensively, and has instead co-opted them through partnerships, merchant QR-and-soundbox and tab banking), shifting customer preferences (the move from branch to digital has been met head-on — over half of fixed deposits done digitally, 66% of new accounts opened on tablets, mobile users up from 14 lakh to 70 lakh in three years, and tablet-sourced savings accounts carrying double the average balance), fashion risk, and the wider stakeholder base of 4 crore customers across 3,412 branches. Government dependency is the one high risk, with policy setting the terms — the RBI's prompt corrective action regime governed strategy for years, RBI circulars created the special vostro and Iran rupee-trade business, and repo transmission drives margins. The remaining risks are watch-list items where the mitigations are the strengths already described: the provision buffer and the granular retail book cushion the credit cycle; the same granularity offsets a lumpy corporate book where AAA names are about a third of exposure. What you are buying, then, is an honest, improving, share-gaining bank growing credit at 16%, 17.7% and 19.4% against 12-14% guidance from a small base, with the loan-to-deposit ratio up from 65% to 80% — but one whose growth is volume-led rather than price-led, whose returns are still thin, and whose long-term resilience the analysis marks UNKNOWN.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** UCO is a public sector bank ~91% owned by the Government of India (95.39% in FY23), whose fortunes are set by policy: RBI's PCA regime governed its strategy for years, RBI circulars created the special vostro/Iran rupee-trade business, repo transmission drives NIM, the ECL framework (June-27 go-live) dictates Rs.2,500-3,000 crore of provisioning, and even the free float depends on a government OFS and the SEBI 25% public-shareholding rule. PSU borrowers are a swing factor in the corporate book.

> "our PSU exposure has come down by around ■6,000 crore... around 8 to 9% of exposure" — *management, on an earnings call*

## What to watch

- Whether growth finally shows up in returns — the Return on Capital area scores -1.0 with ROA at 0.83%, so the Market Share Gainers pattern only matters if 19%+ credit growth starts lifting that number rather than just the balance sheet.
- The June 2027 expected-loss provisioning deadline — roughly Rs1,900 crore is set aside against a Rs2,500-3,000 crore requirement, and closing that gap is the test of the Disciplined Stewards check and the main cushion against the Cyclicality risk.

- Government action on the ~91% stake or on policy — the single HIGH RISK (Government Dependency) is also the only asset behind the Cost to Replicate verdict, so any change cuts both the protection and the constraint at once.
