# Cyient Ltd. (CYIENT) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: IT*

Cyient is an India-based (Hyderabad-headquartered) engineering and technology services company that gets paid by large industrial and technology companies to design, engineer and maintain their products, plants and networks — aircraft and rail systems, telecom and 5G networks, energy and mining plants, automotive systems, geospatial/mapping work and, increasingly, semiconductor chips. Most of its money comes from selling engineering manpower and project work on contract (billed in dollars, euros and pounds), plus a manufacturing arm that builds electronic hardware for customers and a newer chip business that is starting to sell its own chips and intellectual property. Group revenue in the quarters described runs at roughly INR 1,900+ crore (about USD 200+ million) per quarter, with the services arm the bulk of it. It operates globally — India, North America, Europe (including Finland and Portugal via acquisitions), Australia and Taiwan — and grows through frequent acquisitions.

**The parts of the business:**

- **DET (Digital, Engineering & Technology) — reported before FY25 simply as 'Services'** — The core business: engineers designing and supporting customers' products, plants and networks on contract, and the biggest segment by far — about USD 175 million of revenue in a single quarter (Q3 FY25) at roughly a 13-14% operating margin.
- **Transportation business unit (within DET)** — Aerospace and rail engineering work, including for Cyient's single largest customer (an aerospace company); one of the two largest units inside DET and a steady grower.
- **Connectivity / Communications business unit (within DET)** — Helps telecom operators plan and engineer their networks, including 5G rollouts and smart infrastructure — strengthened by buying Portugal's Celfinet and one of the two largest DET units, growing about 5-6% in a quarter.
- **Sustainability business unit (within DET)** — Serves energy, utilities, mining and other asset-heavy industries; described as flattish in the quarters discussed.
- **Plant engineering and consulting (within DET)** — Designing and digitally modelling industrial plants (including 'digital twins') for manufacturers, built up through the Finnish acquisition Citec and consulting firm Grit, which specialises in metals, mining and plant engineering.
- **Automotive (within DET)** — A growth area anchored by one very large customer that management expected to become a top-five client, for which Cyient bought a company outright to onboard about 500 ready-trained engineers.
- **Cyient DLM (Design-Led Manufacturing)** — A separately listed manufacturing arm that designs and builds electronic hardware for customers; around USD 24-25 million of revenue per quarter in FY23, later boosted by the Altek acquisition, and by FY27 running at its highest-ever order book with a book-to-bill above 1.5 and double-digit EBITDA margins.
- **Cyient Semiconductors** — A carved-out chip subsidiary aiming to be India's first large fabless chip company, with three legs — chip design services, custom ASIC turnkey design-and-supply, and its own power chips/IP (ASSPs) — running about USD 17.9 million of quarterly revenue after buying Kinetic Technologies for ~USD 85 million, with an ASIC pipeline over USD 100 million and outside funding from EAAA at a USD 500 million (INR 4,650 crore) valuation.
- **Others** — A small residual reporting segment alongside DET, DLM and Semiconductor; includes items such as the 27.6%-owned associate Azimuth whose profits or losses flow into group earnings.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Cyient's genuine strengths are few but real. Its end markets — engineering research and development outsourcing, rising electronic content, AI data-centre power and India's semiconductor mission — are described as genuinely secular, and the company has pursued its semiconductor build-out consistently for years, protecting those investments even when doing so cost near-term margin. That patience shows up again in how it treats owners: a Rs.720 crore buyback that cancelled 5.76% of the share capital after a sustained fall in the stock, with promoters and key management deliberately not participating. The culture record supports this — the FY23 inventory-valuation accounting error was disclosed without being asked about, and management owned the FY25 miss and its cause. There is also a steady, if modest, innovation cadence: a chief technology officer's office, a semiconductor centre of excellence, USD 11-12 million of AI deal wins in the December 2024 quarter, four patents, seven new gallium-nitride products and an agentic maintenance-and-repair platform. This is why 'Innovation Dominance' is the only pattern rated a likely fit, and why the resilience read is sound.

The trouble is that the same traits which make the growth story exciting are the ones creating the risk. Growth is coming from more people and a weaker rupee rather than from charging more: revenue rose 10.6% in rupees while constant-currency revenue fell 0.9%, and pricing power is only ever described as being available 'in pockets'. That single fact explains three verdicts at once — why 'Pricing Power' is only a partial fit, why the 'Good Enough Goods' risk is elevated (margin is defended through cost-cutting phases and general-and-administrative reductions rather than premium pricing, and rail growth was actually hurt by customers moving work offshore themselves), and why the market-share check scores negative: the digital engineering and technology business has shrunk in constant currency across FY25 and into FY27 despite record order intake, which in a growing market means losing ground, not gaining it. The revenue itself is project-based and lumpy — the December 2024 sustainability decline was blamed on projects ending and deals being pushed out — so 'Recurring Revenue' is only a partial fit, and that same project-budget dependence is exactly what makes cyclicality an elevated risk: this is discretionary spending by customers, not spending tied to their day-to-day operations. Layer on that named single relationships repeatedly move the numbers — the largest aerospace customer driving transportation growth, one automotive win prompting the outright purchase of a roughly 500-person business — and the concentration risk follows from the same lumpiness.

The second connected thread is how the company has chosen to grow and to talk about it. Four years of near-continuous acquisitions — Citec, Celfinet, Grit, the 500-person buyout, Altek, Azimuth, Kinetic at about USD 85 million, and TAO — have been explicitly inorganic and part-funded by debt, with a subsidiary now carrying structured debt of its own, recurring one-off deal costs, and group operating margin falling to 9.7% rather than improving after the purchases. That is why both the stewardship and merger-and-acquisition checks score negative, and it sits uncomfortably beside the shareholder-friendly buyback. The semiconductor venture concentrates the tension: up to USD 100 million committed and an internal valuation of USD 500 million, with the claimed barrier to copying it — partnerships with GlobalFoundries, Navitas and MIPS, the Kinetic portfolio of 250 products and 100 intellectual-property blocks, and the SCL Mohali fab modernisation win — asserted more than demonstrated, which is why 'Cost to Replicate' is only partial. The same venture is anchored in government policy, giving it a government-dependency risk on watch, while management's promotional tone around it costs the company the out-of-limelight check. Talent is a further worry: an abrupt chief executive exit with the managing director stepping in and no internal successor, followed by repeated senior hires from outside. And with about 119 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, the working-capital cycle absorbs money that the acquisition programme also needs. The combined score of 47 out of 100 — 44 on business quality, 33 on multibagger fit, 68 on risk safety — lands the company at Mixed, and the record explains why: real secular markets and honest management, but no demonstrated pricing power, no evidence of winning share, and a growth engine bought rather than built.
