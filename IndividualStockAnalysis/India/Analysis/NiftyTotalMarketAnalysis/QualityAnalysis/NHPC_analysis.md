# NHPC Ltd. (NHPC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

NHPC Limited is an Indian government-owned power producer (CIN prefix "GOI", registered office at Faridabad, Haryana) that generates electricity mainly from large hydroelectric power stations and sells it in bulk to state electricity distribution companies under long-term supply agreements — for example, the Bulk Power Supply Agreement signed for the Teesta-VI project's power with Chhattisgarh State Power Distribution Company. It earns money on a per-unit tariff basis: each project has an approved "levelized tariff" (roughly Rs. 3.92 to Rs. 5.68 per unit across the projects discussed), so revenue depends on how many million units (MUs) its stations generate and on plant availability (PAF). In FY'26 its stations generated 29,619 MUs, producing revenue from operations of Rs. 11,615 Crore and profit after tax of Rs. 3,766 Crore; results swing with monsoon water availability, floods and plant shutdowns (a Teesta basin flood in Oct'23 shut Teesta-V and cut FY'24 generation by 12%). It operates across India's Himalayan and northeastern river basins — Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh — and is steadily adding solar plants in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha and Kerala.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Operating hydroelectric power stations** — The core earning engine — existing stations such as Uri-I, Salal, Dulhasti, Chamera-I/III, Sewa-II, Parbati-II and III, Teesta-V, Kishanganga and Subansiri Lower units, whose combined generation (29,619 MUs in FY'26) drives essentially all of the Rs. 11,615 Crore revenue from operations.
- **Hydro projects under construction (standalone)** — A large pipeline of self-built projects that will become future revenue-earning stations, including the 2,000 MW Subansiri Lower (four of eight units commissioned, Rs. 26,092 Crore spent of Rs. 30,072 Crore) and the 2,880 MW Dibang Multipurpose Project (Rs. 31,876 Crore estimated cost, targeted completion Feb'32) — collectively the biggest use of the company's capital.
- **Chenab Valley Power Projects Limited (CVPPL) — subsidiary** — A subsidiary building three hydro projects in the Chenab basin in J&K — Pakal Dul (1,000 MW, 80% complete), Kiru (624 MW, 82% complete) and Kwar (540 MW, 33% complete) — with attractive tariffs of about Rs. 4.44–5.68 per unit and commissioning targeted between FY'27 and March'28.
- **Ratle Hydroelectric Power Corporation Limited — joint venture** — A 51%-owned JV with J&K State Power Development Corporation building the 850 MW Ratle project (Rs. 5,282 Crore cost, 29% physical progress, targeted November'28) on a turnkey contract.
- **NHDC — subsidiary** — A separate power-generating subsidiary whose stations add to group generation and which pays NHPC dividends — dividend income rose Rs. 121 Crore in FY'24, a meaningful part of the Rs. 1,620 Crore "other income" that year.
- **Stressed projects acquired through the NCLT (insolvency) route** — NHPC buys distressed private hydro projects cheaply and finishes them — Lanco Teesta Hydro Power's Teesta-VI (500 MW, 72% complete, Rs. 9,167 Crore, due Sept'29) and Jal Power's Rangit-IV (120 MW in Sikkim, 96% complete, due Nov'26) — both being legally merged into NHPC.
- **Solar / renewable energy** — A growing but still small side of the business: 1,000 MW of solar allotted under the government's CPSU Scheme (300 MW Karnisar, Bikaner already commissioned as NHPC's largest solar plant; 100 MW in Andhra Pradesh and 600 MW in Gujarat due by Dec'26), plus 40 MW in Ganjam (Odisha), a 50 MW floating solar plant in Kerala and two 200 MW grid-connected solar projects.
- **New projects awaiting clearances** — Roughly 10,000 MW that management said it planned to start in 2026 — Uri-I Stage-II (240 MW), Dulhasti Stage-II (260 MW), Sawalkot (1,856 MW), Subansiri Upper (1,605 MW), Etalin (3,097 MW, called India's largest hydro project) and Kamala (1,720 MW, a Rs. 26,070 Crore JV with the Arunachal Pradesh government approved in April'26).

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

NHPC's genuine strength is that almost nobody else can do what it does. The multibagger screen's one strong hit is Cost to Replicate: river-basin sites are handed out administratively by the Ministry of Power, single projects cost roughly Rs 26,000-32,000 crore, build cycles run 10-15 years, and around 23 statutory and forest clearances stand in the way (Dibang was awarded in FY26 for completion in February 2032). The quality framework agrees from two directions: Industry Structure is the best-scoring area, and the Share donators check earns the top mark because private hydro developers did not merely lose share, they went bankrupt and NHPC bought them out of the courts (Lanco Teesta-VI and Jal Power Rangit-IV via NCLT). The end market is also expanding on purpose rather than by cycle: clean power, an 18 GW pumped-storage pipeline and about 10,000 MW of new hydro starts, which is why Shifting Consumer Preferences is scored as a low risk and Growth scores positively. Spending has followed, from Rs 2,646 crore in H1FY23 to Rs 13,689 crore in FY26, guided toward Rs 15,000 crore.

The trouble is that the very features that build the wall also hold down the returns behind it. Because each plant is enormous, every Rs 1 of assets produces only about Rs 0.10 of sales, and the business generated Rs 4 of operating cash for every Rs 100 of capital employed — the two weakest checks in the framework, and the reason Return on Capital is the worst-scoring area. Because tariffs are set by CERC on a cost-plus basis and locked into 40-year contracts, Pricing Power is a clear no-fit and the Intangible benefits check is negative: when a project costs more, the extra cost is passed into the tariff rather than absorbed in margin. That mechanism turns cost overruns into a competitive problem instead of just an accounting one. Subansiri Lower has climbed from Rs 19,992 crore in November 2022 to Rs 30,072 crore by May 2026, and Parbati-II landed at Rs 6.59 per unit, so the same escalation that fails the Low Price Plus test also creates the Good Enough Goods risk — a state distribution company buying an undifferentiated product will lean toward the cheapest electron, and NHPC is itself selling solar at Rs 2.66-2.68 per unit against its own hydro at roughly Rs 4.4-6.59. That is also the substance behind the Technological Innovation watch flag: the cheaper competing technology is one NHPC already sells.

The risks cluster around the same two dependencies. Government Dependency is the single high risk and it is not separable from the moat — the Ministry of Power allots the basins that keep new entrants out, and the same hands set investment approvals, tariffs and incentives, with items like the Rs 412 crore water cess reversal showing how much sits outside management's control. New Entrants is elevated precisely where that protection stops: in renewables, NHPC bids in open reverse auctions and even acts as an intermediary buyer for 2,000 MW awarded to private developers. Execution is the other soft spot, and it is why Market Share Gainers is only a partial fit and Corporate Culture is a no-fit: Subansiri was promised for full commissioning by March 2024, then March 2025, then May 2026, and by May 2026 only four of eight units were running with the rest pushed to March 2027; the two bankruptcy acquisitions have themselves suffered cost inflation and multi-year slippage. Meanwhile output swings with the weather (FY24 generation down 12%, availability from 88.75% to 77.60%, revenue down 10%, then FY26 up 16%), and offtake often rests on a single state buyer per project, with receivables having once reached Rs 5,854 crore. The combined rating lands at 37 out of 100 — Mixed, with resilience judged strained: a business with a real and durable barrier around it, earning a low regulated return on a very large and still-growing capital base, and reliant on a single owner-regulator to keep both ends working.

## Section 2 — Does it look like a long-term winner? (11 multibagger patterns)

### Cost to Replicate — STRONG FIT
*"Could a newcomer copy this?"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** The replication barrier is concrete and repeatedly evidenced: finite river-basin sites allotted by the Ministry of Power, projects costing Rs 26,000–32,000 Cr with 10–15 year build cycles (Dibang: awarded FY26, completion February '32), 23-odd statutory/forest/defence clearances per project, and 40-year bulk power supply agreements — none of which a well-funded new entrant could rebuild in a few years.

> "The estimated cost of the project is Rs. 31,876 Crore ... the scheduled completion of the project is February '32." — *management, on an earnings call*

### Recurring Revenue (Product Upfront) — PARTIAL
*"Sells once, earns forever"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls are silent and the numbers are mixed (1 of 3 checks supporting).

- ❌ About 83 days of cash is tied up in the trade cycle — customers are not funding the business.
- ❌ Capital spending is about 100% of sales — growth is capital-hungry.
- ✅ Operating cash was positive in 12 of 12 years — the reliable cash engine recurring-revenue businesses show.

### Market Share Gainers — PARTIAL
*"Winning customers from rivals"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (0 of 1 check supporting).

**What the calls show:** Capacity does grow persistently — allotment of the Siang and Subansiri basins by the Ministry of Power (Nov 2022), Nepal MoUs for West Seti/SR-6/Phukot Karnali, ~10,000 MW of new hydro starts promised for FY27 and 18 GW of PSPs at DPR stage (May 2026) — but this is administrative allocation and NCLT acquisitions, never share won against named competitors, and no share figures are ever given.

> "In total, we are planning to start 5-6 projects this year itself, comprising around 10,000 MW of capacity." — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ Sales compounding at 3% a year — below the 12% pace the share-gainer pattern looks for.

### Friendly Middleman — NO FIT
*"The expert sells it for you"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls are silent and no computable check supports the pattern.

- ❌ Operating margin of 45% but swinging ±5.3 points a year — too volatile for a protected franchise.
- ❌ Operating margin moved from about 59% (early years) to 49% (recent years) — margins are ERODING, the opposite of pricing power.

### Toll Roads — NO FIT
*"Small cost, can't skip it"*

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Cyclicality, Stakeholder Concentration, New Entrants, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ⚠️ Borrowings are now 1.3× shareholders' funds (up from 0.7×) — a leveraged balance sheet leaves little room when any of the risks above bites.
- ✅ About 100% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital moderated from about 9% to 7% — lower, but still well clear of the deep slide this check looks for.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Practically every driver of NHPC's fortunes is governmental: projects allotted by the Ministry of Power, CCEA/PIB investment approvals, CERC-set tariffs and PAF/deviation incentives, state water cess (Rs 412 Crore reversal after winning a Himachal High Court case), VGF and CPSU schemes, discom payments governed by Late Payment Surcharge Rules, and MAT credit being discontinued from FY27 forcing a Rs 1,156 Crore deferred tax adjustment. It is fixed hydro infrastructure owned and directed by the state.

> "some of the largest Hydroelectric Projects in the Siang and Subansiri Basins have been allotted to NHPC by Ministry of Power" — *management, on an earnings call*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 2 numeric fingerprints reinforce it.

**What the calls show:** Revenue is regulated cost-plus and electricity is a flow product with stable demand, but generation, PAF and revenue swing materially with hydrology and outages — FY24 generation fell 12%, PAF from 88.75% to 77.60% and revenue 10%, while FY26 rose 16% on new commissioning; meanwhile capex is being pushed to Rs 13,000-15,000 Crore a year into long-gestation projects with large cost overruns (Subansiri Rs 19,992 Crore to Rs 30,072 Crore).

> "generation of 21,773 million units as against 24,619 million units generated in corresponding period of previous financial year which is lower by about 12%" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/operations-linked demand under long-term regulated PPAs with normative recovery ('No, not at all. We are going fine with normative ones'), so swings are hydrological rather than a demand cycle*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 4 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 44% and 62% (±5.3 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 100% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 36% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

## What to watch
