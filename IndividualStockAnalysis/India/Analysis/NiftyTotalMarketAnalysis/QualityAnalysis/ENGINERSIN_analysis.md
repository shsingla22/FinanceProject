# Engineers India Ltd. (ENGINERSIN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: CONSTRUCTION*

Engineers India Limited (EIL) is an Indian project-engineering company that designs and builds industrial plants for others — mainly oil refineries and petrochemical complexes for large Indian energy companies such as BPCL and IOCL (for example BPCL's refinery and IOCL's Paradip petrochemical project). It earns money in two ways: selling engineering brainwork (design, consultancy and project management, where it charges fees and keeps a segment margin of roughly 20-28%), and taking on lump-sum turnkey contracts where it delivers the whole physical project for a fixed price at much thinner margins (management cites about 3-7%). Revenue is recognised as projects are executed, so quarterly results are lumpy and cyclical, and the company works off an order book that stood at a record Rs 15,109 crore as of March 2026 against annual revenue of Rs 3,849 crore in FY26. It works mostly in the Indian domestic market, with an overseas arm serving the Middle East (about 30% of overseas business secured) and an ambition to grow in Africa.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Consultancy and Engineering segment (also referred to as PMC / consultancy)** — Sells engineering design, project management and consultancy services for refinery and petrochemical projects; it is the higher-margin half (20-28% segment profit) and contributed Rs 1,782 crore of FY26 revenue with roughly Rs 7,500 crore of the order book, and management expects it to grow 15-20% a year.
- **Turnkey / LSTK segment (lump-sum turnkey, including OBE jobs)** — Takes full responsibility for delivering complete projects for a fixed price — procurement and construction included — which makes it the bigger revenue line (Rs 2,067 crore in FY26, about Rs 5,000 crore of order book) but at low margins of roughly 3-7%, and it is the main reason revenue and margins swing sharply between quarters.
- **Overseas / international business (Middle East, Africa)** — Serves clients outside India, with the Middle East accounting for about 30% of business secured overseas; management flagged a slowdown there in FY26 with work shifting toward repair and revamp of damaged facilities rather than new greenfield projects, while Africa is being targeted for growth.
- **CEIL (subsidiary) and other investments such as RFL** — EIL holds stakes in other companies whose earnings show up only in consolidated results — subsidiary CEIL earned Rs 24.71 crore in FY26, and consolidated profit of Rs 691.59 crore exceeded standalone profit of Rs 638 crore; an earlier call also cited a Rs 13.85 crore contribution from RFL that had previously been loss-making.

### The story in depth

Engineers India comes out of the three reviews as a financially sturdy business whose best qualities sit in how it is funded rather than in how much it earns. The strongest single check is the cost of working capital, where cash comes back from customers a full 457 days before suppliers have to be paid, so clients effectively finance the business; that sits alongside a debt-free balance sheet with roughly INR1,200 crore of cash, purely organic growth with no acquisitions, and a dividend that has climbed from an assured 60% payout in FY23 to a full 100% payout (Rs 5 on Rs 5 face value) in FY26. The customer-benefits area scores full marks and capital allocation is close behind, and after direct input costs the company keeps about Rs 89 of every Rs 100 of sales. But return on capital scores zero and management scores 0.2 of 1, and the reason is visible in the risk review: this is a people-based project business whose revenue is lumpy and whose margins are contractually banded, not a compounding machine. The overall rating lands at 41 out of 100 — Mixed, two stars — built from 61 on business quality, 18 on multibagger fit and 32 on risk safety, with resilience judged sound.

The patterns and the risks are largely two readings of the same facts. Brand strength is the only pattern rated a likely fit, and it rests on an institutional reputation in hydrocarbon engineering that wins nominated, critical mandates such as Euro-VI and refinery energy optimisation without open tender — the very same evidence that makes the new-entrant risk low. Market share gainers is a partial fit on a real trajectory: the order book has gone from INR8,800 crore in August 2022 to a record INR15,109 crore in March 2026, with inflow around INR8,000 crore two years running and genuine international wins in the Dangote refinery expansion, record Abu Dhabi bookings and a five-plus-three-year Saudi Aramco agreement. That international build-out, which scores a maximum 2 on geographical expansion, is also the main defence offered against the high government-dependency risk, because it moves revenue away from PSU tender cycles and Indian policy timing. The catch is that the same win creates a different exposure: the single Dangote order equals roughly 40% of FY26 inflow, which is exactly why stakeholder concentration is flagged as a watch item. Toll roads and cost to replicate are only partial fits — fees of about INR100 crore on an INR2,500–3,000 crore bioethanol plant are small and on the critical path, and rock and salt cavern work has only a handful of global players, but the underlying asset is people rather than infrastructure. Pricing power is a clear no fit: awards come through competitive PSU tendering with a stated 20–25% hit rate and management declines to claim cost-plus terms. Consultancy margins have nonetheless held in the guided 20–25% band for four years, which is why the good-enough-goods risk is rated low even though pricing power is absent.

The main caution is timing and the quality of the current numbers. Cyclical market growth scores negative because the company is at the top of its cycle, not the bottom — all-time-high order book, record FY26 revenue and profit — while management itself flags a Middle East greenfield slowdown ahead, and cyclicality is rated a high risk with revenue tied almost entirely to customers' capex programmes. The halo-effect check also scores negative: roughly a third of third-quarter profit came from a single penalty-provision reversal, and the order surge reflects government and PSU capex timing rather than proven operating improvement. That same lumpiness is why recurring revenue is a clear no fit — there is no licence, maintenance or subscription stream, only project backlog. Culture is judged partial for the same double-sided reason: leadership is long-tenured, disclosure is candid on provisions, JV shutdowns and the Middle East slowdown, and calls are run by functional officers with no promotional narrative, yet there is a persistent record of missed guidance. On the demand side, both technological innovation and shifting consumer preferences are rated elevated because the franchise is built on hydrocarbon process engineering against a durable decarbonisation shift; the response — green hydrogen, coal gasification, salt caverns through a technology alliance, the Chempolis bamboo bioethanol partnership — earns a positive research-and-development check and produced real orders such as the roughly INR300 crore NRL biofuel award, but no R&D spend is ever disclosed and by November 2024 management conceded these efforts had produced little so far. That is why innovation dominance stays partial: the hedge exists and is monetising in small amounts, but it is not yet large enough to offset the core.

## What to watch

- Dangote concentration (Stakeholder Concentration risk): whether inflow after the roughly 40%-of-FY26 Dangote order can be replaced, or whether one client's timetable starts driving results.
- Order inflow run-rate (Market Share Gainers pattern and Cyclical market growth check): whether the ~INR8,000 crore annual inflow holds once the flagged Middle East greenfield slowdown arrives, or the record INR15,109 crore book starts shrinking.
