# Devyani Intl. (DEVYANI) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Devyani International (DIL) runs fast-food restaurants and coffee shops — it does not own most of the brands it sells under, but operates them as a franchisee, mainly KFC and Pizza Hut (Yum! brands) plus Costa Coffee, alongside its own brands like Vaango and Biryani By Kilo. It makes money by selling food and beverages directly to walk-in diners, takeaway and delivery customers, so revenue is essentially store count times average daily sales per store; the calls track gross margin (~69-71%), 'brand contribution' margin per brand, and pre-IndAS EBITDA. Its main market is India — 215 cities as of Aug 2022, expanding into non-metro towns, food courts and airports — with international operations in Nepal, Nigeria and Thailand (Thailand KFC acquired in Jan 2024). The network grew from 1,008 stores in June 2022 to 2,256 stores at the end of FY26, with consolidated quarterly revenue of about INR 1,441 crore in Q3 FY26; a merger with Sapphire Foods is proposed, targeted for completion by end of FY27.

**The parts of the business:**

- **KFC (India)** — Fried-chicken quick-service restaurants and the company's biggest and healthiest business — 783 stores and INR 586 crore of revenue in Q4 FY26 with 17.0% brand contribution margin, and management calls it the anchor of growth.
- **Pizza Hut (India)** — Pizza restaurants and delivery, the problem child of the portfolio — 639 stores at end-FY26 with same-store sales down 3.7% and slightly negative brand contribution, so management has stopped net store additions and is shutting loss-making outlets.
- **Franchise Brands (Costa Coffee and others)** — Coffee shops run under franchise, a much smaller line — 192 Costa stores and INR 45 crore of quarterly revenue in Q1 FY25, growing about 3% year-on-year in Q4 FY26 with a 16.1% brand contribution margin.
- **Own Brands (Vaango, Biryani By Kilo, Goila Butter Chicken)** — Indian-cuisine brands the company owns outright, acquired via Sky Gate Hospitality — small but improving, reaching break-even brand EBITDA in Q3 FY26 ahead of guidance and now expanding through smaller 'Express' formats.
- **International business (Thailand, Nepal, Nigeria)** — KFC and related stores outside India — 363 stores and INR 390 crore of quarterly revenue in Q1 FY25, with Thailand the growth engine and Nigeria a drag from currency devaluation (an INR 22.5 crore loss in Q1 FY25).
- **Institutional / high-footfall business** — Outlets in food courts, airports and similar captive locations, described as a focus area for making the brands more accessible rather than as a separately sized segment.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Devyani runs about 2,256 restaurants across four countries under brands it licenses rather than owns — KFC, Pizza Hut and Costa Coffee from franchisors, alongside its own Vaango, Biryani by Kilo, Goila Butter Chicken and Sanook Kitchen. Two genuine financial strengths stand out. Customers pay roughly 89 days before suppliers have to be paid, so the customer base effectively funds day-to-day operations, a rare advantage scored at the top of the quality framework. And after direct food costs the company keeps about ₹68 of every ₹100 of sales, with gross margins running 69-77%, which shows customers are willing to pay up for the brands. Together these produced a 16% cash return on the capital employed this year. Expansion has also been handled competently: over 215 cities, smaller towns now making up 52% of the core brands, Thailand growing 19% and Nepal 46% year-on-year, with Nigeria the one persistent drag from currency. Customer Benefits is the strongest quality area at +0.75; Return on Capital is positive at +0.33.

The trouble is that the same traits producing the promising patterns are also producing the elevated risks. Store count went from 1,008 in June 2022 to 2,256 by March 2026, which is what makes the Market Share Gainers pattern look like a fit — but that growth is store-count-led rather than share-led, with roughly ten quarters of falling same-store sales, admitted cannibalisation, and each new store costing about ₹1.25-1.3 crore while KFC's average daily sales per store fell from ₹127,000 to around ₹90,000. So the customer-funded balance sheet and fat gross margins are financing an expansion whose incremental returns are deteriorating, which is why the growth capex check scores negative. The same doubling applies to pricing: the Pricing Power pattern rests almost entirely on FY23, when a 9% KFC price rise was absorbed with sales per store at record highs, after which the company moved to ₹79 pizzas, value layers and 'disruptive value' — that retreat is exactly what the Good Enough Goods and Low Price Plus readings describe, defending share on price in weak demand. The franchise licences are the clearest case of one trait cutting both ways: the Yum! territorial development agreement plus 2,256 built-out prime sites is the durable barrier behind the Cost to Replicate pattern, and it is also the single biggest concentration risk, since the brands are licensed not owned, royalties are payable, Yum! approval governs expansion, and analysts have questioned the store-opening commitments baked into the agreement. Brand pull is real at KFC (4.9% same-store growth and 15% overall growth in the most recent quarter) but failed the durability test at Pizza Hut, which lost consumer relevance over 12-18 months, absorbed stepped-up advertising that did not translate into sales, saw brand contribution fall to around zero or negative, added no net new units in either CY2025 or CY2026, and is now closing loss-making stores.

The bigger structural issue is that the store estate was built for one kind of demand and consumers moved to another. Management now describes online and offline as two different businesses with different pricing, says it must manufacture a reason for customers to walk into a store, and concedes the company's installed technology stack is behind peers — prompting a CTO hire, an outside IT partnership and the DIL Commerce platform. That single shift explains three of the risk ratings at once: delivery aggregators as new entrants (elevated), the channel shift in consumer preference, and the technology gap. It also explains why heavy dine-in capex and negative same-store sales have coincided for so long. Management's record is genuinely mixed rather than poor — long-tenured leadership, unusually candid disclosure of bad news including the technology lag and the loss-making stores, and Biryani by Kilo reaching breakeven ahead of guidance — but deal-making has escalated from small add-ons to a Thailand business with structurally lower gross margins, loss-making acquisitions, and now a merger with Sapphire Foods creating a 3,000-plus store group, which is a large amount of integration on top of an already strained operating base. The framework rates competitive advantage at -0.67 and industry structure at -0.5, no pattern earns a clear fit (three are 'likely' with material caveats, seven partial, one no fit), and the company is the opposite of overlooked, being covered by seven brokerages with a headline billion-dollar merger. The arithmetic lands at 45 out of 100 — a Mixed, two-star rating — with resilience judged STRAINED. The investment case therefore depends on things not yet visible in the numbers: that the merger delivers scale benefits, the delivery build-out works, and same-store sales turn.

- ✅ Operating margin moved from about 4% (early years) to 16% (recent years) — the widening-profitability signature of pricing power / lock-in.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

### Stakeholder Concentration — ELEVATED
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but the only computed fingerprint does not confirm it — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Devyani is essentially a franchisee: KFC and Pizza Hut (Yum!) plus Costa Coffee licences drive the overwhelming majority of revenue, with development agreements dictating store-addition commitments; analysts questioned whether the Yum! agreement's store-opening incentives had been relaxed, and management said the KFC strategy must be presented to "the parent organization" for approval. ### New Entrants — ELEVATED
*"A new player changes the rules"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but none of the 2 computed fingerprints confirms it in the numbers yet — held one step below the top severity.

### Cyclicality — WATCH
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Demand is consumption/flow-based rather than tied to any customer's capex, but the calls show a long discretionary-spend downcycle (5–6 quarters of cautious consumer, then ten-plus quarters of negative SSSG) coinciding with the company's own aggressive store build — 1,008 stores in 2022 to 2,279 by Dec 2025 — with management conceding cannibalization and now shutting loss-making Pizza Huts; input costs (chicken, edible oil, coffee, cocoa) and the Nigerian naira added further swings.

## What to watch

- Same-store sales and KFC's average daily sales per store: a sustained return toward the old ₹127,000 from ~₹90,000 would rescue both the Pricing Power pattern and the negative growth-capex check.
- Pizza Hut: any resumption of net new units and a return of brand contribution above zero would ease the Fashion Risk and the negative advertising-and-promotion check.
- Sapphire Foods merger execution — margin, store closures and integration costs — is the live test of the negative merger-and-acquisition check and of the store-count-led Market Share Gainers pattern.
- Terms of the Yum! development agreement, especially store-opening commitments and royalties, since Stakeholder Concentration is the highest-rated risk and the licensed moat behind Cost to Replicate rests on it.
- Whether DIL Commerce, the CTO hire and the app rebuild close the technology gap, which is what the elevated New Entrants risk and the Technological Innovation risk both hinge on.
- Whether the 89-day negative working capital cycle and 69-77% gross margins hold through the merger — they are the top-scoring strength and the thing funding everything else.
