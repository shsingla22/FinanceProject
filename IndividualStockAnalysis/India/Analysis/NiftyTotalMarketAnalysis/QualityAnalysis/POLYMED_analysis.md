# Poly Medicure (POLYMED) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Poly Medicure (Polymed) is an India-based manufacturer of single-use medical devices — things like infusion/IV sets and needles, dialysis consumables and machines, and more recently heart stents, balloons and orthopaedic implants — which it sells to hospitals and distributors rather than to patients directly. It makes money by producing these devices in volume at its own plants (Faridabad and IMT in Haryana, a Jaipur SEZ unit, plus new sites at Palwal, Haridwar, Mitrol and YEIDA Medical Park) and selling them in India and in over a hundred-odd overseas markets; management quotes device volumes in the crores of units per quarter (32 crore devices in Q3 FY25). Exports were roughly two-thirds of sales in 2022 and remained the larger half through FY26, split between Europe and 'rest of world', with the US still small and dependent on FDA clearances; domestic sales had risen to about 32% of consolidated revenue by FY26, of which ~90% is private hospitals and only ~10% lower-priced government tenders. Consolidated revenue was INR1,875 crores in FY26 (up 12.3%) with operating EBITDA margins in the mid-20s, and the company is deliberately shifting from simple, low-technology plastic disposables toward higher-technology, higher-margin categories, partly by buying companies abroad (PendraCare and Citieffe in Europe, Medyneo in Brazil).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Infusion Therapy (core business)** — The original and still largest business — IV cannulas, infusion sets and related vascular-access disposables — which was about 70% of revenue in the first nine months of FY25 but had fallen to 50% of Q4 FY26 revenue as newer lines grew; management calls it the cash-generating core that funds investment in the new segments.
- **Renal / Dialysis** — Sells dialysis machines plus the consumables that go with them, growing fast off a small base — about 8-9% of revenue in FY25 rising to ~11% by Q4 FY26 (INR133 crores in 9M FY26, expected ~INR180 crores for the full year), with roughly 450 machines placed in FY26 for an installed base of ~1,000 and a claimed ~10% share of the Indian dialysis market, targeting 15-17%.
- **Cardiology** — A newer, high-technology line launched around FY25 with a CDSCO-approved drug-eluting stent, followed by drug-eluting balloons and an intravascular lithotripsy system approved by DCGI in FY26; roughly 11,000 stents were deployed in FY26 and a 2,000-patient clinical registry is under way, and the acquisition of Dutch/European maker PendraCare was made to strengthen this area.
- **Critical Care** — One of two brand-new divisions started around FY25 alongside cardiology, with about 60 sales people added at launch; management said it would take two to three years to scale, and by Q4 FY26 it was counted among the new higher-technology segments that together exceed 50% of revenue. (Management once referred to the pair of new divisions as 'oncology and critical care'.)
- **Orthopedics** — The most recent therapeutic area, entered in FY26 through the acquisition of Italy's Citieffe Group; it is still being integrated, with cross-selling into India awaiting import registrations that take six to nine months.
- **International business (Europe, rest of world, US)** — Exports are the bigger half of sales — INR342 crores in Q3 FY26, with Europe INR162 crores (+25.7%) and rest of world INR181 crores (+9.5%) — and management says the European acquisitions give a 'Made in Europe' advantage, while the US has been slow due to FDA timelines and tariffs and Brazil is being built out via the Medyneo acquisition and a local subsidiary.
- **Domestic business** — About 32% of consolidated revenue by FY26 and growing ~16-20% a year, sold mainly to private hospitals (~90%) through a direct sales force that management has been expanding aggressively (80-90 reps added in nine months of FY26); low-priced, slow-paying government tender business is being deliberately shrunk toward 6-7%.

### The story in depth

Poly Medicure comes across as a genuine product company that has been quietly compounding. Sales have grown roughly 15% a year with unusually steady year-to-year consistency, operating profit runs at about ₹24 on every ₹100 of sales with margins moving only about ±2.3 points a year, and after direct input costs the company keeps about ₹68 of every ₹100 of sales. Behind those numbers sit two of the strongest findings in the whole record: Innovation Dominance and Corporate Culture, both rated a strong fit. The patent count has climbed to 334, a 50-product pipeline turned into roughly 35 launches in FY26, and drug-eluting stents, drug-eluting balloons and intravascular lithotripsy were developed in-house in categories where more than 90% of Indian demand is currently imported. The same family leadership (Himanshu and Rishi Baid) with the same CFO has run the business across all sixteen calls reviewed, sets targets publicly and then scores itself against them out loud — 350 dialysis installations against a promised 500, 450 against 500-600, infusion called 'a laggard', a US setback, a missed PLI qualification. That candor scored the maximum on the quality framework, and it is why the rest of the record can be read at face value. The end markets are expanding for structural reasons too: Indian medical devices moving from unregulated to regulated, import substitution in cardiology, China+1 sourcing shifts, and rising dialysis and angioplasty volumes.

The interesting part is how often one trait shows up as both a strength and a threat. The core infusion portfolio is cheap, simple, certified and repeat-purchase — that is what supports the Toll Roads pattern and the 68% gross margin, and it is also exactly the profile the 'Good Enough Goods' risk attacks, which the review rates ELEVATED. Chinese suppliers with a structurally lower cost base are named across the FY26 calls as the reason renal growth guidance was cut from 30-35% to about 20% and why infusion exports turned slightly negative, so New Entrants is ELEVATED as well; management itself calls the core disposables 'low technology'. The company's answer to that pressure is the same India cost leverage that earns it the 'Low Price Plus' pattern — dialysis machines priced 5-7% below multinationals, components brought in-house, automation to offset wage rises — which means it is fighting a price war with a price weapon in a market management repeatedly says is not a rational oligopoly. The escape route is moving up into high-end cardiology, but the Technological Innovation risk notes those categories are 'completely dominated by multinational companies' and that Polymed is partly buying its way in through PendraCare and Citieffe. Similarly, the push toward the customer that earns the Forward Integrators and Market Share Gainers patterns — the Brazil subsidiary, the $40,000 Medyneo purchase for its licences, 80-90 new sales reps, stepping back from low-price government tenders — is the same export-led model that creates the Stakeholder Concentration risk, with roughly 68-70% of revenue running through a limited set of large distributors and OEM accounts, where destocking at existing European customers alone was enough to drag standalone results.

Where the record is weakest is what all this growth costs in cash. Asset turnover scores negative — every ₹1 of assets produces ₹0.48 of sales — and the model is described as getting heavier still, with several plants being built at once at Palwal, Mitrol and Haridwar plus land at YEIDA, ₹296 crore of capex in FY26, and a large idle cash pile. About 294 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Headline return on capital came in at just ₹7 of operating cash per ₹100 of capital this year, and capital allocation is the one quality area scoring below zero (-0.4). The spending is being made at roughly 24% return on capital employed and is aimed at growth rather than maintenance, but the returns are unproven: standalone revenue grew only 4% in FY26 despite all of it. The dialysis platform is the clearest test — machines placed pull consumables, which is a real razor-and-blade setup, yet across sixteen calls management never discusses attach rates, service revenue share or renewals, which is why Recurring Revenue is only a partial fit. Netting it out, the combined rating is 74 out of 100 — Strong, four stars — built from 63 on business quality, 95 on multibagger pattern fit and 68 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged SOUND. In plain terms: an unusually honest, genuinely inventive company with excellent end markets and a demonstrated ability to launch products, currently spending heavily and tying up a lot of cash while low-cost competition presses on the part of the business that pays for everything else.

## What to watch

- Whether the heavy build-out converts into sales — capex for growth was flagged as unproven when standalone revenue grew only 4% in FY26 despite ₹296 crore of spending; another flat year would turn the negative Asset turn and 7% cash Return on capital into the main story.
- The 294-day working capital cycle and the idle cash pile — if the Cost of working capital check does not improve as the new plants at Palwal, Mitrol and Haridwar come on stream, the Capital Allocation score stays the binding constraint.
- Chinese competition in the core disposables — the ELEVATED New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks already cut renal guidance from 30-35% to ~20% and pushed infusion exports slightly negative; further margin or share loss would undercut the Toll Roads and Pricing Power patterns at once.
- Evidence that the dialysis installed base actually annuitises — Recurring Revenue is only PARTIAL because attach rates, service revenue share and renewals have never been disclosed; management starting to report them (or not) is the tell.
- Export customer concentration — with 68-70% of revenue through a limited set of distributors and one named large US distributor slated to carry the FDA portfolio, any repeat of the European destocking episode would test the Stakeholder Concentration risk and the Market Share Gainers pattern together.
- Whether the bought-in high-end cardiology push holds — the Technological Innovation risk notes IVL, drug-eluting balloons and stents are dominated by multinationals; slippage here removes the escape route from the low-technology core and would weaken the Innovation Dominance verdict.
