# SJVN Ltd. (SJVN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

SJVN Limited is an Indian government-owned power generator — a joint venture of the Government of India and the Government of Himachal Pradesh, headquartered in Shimla and listed on the NSE and BSE. It builds and runs electricity-generating stations (mainly hydro, plus solar, wind and now one coal-based thermal plant) and sells the power under long-term power purchase agreements, typically 25 years, to state distribution companies such as Punjab State Power Corporation, Uttar Pradesh Power Corporation, Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam, Assam Power Distribution Company and the state of Bihar. Revenue comes from selling units of electricity at tariffs that are either set by the regulator CERC for the older hydro stations (with periodic 'truing up' orders that can add back earlier-year revenue) or fixed per unit in competitively bid contracts (for example Rs 2.65, Rs 2.88 and Rs 3.92 per unit for various solar projects), topped up by incentive income for keeping plants highly available, deviation-settlement (UI) income, late-payment surcharges from customers and bank interest. It operates across many Indian states — Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Assam, Madhya Pradesh — and abroad in Nepal, growing from 2,377 MW installed in early 2024 to 4,196.5 MW across 15 projects by March 2026, with a stated ambition of 25,000 MW by 2030 and 50,000 MW by 2040.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Hydro power** — The original and still core business — large run-of-river hydro stations led by the 1,500 MW Nathpa Jhakri plant and the 412 MW Rampur plant, which together produced 9,614 of the company's 13,302 million units in FY26 and earn regulator-set tariffs plus availability incentives; four more hydro projects totalling about 1,558 MW are under construction.
- **Solar (largely through SJVN Green Energy Limited, SGEL)** — The fastest-growing part — a wholly owned renewables subsidiary that wins solar projects through competitive bidding and government (CPSU) schemes and sells the output to state discoms; it added 1,070 MW in FY26 alone, including the flagship 1,000 MW Bikaner project in Rajasthan, and management has discussed listing 10–20% of SGEL to the public via a QIP.
- **Thermal power (Buxar)** — A newly entered segment consisting of the 1,320 MW coal-based Buxar plant in Bihar, whose first 660 MW unit began commercial operation in November 2025 and second unit was synchronised in May 2026; fully running it will generate about 9,828 million units a year, 85% of it going to Bihar — it was the company's biggest capex item for several years.
- **Wind power** — A small legacy piece, essentially the 47.6 MW Khirvire wind project, grouped with solar in early-year generation reporting and dwarfed by the hydro and solar businesses.
- **Transmission lines** — Two operating transmission lines that carry power from the generating stations; they are mentioned in every project-portfolio summary but are never given separate revenue figures.
- **Renewable Energy Implementing Agency (REIA) role** — An agency business, not an ownership business — the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy appointed SJVN to run tenders that match private developers with power buyers toward India's 500 GW-by-2030 target; cumulatively it has floated 19.8 GW of tenders and awarded 16 GW across solar, wind, hybrid and storage, including acting as bid implementing agency for 2,000 MWh of battery storage in Uttar Pradesh and Haryana.
- **PM-Surya Ghar rooftop solar implementation** — A government-scheme execution role covering rooftop solar for households in Himachal Pradesh, Punjab and Arunachal Pradesh plus government buildings across 18 ministries; small so far in scale — about 21.1 MW awarded as of late 2025.
- **International projects (Nepal)** — Cross-border hydro development, chiefly the 900 MW Arun-III project in Nepal (plus an MOU for the 490 MW Arun-4 through a joint venture), with power to be sold back into India — a PPA for Arun-III was signed with Uttar Pradesh Power Corporation and the plant is still under construction.
- **Joint ventures with state utilities** — Partnerships used to build renewable capacity in specific states — a formed JV with Assam Power Distribution Company for 1,000 MW and one under formation with Maharashtra's MAHAGENCO for 5,000 MW.

### The story in depth

SJVN is a state-owned power generator whose numbers describe an extremely asset-heavy business earning very little on what it owns. Every ₹1 of assets produces about ₹0.09 of sales, and FY26 revenue of ₹3,545cr sits on a roughly ₹30,000cr debt-funded asset base, with ₹7,000cr capitalised. For every ₹100 of capital in the business, shareholders' funds plus borrowings, it generated ₹4 of operating cash this year, and about 162 days of cash stays stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. The combined rating lands at 17 out of 100, or Weak, built from 27 for business quality, 10 for multibagger fit and 8 for risk safety, and the risk review describes the company as stressed. Nearly every quality area scores negative, with competitive advantage, customer benefits and management the weakest.

The one genuine strength and the biggest single risk are the same fact. The hydro assets pass a real cost-to-replicate test: sites like Nathpa Jhakri and Rampur, and pipeline projects such as the 3,097 MW Etalin, need finite river locations, forest and environment clearances and land acquisition that take a decade, and the legacy hydro base earns a cost-plus regulated return insulated from price competition. But those same two stations, Nathpa Jhakri at 1,500 MW and Rampur at 412 MW, generate the overwhelming bulk of profit across all fifteen calls, which is exactly why stakeholder concentration is flagged as elevated. The capacity growth story runs the same way. Installed capacity did compound, from 2,377 MW to 3,146 MW to 4,196 MW, with 16 GW awarded as a Renewable Energy Implementing Agency, so the market-share-gainer pattern half fits. Yet the growth is bid- and government-allocation-driven rather than won on merit, which is why government dependency is the single high risk: tariffs are fixed by the regulator, including a ₹2.45 cap under the CPSU scheme, and a mid-period rule barring operation above 100% availability collapsed incentive income from ₹253.9cr to ₹182cr. The growth that creates the pattern is the same growth that creates the dependency.

The patterns that fail outright fail for one shared reason, and it feeds directly into the risks. Electricity is a commodity sold wholesale at regulator-set or auction-set prices — ₹2.45, ₹2.88, an average of ₹2.60, Sadla at ₹2.43 — so there is no pricing power, no brand, no network effect and no proprietary technology, and projects are executed through awarded contracts. Because the buyers, the state distribution companies, hold the negotiating power, they simply decline to sign: of 16 GW awarded, only about 6 GW of purchase agreements were signed by May 2026, and the company funds its customers rather than the other way round, with receivables of ₹1,299cr including ₹906cr unbilled. That price-taking position produced real losses, not just weak returns — impairments of ₹138.7cr in FY24 and ₹174cr in FY26, plus a ₹258cr loss — which is why the low-price pattern is rejected and cyclicality is elevated against a capex programme of ₹8,000–10,000cr a year. Demand is also moving away from what SJVN has bid: the new Buxar thermal plant runs at 60% availability against the 70%-plus it needs to break even, and buyers now want firm, dispatchable configurations combining solar with storage rather than plain solar. Management is candid about all of this, but the delivery record undercuts it — four chairmen in five years, targets set as government obligations rather than chosen, a 12,000 MW-by-March-2026 vision that delivered 4,196 MW, and a $300m foreign loan left entirely unhedged.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Practically every earnings driver is set by government: tariffs fixed by CERC (INR2.45 cap under the CPSU scheme), a mid-period CERC rule barring operation above 100% PAF that collapsed incentive income from INR253.9 crore to INR182 crore and Q1 incentive from INR24.45 crore to INR5 crore, 80-IA tax benefit expiry costing INR52 crore, MNRE/REIA mandates, PM-Surya Ghar state allocations, and CMD appointments made in three-month stopgaps by the Ministry of Power.
