# Gokaldas Exports (GOKEX) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Gokaldas Exports is an Indian garment maker that manufactures apparel on order for global clothing brands and retailers and ships it to them, mainly in the United States and increasingly in Europe and the UK. It does not sell its own brand at retail; it wins style-by-style orders each fashion season, buys the fabric, converts it into finished garments in its factories, and earns money on the spread between the price it charges the brand and its cost of fabric, labour and overheads. Its plants are in India (Karnataka base plus newer low-cost sites in Madhya Pradesh and Ranchi, Jharkhand, and a fabric mill in Tamil Nadu) and in Africa through the acquired Atraco business, so it can serve customers from whichever location has the better duty access. Total income was about INR 4,065 crore in FY26 (up 4%), and profitability swings heavily with trade rules — US tariffs on India cost it over INR 90 crore of discounts given to customers in FY26, and the lapse and later restoration of AGOA duty-free access drove the Africa order book down and then back up.

**The parts of the business:**

- **India apparel operations (standalone Gokaldas Exports)** — The core garment manufacturing business in India — historically woven apparel — which is the largest part of the group, grew 10% in FY26 while Indian apparel exports fell 1.4%, and ran about a 12% operating EBITDA margin in Q4 FY26.
- **Africa operations (Atraco)** — Garment factories in Africa acquired in FY24 that ship mainly to the US and relied on AGOA duty-free access; revenue fell 19% in FY26 (roughly INR 180 crore) when AGOA lapsed, then grew 17% in Q4 after it was restored, with management targeting an 8%–10% EBITDA margin in H2 FY27.
- **BTPL — fabric processing unit (Tamil Nadu)** — A knits fabric processing mill (initial investment a little over INR 120 crore) that supplies the group's own critical raw material — vertical integration for speed, quality and cost — currently running near 50 lakh metres a month against 70 lakh capacity, still loss-making but expected to turn an operating profit in H2 FY27, and being merged into the parent subject to NCLT approval.
- **Other Indian subsidiaries (Matrix and others)** — Additional acquired garment units in India, including a factory at Ranchi in Jharkhand, positioned as low-cost locations meant to compete with Bangladesh; smaller than the core business and hit in Q4 FY26 when some of their customers shifted orders to other countries because of the 50% US tariff.
- **New capacity — Madhya Pradesh and ongoing capex** — Greenfield and brownfield capacity added over the period (the Madhya Pradesh unit started in FY24 and contributed roughly INR 55–60 crore in its first year), with about INR 170 crore spent on new capacity in FY26 alone — the main reason net debt rose by INR 395 crore.

### The story in depth

The one thing Gokaldas does unmistakably well is take business away from other people. It is the single most repeated and verified claim in the record: revenue held flat while Indian apparel exports fell 3.1% in early 2023, growth of 11.2% against an industry at 4.2% in 2024, and India operations up about 10% in FY26 while the industry shrank 1.4%. That is why 'Market Share Gainers' is the only pattern rated a strong fit, and why the growth area scores highest of the seven quality areas, with sales compounding around 13% a year. The share gains have an identifiable source rather than being a mystery: the framework flags an unusual abundance of weak competitors to feed on — distressed capacity in Bangladesh, cost-inflated Vietnam, exits from China — with industry consolidation openly favouring bigger, steadier suppliers. Management's own conduct supports the story rather than dressing it up: it puts numbers on its own bad news (INR12.6 crores of India production disruption, INR8.6 crores of air freight at Atraco, an INR40 crores tariff hit, an INR3.4 crores gratuity restatement) and admits its hedging policy cost it money. That candour is the strongest single check in the record and the reason the culture pattern gets partial credit.

The trouble is that the same trait producing the share gains also produces most of the risks. Gokaldas wins work by being the cheaper place to make a garment — Indian wages of $170-180 against Vietnam's $300, new plants sited in low-cost Madhya Pradesh and Jharkhand — and a cost edge that comes from which country you are standing in is not a cost edge you own. That is exactly why 'Low Price Plus' only earns partial credit, why 'Cost to Replicate' is judged no fit (wage gaps, tariff and trade-deal regimes, and customer relationships are all either outside the company's control or rebuildable by anyone with funding), and why the barriers check scores negative outright: profit margins swing by about 5 points, and the calls describe minimal barriers and irrational pricing. The consequences show up as three separate risk flags that are really one problem seen from different angles. Because the customer is a global brand that picks suppliers on landed cost, Gokaldas absorbed the bulk of the 50% US tariff itself — INR40 crores in a single quarter and over INR90 crores of discounts across FY26 — with margins falling 18% year on year instead of the cost being passed on. That is why the pricing power, toll road and brand strength patterns are all scored as no fit, why the industry structure check calls out overwhelming customer negotiating power in a fragmented global industry, and why 'Good Enough Goods' and 'New Entrants' both sit on watch. The largest risk of all, government dependency, is the same vulnerability in policy form: a tariff regime moved earnings by INR60 crores in one quarter, and the lapse of AGOA cut African revenue by roughly INR180 crores before its restoration handed the money back. Diversifying across India, Africa, Bangladesh subcontracting and now Europe rebalances that exposure but does not remove it.

What ties everything together is that the growth is real but is not yet paying for itself. For every INR100 of shareholder money and borrowings in the business, the company generated INR2 of operating cash this year, and roughly 155 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Meanwhile capex keeps going in — fabric units in Madhya Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, then INR170 crores of new capacity in FY26 — and the record is explicit that the returns on that spending are unproven, with the combined group's return on capital diluted while only the older standalone business carried its weight. Growth that is funded rather than self-financing is also fragile growth, and the order book offers little cushion: management says the business is almost entirely fresh seasonal fashion orders with no repeat programme work, which is why the recurring revenue pattern is a clear no fit and why fashion risk and cyclicality both appear on the risk list. Note that the backward move into fabric processing (the BTPL/Bombay Rayon merger targeted for early FY27) is a genuine attempt to escape the commodity trap by owning more of the value chain — it is the stated silver lining under 'Good Enough Goods' — but it also means more capital going into a business that currently returns very little on the capital it already has, and it is integration in the wrong direction to reach consumers, hence no fit on forward integration. The combined score of 40 out of 100 and a 'Strained' resilience reading are consistent with that picture: a genuinely capable operator gaining ground in a shrinking field, but doing so in an industry that lets it keep very little of what it wins.

Whether share gains continue once the easy pickings are gone — the 'Share Donators' check rests on distressed Bangladeshi, Vietnamese and Chinese capacity, and the 'Market Share Gainers' verdict depends on outgrowing the industry every year, not just in years when rivals are hurting.

Returns on the INR170 crores of new capacity and the BTPL fabric merger — the capex check already warns that incremental returns are unproven and that the combined group's return on capital was diluted; another round of spending without a visible payback would confirm the negative return-on-capital and capital-allocation scores rather than fix them.

The 155-day working capital cycle and the 2% cash return on capital — these are the two weakest checks in the whole framework, and until cash starts coming out of the business rather than going in, the 'Strained' resilience reading stands.

Reversal or repetition of the tariff and discount burden under 'Government Dependency' — INR90 crores of customer discounts in one year for a business earning INR2 of cash on every INR100 of capital is decisive; a favourable policy swing would flatter results without changing the underlying weakness, and another adverse one would compound it.

Any move from one-off seasonal orders toward repeat programme business, and progress on the European and new India/Africa customers cited under 'Stakeholder Concentration' — that is the only realistic route to fixing the 'Recurring Revenue' no-fit and reducing reliance on a small set of large American brands.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Fortunes are set almost entirely by trade policy: a 50% US reciprocal tariff plus penal tariff cost INR60cr in Q3FY26 alone and over INR90cr of customer discounts for FY26; AGOA's expiry cut Africa revenue by ~INR180cr and its restoration reversed it; UK/EU FTAs, Section 301 and a US Supreme Court ruling are repeatedly cited as the swing factors on both revenue and margin, and management explicitly refuses to forecast until policy 'fructifies'.

> "This was the first full quarter impacted by the steep 50% U.S. tariff on India and the expiry of AGOA" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Multi-geography production (India, Africa, Bangladesh subcontracting) and a push into Europe are deliberate policy-hedges, but they only rebalance exposure — they do not remove it.*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Demand swings with US/EU retail inventory cycles rather than customer capex: FY23 saw three sequential quarterly declines as brands 'tapered down their buying', while FY26 saw retailers pare imports despite strong retail sales; EBITDA margin moved from 13.1% (Q3FY23) to ~8% (Q3FY26) and the company kept adding capex (MP, Tamil Nadu knits, Atraco/Matrix/BTPL, INR170cr in FY26) through the downturn, lifting net debt by INR395cr.

> "On a sequential basis, our revenue has been falling from Q1 to Q3." — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Apparel is consumption-linked (a flow product), not tied to customer capex programmes; management invested counter-cyclically during the 2023 downturn to take market share, and India revenue grew 10% in FY26 while Indian apparel exports fell 1.4%.*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 2 of the last 11 years with swings of ±24 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
