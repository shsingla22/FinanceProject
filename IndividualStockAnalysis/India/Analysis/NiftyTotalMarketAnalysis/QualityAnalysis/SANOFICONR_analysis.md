# Sanofi Cons. Health (SANOFICONR) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Sanofi Consumer Healthcare India Limited (SCHIL) sells everyday, non-prescription-style healthcare brands in India — medicines and supplements for allergy, pain and vitamins — under names like Allegra, Avil, Combiflam and DePURA. It was demerged from Sanofi's India pharma business about two years ago and listed separately, and globally it sits under Opella (in which CD&R took a stake); despite that ownership, management says the India business owns its full P&L, makes its own decisions and manufactures almost 100% of its products in India. It makes money by selling these branded products through chemists and retail pharmacies (with e-commerce and modern trade still only a single-digit share but growing faster), and demand is generated two ways: field reps detailing roughly 350-strong to doctors such as paediatricians, allergologists and chest physicians, plus direct consumer advertising and in-store visibility. The business is about ₹880 crore of turnover with profit from operations near 37% and ROCE of 62.5%; in 2025 revenue rose 21%, operating profit 13% and PAT 33%, and it operates only in India, with North (including UP) its strongest region and East its weakest.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Allergy (Allegra, Avil)** — Anti-allergy medicines where Avil is the number one player by volume and Allegra number three by value, with a newer variant Allegra D launched last year; management calls allergy a core category with 3 in 10 people having had an allergy in the past six months.
- **Pain (Combiflam)** — A well-known pain-relief brand that ranks about fifth in its category even though, management says, the company had not called on doctors about it for 15 years — showing how much headroom is left; 9 in 10 Indians report some pain episode.
- **VMS / Vitamins (DePURA)** — The company's entry into the Vitamin D space, already among the top five brands in that category, in a market where 7 out of 10 Indians are said to be Vitamin D deficient.
- **HCP (doctor) engagement** — A roughly 350-person medical rep field force detailing all four brands to paediatricians, allergologists, chest physicians and GPs; management plans to double face-to-face doctor reach in 2026 and add 50,000 more doctors digitally.
- **Consumer engagement / brand building** — Direct-to-consumer advertising, social media and category-education campaigns, funded by lifting A&P spend from low single digits of sales to mid-teens — a change only started around the second quarter of last year.
- **Retail / point of sale** — Direct coverage of retail pharmacies for visibility and range selling, which management wants to double; e-commerce and modern trade are listed but still only single-digit contributors, though growing faster than the core.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Sanofi Consumer Health earns its 'Strong' rating (65 out of 100) mostly on the strength of what it does with capital rather than on any moat. The scoring puts Return on Capital at the top of the range: every ₹100 of shareholders' funds and borrowings threw off ₹84 of operating cash this year, management cited a 62.5% return on capital employed on roughly ₹880 crore of revenue with about 37% profit from operations, and ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.24 of sales because manufacturing is fully outsourced to contract makers. The economics compound from there — after direct input costs the company keeps ₹74 of every ₹100 of sales, ₹35 of every ₹100 ends up as operating profit with year-to-year swings of only about two points, and customers pay 16 days before suppliers need to be paid, so the working capital is funded by the trade rather than by the balance sheet. It sells into a self-care market the records describe as in clear secular expansion, with rising self-medication, more online health searching and low penetration in allergy, pain and Vitamin D. Management scores well on candor too: it volunteered the damage from the product recalls, admitted competitors took share, and separated the 22% headline domestic growth from the 11% underlying figure once the recall rebound is stripped out.

The two patterns that fit strongly — Friendly Middleman and Brand Strength — describe the same machine, and that machine is also the source of the single biggest risk. Demand is started by a doctor or pharmacist who recommends while the patient pays, and the first lever of the growth plan is doubling face-to-face reach using a 350-strong medical rep force aimed at paediatricians, allergologists and chest physicians. Sitting behind that are heritage brands that held top-five category positions with essentially no support for years — Combiflam stayed top-five through 15 years without doctor or consumer investment, Avil leads its category on volume — which is also the strongest barrier to replication the company can point to. But the portfolio rests on molecules that went off patent long ago (fexofenadine, ibuprofen-paracetamol, pheniramine, Vitamin D), where cheap equivalents are freely available and the same intermediary who recommends the brand can just as easily recommend the substitute. That is why the risk review rates 'Good Enough Goods' as the one HIGH risk even while the two brand-and-recommendation patterns rate STRONG FIT: brand recall and the rep force are simultaneously the defence and the admission that the product itself is not the defence. It shows up in the scoring as a Competitive Advantage area score of -0.5 against a Return on Capital score of 2.0. Three further patterns — Pricing Power, Toll Roads and Recurring Revenue — register only as quantitative signals with no supporting discussion at all; growth is framed entirely as volume, penetration and category expansion, with no talk of price increases or premiumisation, and sales are one-off product purchases through pharmacies with no subscriptions or service contracts.

The response to that vulnerability is the advertising and promotion step-up — from low single digits to the mid-teens as a share of sales — and that single decision links the weakest check to two of the watch-rated risks. It is graded only a partial positive because the payoff is still early: FY25 revenue rose 21% with margins largely held, but the brand-building spend is a bet, not a proven return. Its ceiling is regulatory. The pending over-the-counter policy decides whether the company may advertise its lead brands directly to consumers at all — management calls this 'the biggest unlock' — which is why Government Dependency sits at WATCH even though not a rupee of revenue comes from the state; demand is private out-of-pocket spending, and the company has already used brand extensions such as Combiflam cream to talk to consumers within the current rules. The second watch item is that the brands, the pipeline and the research all originate with the Opella and Sanofi group: several India products still sit outside the listed entity and are run by the same team on a cross-charge, and ownership itself shifted when CD&R took a stake in Opella. Research capability has the same shape — a small in-house team drawing on Opella's global network produced one genuinely differentiated launch in Allegra D, filling a vacant congestion-remedy space, but management declined to quantify spend or pipeline when pressed, which is why Innovation Dominance is only a likely fit. Corporate Culture and Market Share Gainers are partial for a shared reason: this is a two-year-old demerged company with a new leadership team, so the honest disclosure, the B-Corp certification and the market-share KPI have no multi-year record behind them, and the recent share performance is recovery of ground lost to the recalls rather than sustained gain. Everything else is quiet — cyclicality, technology, new entrants, changing consumer taste and fashion all rate LOW, with the shift toward self-medication actually running in the company's favour — so overall resilience is judged SOUND.
