# TVS Supply Chain Sol (TVSSCS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

TVS Supply Chain Solutions is an Indian-headquartered logistics company that runs other companies' supply chains for them — moving, storing, sourcing and managing goods and spare parts on their behalf. It sells these services to corporate customers across many industries under long-term, tailor-made outsourcing contracts (mostly 3PL, with 4PL services in select overseas markets), typically deploying its own people, technology and hired transport rather than owning heavy assets, which management calls 'tech-led and asset-light'. Revenue comes partly from these multi-year service contracts and partly from arranging air and ocean freight, where it earns on the volume of cargo it handles and the prevailing freight rates. It operates across four continents — Asia, Europe, North America and Oceania — with India, the UK/Europe and North America as the main regions; FY26 revenue crossed Rs. 11,000 crores with adjusted EBITDA of Rs. 773 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Integrated Supply Chain Solutions (ISCS)** — Long-term outsourcing contracts covering sourcing and procurement, in-plant/manufacturing support, aftermarket and spares solutions, integrated transportation and aftermarket fulfilment; it is the larger and more profitable of the two segments — about Rs. 2,283 crores of the Rs. 3,032 crores of Q4 FY26 revenue, with adjusted EBITDA margins around 9%, and management repeatedly calls it the driver of overall profitability.
- **Global Forwarding Solutions (GFS) — earlier reported inside 'Network Solutions'** — The freight forwarding business, arranging end-to-end air and ocean shipments along with warehousing and ground transport through a global forwarding network; it is the smaller, structurally lower-margin segment (roughly Rs. 736 crores in Q3 FY26 at about 2.3% adjusted EBITDA margin), and its results swing with freight rates and events like the Red Sea diversions, US port strikes and tariff volatility, with recent growth coming mainly from India ocean volumes.
- **Integrated Final Mile (IFM) — the other half of the old Network Solutions segment** — A final-mile delivery network offering time-critical services such as courier, break-bulk support and spares management (originally described as 'time-critical last mile'); it was loss-making and in a 'turnaround phase' as of FY25, and in the later calls the company reports only ISCS and GFS as its two segments.
- **Business development / new business wins** — Revenue from newly signed contracts and from 'encirclement' — widening existing customer relationships into new services or new countries — which management tracks as a headline number: roughly Rs. 255-280 crores per quarter, about 12% of revenue, against a stated pipeline of about Rs. 4,500 crores.
- **Cost and transformation programmes (Project One) and acquisitions** — Project One is a UK/Europe cost takeout programme targeting Rs. 110-120 crores of annualised savings and credited with the European margin turnaround, alongside rightsizing and right-shoring across regions; separately the company acquired Swamy & Sons 3PL in India (completed May 2026) to build FMCG and consumption-led supply chain capability.

### The story in depth

TVS Supply Chain Solutions earns its best marks on how the business is financed and how hard the money works, not on how protected it is. Every rupee of assets produces Rs.1.54 of sales, and customers pay in roughly 170 days before suppliers have to be paid — so the business runs on other people's cash rather than its own, and it turned Rs.16 of operating cash for every Rs.100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings this year. Management has behaved consistently with that model: it used IPO proceeds to cut debt, walked away from unprofitable contracts, took out structural cost (Project One, worth Rs.110-120 crore a year in savings), and kept acquisitions small, complementary and paid for from its own funds. The result is a company that scores well on capital allocation and returns while scoring negatively on competitive advantage — and those two facts have the same root. An asset-light contract logistics and freight forwarding business that wins work through competitive bids is cheap to run, but it is also cheap for someone else to copy, which is why the 'Cost to Replicate' pattern is a clear no-fit and why the company's own North America win — displacing an incumbent provider — is as much a warning about how contestable these mandates are as it is a success story.

The multibagger screen fits this picture and explains the low score of 18 out of 100 on that dimension. Nothing tests as a full fit; the closest is recurring revenue, because the contracts genuinely are long (relationships averaging over ten years, a 7-year UK framework, a North America engagement of more than five years worth about USD 30 million a year) and existing accounts get expanded — but there is no upfront product sale that pulls follow-on revenue behind it. Market share gains, cost discipline, brand pull and technology all register as partial rather than proven: new business wins are real and rising (Rs.1,206.7 crore in FY26, equal to 12.1% of FY25 revenue, against a pipeline of Rs.6,100-6,300 crore, with Fortune 500 clients up from 91 to 100), technology repeatedly converts into wins through AI deployments, an accepted patent and robotics, and cost savings landed as guided. But the cost work reads as a turnaround clean-up rather than a permanent low-cost culture, no research budget is ever disclosed, and the delivery record is mixed — profitability targets for the facilities-management business and the 7% Network margin promise were both pushed back. Pricing power is the sharpest failure: growth is explicitly volume-led while price works against the company, with ocean rates down roughly 55% year-on-year in one quarter and freight rates still described as subdued in FY26.

The risks are the same traits seen from the other side, which is why the overall rating lands at 48 out of 100 — Mixed — even though resilience is judged sound. The two elevated risks, cyclicality and commoditisation, both sit in the freight-forwarding arm, and that is exactly where the quality framework sees upside: rates are near a cyclical trough with volumes already rebounding, so normalising rates would help, but the same segment swung revenue down 35.1% in one quarter and dragged the whole company down 12.4%, and it runs at 1.6-2.4% margins because customers treat it as a negotiated, replaceable line of cost. The offset is the contract logistics side, roughly 75% of revenue, where bespoke solutions embedded in customers' operations earn 9.2-9.3% margins and are far harder to switch away from. Two further risks are flagged for watching rather than acting on: dependence on a handful of large engagements — the Centrica go-live, the Rs.2,200 crore-plus North America contract, and a European project exit visible in the cost line — which the widening Fortune 500 roster is meant to dilute, and exposure to UK public-sector work and policy variables such as tariffs. Technology change and shifting tastes are not threats here; demand is contractual, functional and spread across four continents and many industries. In short: a well-run, cash-efficient share gainer operating in a market that does not let it charge more, so value depends on volume, cost control and the cycle rather than on any moat.
