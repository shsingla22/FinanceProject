# Skipper (SKIPPER) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Skipper Limited is an India-based (Kolkata headquartered) manufacturer that makes steel transmission towers, poles and related structures used to build electricity transmission and distribution (T&D) networks, and it also takes on turnkey EPC contracts to actually build transmission lines (qualified up to the highest 765 kV and 800 kV voltage levels, and executing EHV and HVDC lines). Its customers are power utilities and grid operators such as Power Grid Corporation (PGCIL), state electricity boards and private utilities in India, plus roughly 200-250 global EPC contractors and overseas utilities across the Middle East, Africa, Southeast Asia, Asia Pacific, Latin America, Australia, Europe and North America. It earns money by winning tendered supply and EPC orders that build into an order book (an all-time-high INR8,501.9 crores at March 2026) and converting them into revenue from its own plants, so profits improve as capacity utilisation rises; revenue was INR5,552.8 crores in FY26 with EBITDA margins around 10.3%. Alongside this it runs a separate consumer-facing business making PVC/polymer pipes sold through retail and project channels in India.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Engineering (Engineering Products)** — Manufactures and supplies transmission and distribution towers, poles and structures — plus telecom-related structures — to Indian and international utilities and EPC contractors; it is by far the largest segment at INR4,359 crores of FY26 revenue (about 24% growth) with segment EBITDA margins of roughly 11%-13%, and management calls its capacity, now heading to 450,000 tonnes a year at over 85% utilisation, the core of the company.
- **Infrastructure / EPC** — Executes turnkey transmission-line projects (not just supplying parts), including 765 kV lines in Uttar Pradesh and Karnataka and about 5,000 circuit kilometres of EHV and HVDC work across 25 active PGCIL projects; it is a much smaller but fast-growing piece — INR168 crores in a single quarter (Q1 FY25, up roughly 16x year-on-year) — and management says the high-voltage EPC work carries better margins because few competitors qualify for it.
- **Polymer** — Makes PVC pipes and fittings sold both to retail customers and to projects in India; revenue crossed INR500 crores in FY26 (its highest ever, up from INR320 crores in FY22), making it the second-largest business, though it is sensitive to project timing — sales fell 26% in one quarter because of elections.
- **Exports (within Engineering)** — Overseas sales of towers and poles, which management pushed from under half of engineering revenue in FY23 toward a much larger share, supported by sales staff in 6-7 countries, agents in 70-80 active markets and a China Plus One sourcing shift; exports were about 10% of the order book at FY26 year-end after a geopolitics-related hit, but include a first multimillion-dollar order from a leading North American utility.
- **R&D, tower testing and qualification infrastructure** — An in-house design and R&D centre plus tower testing stations — including a second test bed commissioned in FY26 that management says makes Skipper the only company globally with dual test beds at one location — which management describes not as a revenue line but as what wins customer approvals and design-cost savings that help secure orders.

### The story in depth

Skipper is a transmission tower and equipment maker that sells into a market with a very long runway: the records point to INR9 lakh crore of Indian transmission spending planned to 2032 and a further INR6.5 lakh crore Arunachal hydro opportunity to 2035, which is why 'structural end-market growth' scores the top mark of 2. The company is putting money behind that runway rather than harvesting it — capacity is going from 300k to 450k tonnes with another 75k plus 75k planned to reach 600k by FY28 at roughly INR250 crores a year, and management has quantified INR800-1,000 crores of extra revenue from a roughly INR200 crore spend while holding returns on capital. The evidence that it is winning is real: the order book went from INR2,163 crores in FY23 to INR8,502 crores in FY26, capacity use rose from 70% to above 85%, FY26 revenue hit a record INR5,553 crores, and it broke into a North American utility for the first time. That combination is why 'Market Share Gainers' is the one pattern rated a likely fit, why the market-share check scores positively, and why Management is the strongest quality area at 0.83, helped by stable family leadership, conservative borrowing at 0.62x debt to equity, and a habit of admitting misses — the 25% growth aspiration trimmed to 21%, capex slipping a quarter.

The trouble is that the same trait that makes Skipper competitive is also what caps what it can earn. Its edge is cost, not price: it is the only end-to-end backward-integrated player from rolling mills through to project execution, and that scale lifted operating margin from about 9.6% to 10.3% on thin net margins of 3.7%. But towers are sold through competitive tenders where 8-10 qualified manufacturers bid and the buyer — usually a utility or one of 200-250 global project contractors — decides on price. So 'Low Price Plus' is only a partial fit, while 'Pricing Power', 'Toll Roads' and 'Recurring Revenue' are outright no-fits, and the same tender dynamic drives the 'Good Enough Goods' risk flagged as one to watch. Realisations follow commodity and tender pricing rather than the company's own decisions — the PVC business took inventory losses when commodity prices fell — and management defends margins through contract structure and buffers rather than raising prices. This is why the multibagger screen scores only 18 out of 100 despite one clear fit: six patterns land at partial and four at no fit, and the overall grade lands at Mixed, 44 out of 100. The genuine barriers that do exist — 15-plus years of utility registrations across 70-80 markets, 765/800 kV qualification where competition is described as very limited, completed plant audits in North America, Europe and Australia, and the only site in the world with dual tower test beds — support the entry-barrier check and the 'Cost to Replicate' partial fit, but they protect the top end of the range rather than the whole business.

The risk review reads as the flip side of the growth story rather than a separate list. Government dependency is the one high risk precisely because the end-market that scores 2 for structural growth is a public one: state boards and the national grid company dominate an order book that is 90% domestic, with 25 active projects at the grid company alone, and demand is framed by government capex plans and tariff-based bidding. Cyclicality is elevated for the same reason — revenue is a direct function of someone else's capital spending, management concedes ordering swings year to year, and FY26 already saw a temporary moderation in ordering, which matches the negative 'persistence of growth' check where sales have compounded around 17% but lumpily. Customer concentration sits on the watch list partly because management could not answer an analyst's direct question about how much of the order book the two swing customers represent. Alongside that, two checks score negative in ways that connect: the export thesis has repeatedly missed explicit promises — exports were meant to be 50% of engineering revenue in FY23 and 75% by FY24, yet the FY26 book is 90% domestic — which weakens the very diversification offered as the silver lining against government dependency; and capital has flowed towards shareholders' pockets in reverse, with a INR200 crore rights issue raised for working capital and no dividend or buyback framework discussed. With resilience rated strained, receivables of INR1,485 crores, and heavy ongoing capex, the honest reading is a genuine share gainer in a genuinely growing market whose economics and cash needs are set largely by customers and commodity prices rather than by itself.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** The business is anchored in fixed public infrastructure and policy: PGCIL and state electricity boards dominate the 90%-domestic order book (25 active PGCIL projects), demand is framed by the government's INR9 lakh crore transmission capex plan, NEP, TBCB tariff-based bidding and CEA's bidding cadence; BSNL orders and Jal Jeevan Mission drive telecom and PVC, and general elections directly dented polymer revenue in Q1 FY25. > "The Polymer performance was impacted on account of an elections in quarter 1 this year" — *management, on an earnings call*

*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but none of the 3 computed fingerprints confirms it in the numbers yet — held one step below the top severity.
