# Medplus Health Services (MEDPLUS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

MedPlus Health Services runs a chain of neighbourhood pharmacy stores in India, selling prescription and over-the-counter medicines plus non-pharmacy household, wellness and personal-care items to walk-in retail customers, with the same range also offered online for two-hour home delivery or store pickup in the areas its stores cover. It makes money on the retail margin between what it buys stock for and what it sells it at, and it lifts that margin by pushing its own MedPlus-branded 'private label' products, which it sells at large discounts to branded equivalents but earns more on — private label was about 20-22% of revenue in FY26-27 versus roughly 14% in early FY23. Around 99% of revenue comes from this pharmacy retail business; a small diagnostics (lab testing) arm makes up almost all of the rest. The business is India-only and concentrated in the south, having grown from 3,557 stores in 497 cities across 7 states at end-2022 to 5,476 stores across 13 states by June 2026, at an average store size of about 528-539 square feet.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Pharmacy operations (retail stores)** — The core business of company-owned neighbourhood chemist shops selling branded medicines, MedPlus private-label medicines and non-pharma goods; it is roughly 99% of consolidated revenue, and stores older than 12 months (94-96% of pharmacy revenue) run at store-level EBITDA margins of about 10-13%.
- **Private label** — MedPlus's own-brand range across pharma and non-pharma — over 900 products in early 2023 growing to 1,200-plus SKUs — sold at 50-80% discounts to branded products; management treats it as the main margin lever, at about 20-22% of total revenue in FY26-FY27 (roughly 11% pharma, 9-10% non-pharma).
- **Omnichannel / online** — Online ordering fulfilled from the same local stores, with two-hour delivery and store pickup (pickup consistently the larger share); management deliberately spends almost nothing on acquiring online customers, treating it as a profitable add-on to the store network rather than a separate business.
- **Diagnostics** — Lab and radiology testing sold mainly through prepaid membership plans that give members tests at steep discounts to MRP, run out of full-service diagnostic centres (three in Hyderabad initially) plus 100-plus collection centres; small but now profitable, at INR 1,310 million revenue in FY26 with about 2 lakh active plans and a low on-time renewal rate of roughly 21-26%.
- **Franchisee stores** — A newer, faster-growing way to add outlets where a franchisee runs the store; around 500-plus of the roughly 5,300 stores by end-FY26, and management says these are profitable from day one, unlike company-owned stores which lose money in year one.
- **Opticals / others** — A small optical-retail line that management notes is shown under 'others' in the investor presentation but grouped under retail in the regulatory filings; it is never discussed as a material contributor.
- **New ventures (on hold)** — Board-approved capex of about INR 40 crore for a food park and oil-extraction unit (backward integration into supply) and a wellness/concierge-medicine facility meant to extend diagnostics into prevention and follow-up care — all of it was put on hold as of the July 2026 call, with management saying it is unlikely to be pursued within MedPlus.

### The story in depth

MedPlus earns a "Decent" 55 out of 100, and the arithmetic behind that (45% x 60 for business quality, 30% x 26 for multibagger fit, 25% x 80 for risk safety) tells the story on its own: this is a well-run, low-risk, fast-growing retailer that lacks the pricing and franchise characteristics that turn growth into outsized long-run returns. The strengths are real and concentrated in execution. The store network has gone from 3,557 stores in 7 states to 5,476 stores in 13 states, pharmacy revenue is growing 21-23% a year, sales have compounded around 16% annually with unusual year-to-year consistency, and revenue has risen from INR 11.9bn in Q3FY23 to INR 18.8bn in Q1FY27. It is done asset-light -- every ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.75 of sales, stores are leased, and working capital has tightened from 63 days to 53-54 days -- which is why store-level return on capital sits above 60% even though company-wide EBITDA margin is only in the 3.1%-5.8% range. Management scores full marks: the founder-CEO has run the business since inception, return on capital is disclosed every quarter, mistakes are admitted plainly (staff "may have gone a little overboard" pushing private label and annoyed customers, corrigenda published on cash-flow numbers, an open admission that GLP-1 drug impact simply is not tracked), and the FY26 promise of 600 new stores was delivered at 618.

The important thing to notice is that the same two traits drive both the patterns that fit and the risks that don't go away. First, aggressive discounting. Standard 10%/20% discounts plus own-brand products priced 50-80% below the brand leaders are exactly what makes the "Low Price Plus" pattern a likely fit -- and are exactly why "Pricing Power" is a clear non-fit. Management itself concedes that reported sales look muted "because the private label sells at a much lower rate"; margin improvement comes from product mix and supplier rebates, not from charging more, and the only real price action in four years was trimming a discount from 20% to 19% in July 2026 to protect margin. The same private-label push -- from 7.9% of goods sold to about 17.7%, roughly 20-22% of revenue across 1,200-plus own-brand items -- makes MedPlus the attacker in the "Good Enough Goods" risk, displacing branded medicine with its own cheaper equivalents at the shelf it controls; but the risk review flags that this is a low-complexity proposition, so what MedPlus is doing to branded pharma someone else can eventually do to MedPlus. Second, physical store density. The cluster network, roughly 10 new regional warehouses, cold-chain capability and two-hour delivery at essentially zero customer acquisition cost are the genuine moat and the reason customers keep coming back -- and physical pharmacy retail is precisely the format that quick commerce and online pharmacies attack, which is why "New Entrants" stays on watch. The clearest single number linking the two sides is the share of new stores breaking even within six months, which has fallen from 69% to about 55%: it shows up as a mark against geographic expansion in the quality checks and as evidence of competitive pressure in the risk review.

What holds the score down is not danger but incompleteness. Every one of the eight risk channels came back either LOW (cyclicality, customer concentration, fashion risk) or WATCH -- none is severe, and overall resilience is judged SOUND, which is why the risk component scored 80. But on the quality side, competitive advantage and capital allocation both score zero, and industry structure is negative. Capital allocation is the sharpest contradiction with the otherwise excellent management score: growth spending into stores and warehouses at 60%-plus store returns is well judged, but the board approved an unrelated food-park, oil-extraction and wellness venture that was later shelved, and the cash now sits idle with its use undecided, while no dividend or buyback policy is articulated across thirteen calls. On the pattern screen, only two of eleven patterns are likely fits ("Low Price Plus" and "Market Share Gainers", the latter weakened by management never quoting a market-share figure or naming a competitor); five are partial and four are outright misses. The recurring-revenue layer is real but partial -- around 44-45 lakh pharmacy members whose fee was lifted from ₹99 to ₹149 without losing them, and diagnostics plans up from 152,000 to about 200,000 -- while brand strength is capped by management explicitly refusing sustained advertising spend, and innovation dominance fails outright with no research spending or pipeline disclosed anywhere. In short: a disciplined, honest, fast-growing share-gainer in a fragmented market with a long runway, whose own low-price model and replicable format are what keep it from being more than that.
