# Hindustan Constr (HCC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

HCC (Hindustan Construction Company) is a roughly 100-year-old Indian construction contractor that builds large, technically difficult infrastructure — highways, road and rail tunnels, metro lines and stations, bridges, dams and hydropower plants, pumped-storage schemes, nuclear power facilities and water projects. Its customers are almost entirely government bodies and state-owned agencies (railways, metro corporations, NTPC, NHPC/THDC-type hydro developers, the Department of Atomic Energy), which award work by competitive bidding; HCC makes money by executing these contracts over several years and billing against milestones, earning an EBITDA margin that has run from about 10% to 18%. It works across India — Maharashtra, Uttarakhand, Delhi, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, J&K, Tamil Nadu and elsewhere — plus a hydro project in Bhutan and a property-development subsidiary in Switzerland. The calls describe a company whose sales run around ₹4,000 crore a year standalone on an order backlog of roughly ₹11,000–15,000 crore, and which spent these years paying down heavy debt (from a lender restructuring into an SPV, then a ₹1,000 crore rights issue, cutting debt to about ₹1,995 crore by March 2026) while chasing a large pipeline of new bids.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Engineering & Construction (E&C) — the standalone business** — The core contracting arm that actually builds the projects and produces essentially all of the standalone revenue (₹3,937 crore in FY26); management describes the order book as diversified across sectors and geographies, around ₹13,000 crore at end-FY26.
- **Transport** — Roads, highways, bridges and especially urban metro work (Delhi DC06, Mumbai Metro stations, Mumbai Coastal Road, Patna Metro PC05/PC06, Indore Metro, the Anji Khad cable-stayed bridge, the T-49 railway tunnel in J&K, Agardanda Creek bridge) — consistently the single largest slice of the order backlog, around half of it, and the company says it built India's first metro plus work in Delhi, Chennai and Mumbai.
- **Hydropower and Pumped Storage (PSP)** — Dams, head-race tunnels and underground powerhouses — Vishnugad Pipalkoti, Tapovan Vishnugad, Tehri 1,000 MW pumped storage (all four units now commissioned), Bhivpuri PSP and Nikachhu in Bhutan; the second-biggest part of the backlog, and management claims HCC has built about 26–27% of India's installed hydro capacity and sees a ₹70,000 crore near-term bidding opportunity here.
- **Nuclear and special projects** — Reactor buildings, containment domes, fuel-recycling and fuel-cycle facilities for the atomic energy programme (RAPP Units 7 & 8, BARC Tarapur, the Fast Reactor Fuel Cycle Facility at Kalpakkam); smaller in current backlog but repeatedly called HCC's most distinctive niche — it says it has built over 60–65% of India's installed nuclear capacity and expects a large order wave as the country targets 100 GW of nuclear by 2047, with real contracts only from about 2028.
- **Water and buildings** — Water-sector and building works listed as the smaller remaining categories of the order backlog, mentioned only in passing alongside the four main sectors.
- **Concessions / BOT assets (HCC Infrastructure)** — Toll-road assets the group owns and earns traffic-based revenue from, including BFHL, which grew 31% in the June 2022 quarter; the calls treat it as a portfolio being monetised — a stake sale in BFHL was 'on track' and management spoke of rebuilding the portfolio with new bids — and it fades out of the discussion in later calls.
- **Steiner AG (Switzerland)** — A Zurich-based subsidiary founded in 1915 and bought by HCC in 2010, which sold off its low-margin construction business in 2023 and now does only real estate development, with 65+ active projects mainly in western Switzerland; HCC treats it as an asset to monetise and pull cash back from — ₹84 crore of a ₹89 crore receivable was recovered by Q3 FY26.

### The story in depth

Hindustan Constr is a hundred-year-old engineering and construction contractor whose real assets are its credentials rather than its balance sheet. It has built more than 60% of India's nuclear capacity and roughly 26-27% of its hydro capacity, along with 4,000 km of highways, and that track record wins the prequalification needed to bid for work others simply cannot bid for — the aluminium order came from work done ten years earlier. The engineering itself is genuinely good: India's first large-scale monopile foundations, which cut 424 piles down to 79, a 9.86 m double-shield tunnel boring machine running at 431 m a month, and 120 m composite navigation spans. The numbers show two bright spots. Operating profit is about ₹16 of every ₹100 of sales and has been steady, moving only about three points a year, and this year the business generated ₹28 of operating cash for every ₹100 of shareholder funds plus borrowings. Cash also comes in from customers well before suppliers have to be paid. On the combined scorecard this still adds up to 34 out of 100 — a Weak, one-star rating built from 45% of a business-quality score of 50, 30% of a multibagger-fit score of 10, and 25% of a risk-safety score of 36 — though the company is judged financially sound rather than fragile.

Those strengths stop short of becoming a durable business model, which is why four of the eleven multibagger patterns come out only partial and seven do not fit at all. The brand, the innovation record, the management follow-through and the barrier to replication are all real but incomplete: the heritage is a credentials list rather than something customers pay more for, the engineering firsts come with no disclosed research spending, and management, while it did deliver the promised debt carve-out, a rights issue that was subscribed twice over and a 38% debt reduction to ₹1,995 crore, also has a record of chronic slippage. The reason the other seven patterns fail traces back to a single fact: work is awarded to the lowest bidder in public tenders. That removes pricing power — prices are fixed at bid and cost overruns are recovered years later through claims and arbitration, not price increases, which is why quarterly margins swing between 10.5% and 31%. It also rules out recurring revenue, any middleman channel, and any toll-road-style position where the customer barely notices the price. Nor is the company gaining ground: the order backlog went from ₹14,875 crore in mid-2022 to ₹11,000 crore in late 2023 and only ₹13,000 crore by FY26, revenue fell from ₹4,801 crore to ₹3,937 crore, and FY26 new orders of ₹5,654 crore badly missed the roughly ₹10,000 crore target. The direction of travel is also inward, not outward — the Swiss subsidiary's construction arm was sold, concession assets were sold, overseas nuclear work was ruled out, and the earlier Steiner acquisition and Lavasa both ended badly.

The risk review lines up with all of this, and in several places the very same trait produces both the strength and the danger. The nuclear and Himalayan hydro qualifications that make the business hard to replicate are also what makes it a lowest-bidder business in everything else, so 'good enough' competition sits at elevated risk with those same niches as its only offset. The customer advances that make working capital look free are the flip side of a government and public-sector order book — essentially the entire book comes from bodies such as NHAI, DMRC, NPCIL, NTPC and state metro corporations — and the roughly ₹1,000 crore of client advances is interest-bearing and being unwound, so it is a cost being paid down rather than free funding, sitting alongside receivables that are stuck. That is why government dependency is high risk and why the asset base looks heavy, turning only ₹0.47 of sales per ₹1 of assets. Because revenue depends entirely on customers' own capex plans, results swing violently: a ₹279 crore consolidated loss in one quarter, a ₹233 crore profit in another. Concentration in a handful of mega-projects is elevated — one quarter's shortfall was blamed on Mumbai projects alone, with fishermen protests at the coastal road and a skilled-labour shortage. The two comforting readings are that neither new entrants nor technological disruption threatens the core niches. Capital allocation is where the story ends up: there are no dividends or buybacks, shareholders were diluted by a ₹1,000 crore rights issue with a further fundraising resolution in hand, and money has gone to repaying lenders rather than to growth, with only incidental project assets such as a nine-acre casting yard and tunnel boring machines to show for it. That makes the 28% cash return a snapshot of a shrinking business paying down roughly ₹1,000 crore a year of interest, not the engine of compounding it would be elsewhere.

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration, Good Enough Goods, Fashion Risk. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** HCC is a pure EPC contractor whose revenue is entirely a function of customers' capex programmes (metro, hydro, pumped storage, nuclear, roads), and results swing violently across the 13 calls — a ₹279cr consolidated loss in Q1FY23, ₹233cr profit in Q3FY24, standalone turnover falling from ₹4,801cr (FY25) to ₹3,937cr (FY26), and EBITDA margins ranging from 10.5% to 31% quarter to quarter. Management itself frames the outlook off government capex plans and admits lumpiness ('jobs... in that sort of 1000 to 3000 crore range. And so there will be some lumpiness'), with revenue admitted to be constrained until the order book converts.

> "our top line may continue to be constrained until the order book flows into sort of a top line" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 7 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 5% and 16% (±3.1 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 1% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 9%).

## What to watch

- Order intake versus target — FY26 brought in ₹5,654 crore against roughly ₹10,000 crore; a backlog that climbs decisively above ₹13,000 crore would start to challenge the 'Market Share Gainers: no fit' and the negative market-share check.

- Debt and interest after the 38% cut to ₹1,995 crore — if the roughly ₹1,000 crore annual interest bill falls far enough for cash to go into growth rather than lenders, the 'capex for growth, not maintenance' check would change.
- Any further dilution under the enabling fundraising resolution, or the first dividend or buyback — the distribution-to-shareholders check is currently the weakest single item in the file.
- Quarterly margin swings and one-off project disruptions of the Mumbai coastal-road type — repeated episodes would confirm the high cyclicality and elevated stakeholder-concentration verdicts; several clean, steady quarters would soften them.
