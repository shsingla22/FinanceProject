# Home First Finan (HOMEFIRST) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Home First Finance is an Indian housing finance company that lends money to people buying or building homes, mostly in the affordable-housing segment, and takes the property as security. It makes loans at an origination yield of about 13% while borrowing at roughly 8% from banks, the National Housing Bank, and other sources, and earns the difference — a spread of about 5.3% and a net interest margin near 5.7%–6% — plus fees; profit after tax was Rs 540 crore in FY26 on assets under management of Rs 15,878 crore, up from Rs 5,832 crore in mid-2022. It runs a branch-and-touchpoint network (171 branches and 373 touchpoints as of March 2026) concentrated in Gujarat, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh. The business is deliberately technology-heavy — app-based customer service, account-aggregator-based approvals, e-agreements and e-NACH mandates, and now AI tools in underwriting — which management says keeps operating costs at about 2.7% of assets.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Individual housing loans (home loans)** — The core product — home loans to individual buyers, making up about 83% of new disbursements, priced at roughly 13% and all on a floating rate so they can be repriced when funding costs move.
- **Loan against property (LAP)** — Loans given against a property the customer already owns; management says this runs steadily at about 15%–20% of disbursements, a small but consistent slice alongside home loans.
- **Co-lending with banks** — Loans originated jointly with banks (starting with a Union Bank of India tie-up in 2022), which lets Home First serve larger-ticket customers without using its own balance sheet; the co-lending book was Rs 585 crore, or 3.9% of AUM, in Dec 2025, and management wants it at 10% of AUM.
- **Direct assignment (portfolio sell-down)** — Selling pools of already-made loans to banks and other buyers to raise cash — described as a liquidity strategy, done in chunks like Rs 152 crore in Q1 FY25 and Rs 215 crore in Q3 FY26; assignment plus co-lending together funded about 20% of the balance sheet.
- **Borrowings / treasury** — The funding engine rather than a sales line: about 57% from public and private sector banks, 16% NHB refinance, the rest from assignment, co-lending, NCDs and external commercial borrowings, with no commercial paper at all, at a cost of borrowing of about 7.9%.
- **Distribution network (branches and touchpoints)** — The physical footprint that sources the loans, expanded steadily from 93 branches and 224 distribution points in 2022 to 171 branches and 373 touchpoints by March 2026, with state-by-state performance discussed as the main growth driver.
- **Technology and digital platform** — Not sold separately, but management calls it central to how they source, underwrite, service and scale — 96% of customers on the mobile app, 81% of approvals via account aggregator, 80%+ of loans digitally fulfilled, and proprietary AI agents now live for income assessment and bank-statement analysis.
- **PMAY 2.0 government subsidy facilitation** — Helping customers claim government housing subsidies — around 4,500 applications received as of January 2026, with 103 customers having received a first tranche; small today but expected to gain traction.
- **Green homes certification** — A sustainability initiative in which financed homes are certified as green — 450 cumulative certifications by March 2026, mentioned as a side initiative rather than a revenue line.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Home First is, on the evidence here, a well-run affordable housing lender whose strengths sit almost entirely in how it is managed and how it reaches customers rather than in the economics of what it sells. Management scores highest of any quality area (1.67), and the record backs it: Manoj Viswanathan has led continuously across all 16 calls, the three-year distribution plan set out in FY22 was delivered on schedule — branches from 93 to 171, touchpoints from 224 to 373, entry into UP, MP and Rajasthan, a breakthrough in Mumbai and Pune that were previously 'bank-only' markets — and mid-teen returns promised 'in a couple of years' arrived by FY25. Just as telling, the team volunteers bad news it did not have to disclose: Tamil Nadu team churn and market-share loss, understaffed locations, tariff-driven delinquency, the e-khata land-record problem in Karnataka, and a plain admission that its extra provision buffers cannot be maintained forever. Growth capex has gone into distribution and technology while cost-to-income still improved 330 basis points to 32.5% and operating costs held at 2.6-2.7% of assets. This is the one pattern that fits cleanly: Corporate Culture is a STRONG FIT, and it is the reason the business quality score reaches 67.

The problem is that the same traits which power the growth story also cap it. The connector-led network of roughly 3,500 agents is the strongest moat evidence in the file, and it is what makes the Friendly Middleman and Market Share Gainers patterns partially work — AUM compounded from Rs 5,832 crore in June 2022 to Rs 15,878 crore in March 2026. But that loyalty has to be re-bought every year with faster turnaround times and a wider product range, which is exactly the mechanism behind the 'Good Enough Goods' risk: a not-very-complex product priced around 13% and sold through intermediaries who can point the same borrower elsewhere. Technology tells the same double story. Adoption is real and measurable — app registration 84% to 96%, account aggregator 36% to 81%, over 80% of loans fulfilled digitally, proprietary AI agents for income assessment — yet management itself concedes the edge is copyable within quarters, competitors were already running the same Salesforce stack back in 2022, and no scarce licence or data asset stands in a funded entrant's way. So Innovation Dominance is only a partial fit and Cost to Replicate is no fit at all. Most decisively, there is no pricing power anywhere: the book is 100% floating and priced off borrowing cost, the policy rate was raised 35bps in August 2024 purely to defend the spread and cut 10bps in January 2026 as funding costs fell, origination yield drifted from 13.4% to 13.0%, and customers demonstrably shop on price — balance transfers out ran from 5.5% up to 8.3%. That kills the Pricing Power, Toll Roads and Intangible Benefits cases outright and is why the multibagger fit score is only 15 out of 100.

The risk picture is the reassuring part of the file and follows directly from the same design choices. Nothing scores worse than WATCH, three risks are outright LOW, and the safety score of 80 is the single highest input into the 55-out-of-100 'Decent' rating. Concentration is low because the book is deliberately granular — small tickets of Rs 5 lakh to Rs 40 lakh, spread across nine states, sourced through thousands of connectors and funded by a widened set of banks alongside assignment and co-lending. Fashion and taste risk are low because the product is functional and contractual, held at 83% individual home loans and 15-20% loan-against-property for four years, with management explicitly wanting to be known only as a housing lender. Balance-sheet stewardship reinforces this: purely organic growth, no acquisitions, debt-to-equity around 3.6 times against 44% capital adequacy, provisions held above the modelled requirement, and a refusal to loosen tightened underwriting even when it costs growth. The four WATCH items — the credit cycle biting in the first half of FY26, the shallow technology moat, policy dependence through NHB refinance at 15-19% of borrowings and the RBI classification rule that inflates reported bad loans to 1.7% against 1.3% adjusted, and banks moving down-market with cheaper funding — are all live but none is rated severe. The honest read is a disciplined operator in a genuinely large and underpenetrated market whose industry structure scores negative (-0.5) and whose returns-on-capital and capital-allocation evidence is thin (0.0 and 0.5), with overall resilience still unproven. Good business, ordinary economics.

## What to watch

- Spread discipline: the Pricing Power check fails because the book is 100% floating; if the stated 5%-plus spread target slips as origination yield keeps drifting below 13.0%, the cost advantage stops offsetting the lack of pricing power.
- Balance-transfer-out rate: already up from 5.5% to 8.3% under the New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks — further increase would confirm banks are winning the seasoned, better-quality customers on price.
- Asset quality after the overlay runs out: management has admitted under the Candor check that ECL overlays cannot be maintained; watch whether the FY26 first-half stress reverses before that cushion is gone.
- Connector loyalty and staffing: the Friendly Middleman pattern depends on turnaround times and a wider bouquet; a repeat of the Tamil Nadu team churn and share loss in another state would show the distribution moat is people-dependent rather than structural.
- Policy and funding mix: NHB refinance at 15-19% of borrowings plus the e-khata and disbursement-circular disruptions under the Government Dependency risk — any tightening there hits funding cost and disbursements at the same time.
