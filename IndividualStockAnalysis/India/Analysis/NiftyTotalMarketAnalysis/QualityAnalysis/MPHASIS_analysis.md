# MphasiS Ltd. (MPHASIS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: IT*

Mphasis is an India-listed IT services company (traded on the BSE and NSE) that builds, modernises and runs computer systems and software for large companies overseas, with the United States described as its "anchor geography" and Europe as a smaller second market. It sells things like application development and modernisation of old systems (COBOL, Java), cloud migration, data work and, increasingly, AI and "agentic AI" solutions, mostly to banks and financial firms, but also telecom/media/tech, insurance, healthcare, retail and consumer-goods clients. It makes money by signing multi-year deals with those clients — management tracks "net new TCV" (total contract value of newly won work, $461 million in the June 2026 quarter) which converts into revenue as work ramps, billed either on time-and-materials or on a growing share of fixed-price contracts, and more recently as recurring, platform-attached revenue (ARR) and managed services. Much of the delivery is done offshore/nearshore from lower-cost locations, which is part of the cost saving it pitches to clients; FY27 guidance is high single-digit to low double-digit constant-currency revenue growth at a 14.75%-15.75% EBIT margin.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Direct business** — All the work the company sells straight to its own client relationships — this is essentially the whole company, at 94% of revenue as of the July 2022 call, and management says its growth 'more accurately represents our overall growth trajectory'.
- **DXC channel** — Legacy revenue that comes through partner DXC rather than direct client relationships; described as small and shrinking, down to 4.8% of revenue by mid-2022, and management effectively asks investors to look past it.
- **Banking and Financial Services (BFS)** — The company's self-described 'anchor vertical' — technology work for banks, lenders and mortgage firms (KYC, payments, underwriting, document processing) which had grown 20%+ year-on-year for eight straight quarters as of mid-2022 and is the single most important source of business.
- **TMT (technology, media and telecom)** — A named 'focus vertical' that management says more than doubled in FY22 and was still growing over 50% year-on-year in mid-2022 — smaller than BFS but one of the fastest-growing pieces.
- **Others / Enterprise Verticals (incl. healthcare, insurance, retail, CPG, logistics)** — A bundle of non-BFS industries, with healthcare called out as a growth driver on large deal wins and CPG/retail clients arriving via the Theory and Practice acquisition; management created five 'Enterprise Verticals' with dedicated leadership to win new clients here, and by 2026 highlights non-BFS pipeline growing faster than the total.
- **Applications service line** — The core delivery business of writing, maintaining and modernising client software, described in mid-2022 as the main growth driver with 41% growth, run through the company's 'Tribes & Squads' model of reusable skill teams and deal templates.
- **Mphasis.ai and the AI platform stack (NeoZeta, NeoCrux, NeoIP, Tria)** — A dedicated AI unit and a set of in-house software platforms — NeoZeta for converting legacy code, NeoCrux for speeding up developers, NeoIP and then Tria (with its 'Modernize' and 'Optimize' motions) for running governed AI decision-making across a client's business — which management now positions as the centre of the company: AI-led deals went from 12% to about 70% of the pipeline, and total pipeline is 2.8x its size at Mphasis.ai's launch.
- **Theory and Practice / Continuum AI (acquisition, 'TAP')** — An acquired team and 'decision intelligence' software used for forecasting, pricing, marketing and supply-chain decisions, bought to extend Mphasis beyond IT modernisation into business decision-making; it came with tier-one consumer-goods and retail clients already using it in production.
- **Hyperscaler and technology partnerships** — Joint go-to-market arrangements with cloud and AI vendors — notably an AWS Gen AI 'foundry' for financial-services clients and Microsoft Copilot adoption work — that source and accelerate deals rather than being a standalone revenue line.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

MphasiS comes out of these three reviews as a genuinely good operator inside a difficult industry, which is why the combined score lands at 61 out of 100 — a "Decent" three-star rating built from 64 on business quality, 55 on multibagger fit and 64 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound. The strongest part of the record is growth and the machine behind it: the quality framework scores Growth a perfect 1.0 and gives top marks to market-share gain (from 28% direct constant-currency growth in FY23 to more than twice industry growth in FY26, with wallet-share gains), to research and development (a continuous line from the AI labs of 2018 through NeoZeta and NeoCrux in 2024 to NeoIP and Tria in 2026), to steady margins (₹19 of operating profit per ₹100 of sales, moving only about ±1.7 points a year), and to the end market itself (38% pipeline growth, 69% of pipeline AI-led). Management scores well for the same reason: Nitin Rakesh has run one coherent strategy for a decade, held guidance bands through the 2024-25 revenue stall, and come out the other side with record TCV of $2.12bn. The pattern screen confirms all of this from a different angle — Innovation Dominance and Market Share Gainers are both rated a likely fit, and Brand Strength a likely fit on thirty years of banking and financial services reputation, with capital allocation scoring 0.8 on the back of consistently funding capability ahead of demand and paying out INR 62 per share for FY26 against 80-110% operating cash conversion.

The important thing is that the same traits producing those high marks are the ones producing the biggest risks, and the two reviews are describing one situation, not two. The AI investment that earns a top R&D score and drives the Innovation Dominance pattern is also why Technological Innovation is flagged as the dominant elevated risk across the whole seventeen-call arc: management's own language moved from AI being a tailwind in 2022, to conceding volume destruction in people-based service lines in July 2024, to admitting in July 2026 that annuity run deals are highly susceptible. The AI platform work is the defence against the very disruption it accelerates. Likewise, the deep, long-lived large-account relationships that give substance to the Cost to Replicate pattern — six clients above $75m, top five averaging around $150m each, one above $150m, thirty years of brownfield integration depth that AI-native startups lack — are precisely the Stakeholder Concentration risk, the second elevated flag, which bit visibly when top-10 accounts fell 10.1% year-on-year in Q1FY25. The offsetting evidence is real (DXC dependence run down from 4.8% of revenue to about 1.4%, new-client revenue growing 22-68% a year), but the book is deliberately built this way. And the cost-arbitrage story that has kept the company selling through downturns — "zero cost transformation", "Savings Led Transformation", "structural efficiency in a self-funded way" — is the Low Price Plus pattern rated only partial, and is the same fact underneath the Good Enough Goods risk, since clients now demand productivity gains be handed back.

That tension is what caps the score rather than any weakness in execution. Competitive advantage scores only 0.33 and industry structure is actually negative at -0.17, and the pattern screen agrees where it matters most: Pricing Power and Toll Roads are both outright no-fits. The evidence is the same in both places — the stated EBIT band was stepped down twice over the window, from 15.25-17% to 14.6-16% to 14.75-15.75%, and management describes pricing moving away from per-widget rates with volume destruction and client pass-backs. Four further risks sit on watch: cyclicality, given revenue swung from 22-28% constant-currency growth in FY23 down to roughly 3% by Q1FY25 and back to 7-8% by FY26/FY27; new entrants, with AI-native startups and hyperscalers discussed in explicitly defensive "right to win" terms; shifting customer preferences, with budget migrating out of the CIO's control into business-function P&Ls and management saying discretionary spend will not return to its former shape; and good-enough goods as above. Only fashion risk shows no signal at all. The company's answer to all four is the same single bet — move from selling people to selling platforms and IP, which is why the Recurring Revenue pattern is rated partial: fixed-price share is up about 490 basis points and the July 2026 call is the first to describe genuine platform-attached recurring revenue with ARR bundled into managed services, but that is a very recent development. Two smaller drags round it out: about 96 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and a bolt-on acquisition habit (Blink, Silverline, Theory and Practice) where each deal has dented margins in the integration quarter rather than improving them.
