# Samvardh. Mothe. (MOTHERSON) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Samvardhana Motherson International (SAMIL, listed as MOTHERSON) is an India-headquartered supplier that designs, engineers, manufactures, assembles and ships parts and sub-assemblies for other companies' products — management calls this its "D.E.M.A.L." (Design, Engineering, Manufacturing, Assembly and Logistics) capability. Most of its money comes from selling car parts — wiring harnesses, mirrors and vision systems, plastic modules, lighting and electronics — directly to global vehicle makers under long-term supply contracts, with raw-material cost swings (copper, polymers) largely passed through to customers after a one-to-two quarter lag. It runs a "globally local" model, making parts in or near the markets it serves — around 300 facilities across 41 countries at the 2022 investor meet — and grows through a mix of new greenfield plants in emerging markets and a steady stream of acquisitions. Revenue crossed INR 1.25 lakh crore in FY26 (up 11%, EBITDA margin 9.5%), with a stated order book ("book business") of USD 96 billion, and the company is deliberately diversifying into non-automotive areas such as aerospace and consumer electronics under its 3CX10 rule that no single country, customer or component should exceed 10% of business.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Wiring Harness** — Makes the bundled electrical wiring that runs through vehicles; a core automotive division that is copper-price sensitive, is adding a greenfield plant in Morocco, and is being expanded via the announced acquisition of 100% of Nexans Autoelectric's wiring harness business as a scalable platform for passenger- and commercial-vehicle growth globally.
- **Vision Systems Business Division** — Supplies mirrors and related vision products to carmakers; important enough to have its own COO on every earnings call and is adding a new greenfield plant in India.
- **Modules and Polymer** — Makes plastic parts and assembled modules for vehicles; management credits cost optimisation and plant efficiency gains here for holding FY26 group margins steady despite commodity inflation.
- **Lighting and Electronics** — One of the newer "emerging businesses"; its improving profitability was cited as a main driver of the 200 basis point EBITDA margin expansion in Q4 FY26.
- **Aerospace** — Makes metallic parts, sub-assemblies and wire harnesses for aircraft including business jets and rotary-wing (helicopter) programmes; revenue grew 40% in FY26 and roughly 10x over three years, with an order book up over 20% to USD 1.6 billion, built partly through the ADI Industries acquisition for engine-component machining.
- **Consumer Electronics** — A new vertical (including a JV with BIEL Crystal) making components for consumer electronics customers, with roughly INR 2,600 crore of planned investment; revenue rose about 7.5x in FY26, it turned EBITDA-profitable, two plants are running at a 14-16 million unit annual run-rate and a third plant with more upstream integration is due in Q3 FY27, aided by government ECMS incentives.
- **Non-automotive diversification (including Health and Medical)** — A broader push, first set out in the Vision 2025 plan and continued under Vision 2030, to apply the same manufacturing platform outside cars; several of the 12 greenfield plants under development are non-automotive, and an analyst noted the platform now spans aerospace, consumer electronics and health and medical.
- **Logistics and services / partnerships** — The "L" in D.E.M.A.L. — includes a dedicated RoRo terminal being developed at Dighi Port, Maharashtra for end-to-end handling of finished vehicles, plus a JV with Egtronics for clean-mobility electronics manufacturing.
- **Joint ventures and associates** — A portfolio of partly owned companies (including Japanese acquisitions Yachiyo, Atsumitec and the pending Yutaka Giken) whose profit share is a growing contributor to reported profit after tax.

### The story in depth

Motherson's story starts with the customer, and that is where the quality work scores highest — Customer Benefits is its strongest area (1.25) on the back of three full marks. The proposition is certainty: carmakers hand the company troubled plants and new geographies because they trust it to deliver, and relationships get closer, not looser, when conditions are rough. It builds in or near every market it serves, roughly 300 facilities across 41 countries sitting next to the customer, backed by a dedicated RoRo terminal at Dighi Port and a step up from third- to fourth-party logistics with Hellmann. The finances underneath are sound rather than spectacular: about ₹10 of operating profit on every ₹100 of sales with margins moving only around a point a year, sales compounding near 12% a year, and ₹19 of operating cash for every ₹100 of shareholder funds plus borrowings. Best of all, cash arrives from customers 26 days before suppliers have to be paid, so customers effectively finance the business.

Those same traits explain the one pattern that fits cleanly. Market Share Gainers is a strong fit precisely because weak European suppliers are the source of growth — carmakers hand over distressed businesses to be fixed, and loss-making 'red units' have been turned profitable. That shows up as growth well ahead of the market it sells into: outpacing industry growth by 8-9% in December 2022 while passenger vehicles were falling, and revenue up 17% in the March 2026 quarter and 11% for the year against roughly 2% global passenger vehicle growth, with a record USD 96bn of booked business. But the very mechanism that drives the pattern also drives the biggest risk. Stakeholder Concentration is the only channel rated elevated because the growth engines are relationship-anchored: three acquisitions tied to one Honda-related group, and deals done at the customer's behest. The stated 3CX10 policy — no single customer, country or component above 10% — is a real structural protection, but it constrains size, not dependence on who is doing the handing over. Everything else scores lower for related reasons. Only eight of the eleven patterns come out partial or absent, and the framework's weakest areas — Competitive Advantage, Industry Structure and Management, all at 0.33 — line up with the same picture. Entry barriers are meaningful (multi-year qualification, capital intensity) and margins have barely moved, consistent with rational pricing, yet the products themselves are contested. This is also plainly not an overlooked company: a $22.9bn-revenue listed group covered by Nomura, Jefferies, IIFL and Nuvama scores the worst possible mark on obscurity.

The risks are mostly the strengths viewed from the other side. The genuine cost culture — 'globally local' manufacturing, European transformative measures, closing red units, an obsession with 40% returns and asset turns — produces the thin 9.5% margin that makes Low Price Plus only a partial fit, and management explicitly rejects margin percentage as the yardstick, describing content growth as diluting it and absorbing a 38% rise in copper through lagged pass-throughs. That is why Pricing Power is a clear non-fit, and why Good Enough Goods sits on watch: much of the range is engineered to the customer's print, with the carmaker rather than a brand-aware end buyer controlling sourcing. Technology cuts both ways too — real in-house engineering (D.E.M.A.L., camera-mirror systems, aerospace parts) makes Innovation Dominance a likely fit, but research spending is never disclosed or discussed on calls and management deliberately avoids frontier bets, while the products sit directly in the path of mirrors becoming cameras and combustion giving way to electric. The order book is powertrain-agnostic at 22% electric with both types won, which softens that. New entrants is the least well answered: analysts pressed repeatedly on lower-cost Chinese carmakers displacing the European customers Motherson is most exposed to, and the chairman deflected rather than produced evidence. Management's own record is mixed — long-tenured family leadership, walking away from over-priced 2021 deals, borrowings cut to an all-time low of 0.8 times — but the headline $36bn revenue and 40% return promise was badly missed. Netting it out, the combined rating is 56 out of 100, or Decent: 66 for business quality, 36 for multibagger fit and 62 for risk safety, with overall resilience judged strained.

### Market Share Gainers — STRONG FIT
*"Winning customers from rivals"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence and the numbers agree (1 of 1 check computable supporting).

**What the calls show:** Persistent, repeated outperformance of underlying production across the full period: Dec 2022 'outpacing the industry growth by 8-9%' while PV declined; Q4 FY26 revenue +17% and FY26 +11% against ~2% global PV growth, with a record USD 96bn book business and 10x aerospace / 7.5x consumer-electronics scale-up.

> "the passenger industry has actually declined. There is de-growth in the industry, yet Motherson is growing, is outpacing the industry growth by 8- 9%" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Sales compounding at 12% while margins held or improved (9% → 9%) — growth that was WON, not bought with price cuts.

### Innovation Dominance — LIKELY FIT
*"Out-inventing the competition"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits and every one of the 2 computable checks supports the pattern.
