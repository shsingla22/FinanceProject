# Power Finance Corporation Ltd. (PFC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

PFC (Power Finance Corporation Ltd.) is a government-of-India-owned non-banking finance company, based in New Delhi, that lends money to India's power sector — it finances power and infrastructure projects for both government-owned and private borrowers, including a large renewable-energy book. It makes money on the spread between what it charges borrowers and what it pays to raise funds: in FY26 its lending yield was 9.96% against a 7.50% cost of funds, giving a spread of 2.46% and a net interest margin of 3.55%. It funds itself through borrowings that include large foreign-currency deals, such as a USD 1.265 billion foreign currency term loan arranged with banks in IFSC GIFT City. On a consolidated basis it runs the largest NBFC loan book in India at about INR 11.64 lakh crore with the highest NBFC profit (FY26 PAT of INR 33,625 crore), and it also spends real effort recovering money from a shrinking pile of stressed/defaulted power projects.

**The parts of the business:**

- **PFC standalone lending (power and infrastructure financing)** — The core business of sanctioning and disbursing project loans to power and infrastructure borrowers in India — the standalone loan book was INR 493,363 crore in H1 FY25 with INR 66,146 crore disbursed in that half-year, and standalone FY26 net profit was a record INR 20,051 crore.
- **REC (52.63%-owned subsidiary)** — A separately listed power-sector lender that PFC acquired a controlling 52.63% stake in back in 2019, with a very similar business model and regulatory framework; it is the main reason the consolidated book (~INR 11.64 lakh crore) is more than twice the standalone book, and PFC and REC are now working toward a full merger targeted for 1 April 2027.
- **Renewable energy financing** — Lending to renewable power projects, where management says the group holds a leadership position with one of the largest renewable books in the market at INR 1.65 lakh crore as of FY26.
- **PFC Infra Finance IFSC Limited (GIFT City subsidiary)** — A newly approved finance-company subsidiary in IFSC GIFT City, Gujarat — described as the first IFSC finance company dedicated to power and infrastructure — set up to make foreign-currency loans to both Indian and overseas power and infrastructure projects, government and private.
- **Stressed asset resolution / recoveries** — An ongoing workout function that resolves defaulted project loans through NCLT (bankruptcy) and outside it; roughly 80% of the peak NPA book has been resolved, cutting the Stage-3 book to INR 6,323 crore across 19 projects by FY26, and recoveries release provisions back into profit (about INR 1,800 crore of provision write-backs in FY26).

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

The strongest part of the story is the market PFC lends into, and the scale it has built there. The quality framework gives its best mark to structural end-market growth: installed power capacity past 530 GW, a record 55 GW of non-fossil additions in FY26, record peak demand of 256 GW, and new financing needs from storage and hybrid projects. On top of that sits an identifiable capex upturn, with roughly 84 GW of thermal additions targeted by 2030 and large conventional sanctions already booked. PFC meets that demand as the largest NBFC by loan book (about INR 11.64 lakh crore consolidated), the highest NBFC profit at INR 33,625 crore, and the largest renewable book at INR 1.65 lakh crore. Behind those numbers is a genuinely repaired balance sheet — gross NPAs of 9.39% in FY19 down to 1.09% credit-impaired in FY26, net NPAs of 0.13%, the bad-loan book down 80% from its peak — and a management team that resolved Lanco Amarkantak, TRN Energy and Sinnar Thermal exactly as it had told investors it would.

The problem is that almost none of that scale converts into pricing power, and the same traits that create the favourable patterns also create the risks. The 'Cost to Replicate' and 'Low Price Plus' patterns both rest on cheap, sovereign-linked funding (incremental borrowing at 6.27% blended in H1 FY25, a weighted average of 7.5%, access to dollar, yen and euro markets including a USD 1.265bn facility) and on decades of power-sector underwriting and recovery skill worth more than 60% on resolved stressed assets. But that same government anchoring is the single highest-rated risk in the review: borrowers are overwhelmingly state utilities, asset growth tracks national capacity targets, provisioning swings on a government rating exercise, and the corporate structure itself is set by the Union Budget. Likewise, the scale that supports the 'Market Share Gainers' case is exactly what is now being attacked. Once banks and lenders previously absent from power financing began refinancing PFC's commissioned assets, PFC defended volume with price rather than holding rate — the framework's harshest check, at minus two. Yields fell from 10.11% to 9.96%, spreads slipped from 2.61% to 2.46% with guidance trimmed to 2.40–2.50%, prepayments ran ahead of plan, and loan growth of 7% still missed the 10–11% guidance. That is the 'New Entrants' and 'Good Enough Goods' risks and the weak-barriers check describing one event: lending here is a largely undifferentiated product, so a rival with cheaper money simply takes the loan.

The remaining strengths are real but small or unproven relative to that pressure. Innovation shows up as new financing formats rather than technology — the first IFSC-based finance company dedicated to power and infrastructure, a BCG-led process overhaul from FY25, and early sanctioning of battery and pumped storage worth about INR 16,000 crore cumulatively — and the same GIFT City vehicle plus roughly INR 21,000 crore of non-power infrastructure lending is the main answer to the concentration risk, though management itself says the international push must start slow pending capital. Shareholder distribution is disciplined and consistent at INR 18.55 per share in FY26, but there are no buybacks and no link between payout and valuation. The largest capital-allocation event is not of the company's choosing: a government-announced mega-merger with REC, its own 52.63%-owned subsidiary, still needing cabinet and presidential approval with the valuation undetermined — which the framework marks down and which sits squarely inside the government-dependency risk. Netting it out, the scoring gives business quality 46, multibagger fit 11 and risk safety 48, for 36 out of 100 and a Mixed, two-star verdict: a scale lender in a genuinely growing market whose returns are being competed away, with return on capital scored at the floor and industry structure negative, and with overall resilience still unknown.
