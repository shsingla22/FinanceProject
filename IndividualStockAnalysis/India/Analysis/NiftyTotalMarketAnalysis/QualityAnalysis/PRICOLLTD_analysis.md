# Pricol (PRICOLLTD) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Pricol is an Indian auto-components maker headquartered in Coimbatore that designs and manufactures parts sold mainly to vehicle makers (OEMs) — two-wheeler, passenger vehicle and commercial vehicle companies such as TVS, Suzuki Motorcycle and Tata Motors — rather than to end consumers. Its best-known product is the instrument cluster (the dashboard display behind the handlebar or steering wheel), and it also makes pumps, disc brakes, chain tensioners and, more recently, moulded polymer parts. It earns money by winning a place on a customer's vehicle model, then supplying parts month after month against rolling 24-month schedules (management says there is no conventional 'order book'); raw-material and commodity cost increases are passed back to customers on a roughly 100% indexed basis with a three-to-six month lag. It operates mainly in India with some exports and international OEM business, spends about 4.5–4.7% of revenue on engineering/R&D, and had consolidated revenue of just under ₹4,000 crore in FY26 with EBITDA margins of roughly 12–13% and very little net debt.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Driver Information and Connected Vehicle Solutions (DIS / DICVS)** — Instrument clusters and dashboard electronics — from cheap mechanical clusters (~₹300 each) up to electromechanical and TFT digital clusters (~₹1,500 to ₹8,800 each) — plus connected-vehicle and telematics software, HMI screens and an 'E-Cockpit' product still at prototype/concept stage; this is the largest business, about 69% of revenue in FY24, and management says its cluster market share has held by volume and risen by value.
- **Actuation, Control and Fluid Management Systems (ACFMS)** — Mechanical and fluid-handling parts — pumps (including complicated industrial oil pumps at around $800 each), chain tensioners (~₹75), and the strategic new disc brake system (~₹1,150), which started with six customers and EV startups and is due to ramp with a large Indian two-wheeler OEM; this was about 31% of revenue in FY24 and was expected to rise toward 35–36%.
- **Pricol Precision Products / 'P3L' (polymers)** — A recently acquired polymers/precision-moulding business that management counts as the third segment and credits for much of FY26's inorganic growth; it has won new customer business, is currently capacity-constrained, and is getting a new plant plus a technology centre of excellence.
- **Technology partnerships and new products** — Not a separate reporting segment, but a repeated theme: tie-ups with Sibros (connected-vehicle software), BMS PowerSafe (EV battery management systems), Candera (HMI software), a Chinese firm TYW (screens) and PSG College of Technology, which management uses to argue Pricol is a technology company rather than a commodity component supplier.

### The story in depth

Pricol is an automotive component supplier whose strongest suit is the people running it. The management checks score highest of any area (1.33), and the Corporate Culture pattern is the one outright STRONG FIT in the screen: a promise made in 2022 to reach ₹4,000 crore of revenue by FY26 with debt no greater than equity was delivered on time with next-to-no debt (net debt ₹63 crore), long-term borrowings were taken to nil and kept there, and every rupee of a large building programme — ₹350-400 crore in the FY23 plan, a ₹600 crore three-year organic programme, ₹500 crore for the next two to three years — came out of the company's own cash. Return on capital employed rose from 20.68% to 23.18% while that spending happened, and the business turns ₹1 of assets into ₹1.63 of sales. Management also talks straight about what goes wrong: it owned the export failure, the four customers whose delayed start-of-production cut FY24 growth from about 19% to 16%, and it pre-flagged that one of four new product bets could fail. The end markets help — more electronics per vehicle, mechanical dials giving way to TFT screens, telematics, and a likely braking mandate for two-wheelers.

What that good behaviour has not yet bought is a moat you can price with. Only one other pattern comes close to fitting — Market Share Gainers, where cluster share on Tata cars went from 58% in May 2024 to 75-80% by May 2026, alongside stated majority positions in two-wheelers, commercial and off-road vehicles. Four patterns are outright misses: there is no consumer brand (only customer quality awards), no dealer or installer channel choosing the product, no move toward owning the customer directly (the company is integrating backwards into circuit boards and displays instead), and, most importantly, no pricing power. Prices are cost-plus, negotiated with the vehicle makers; in May 2026 management said the input shock — polymer up 55%, aluminium up 62%, semiconductors up 35% — would have to be shared and could not be fully recovered. That single trait cuts both ways. Back-to-back commodity indexation with customers is exactly what keeps the Cyclicality risk down to WATCH rather than worse, and it is exactly why Pricing Power scores NO FIT and why Toll Roads is only PARTIAL: the parts are small, critical and locked in by multi-year letters of intent, but the toll is not set on Pricol's terms. The same is true of the engineering spend — a steady 4.5-4.7% of sales with new products running 20-25% of revenue every year is the basis of both the PARTIAL Innovation Dominance verdict and the PARTIAL Cost to Replicate barrier, yet the flagship battery-management and E-Cockpit programmes have slipped since 2022.

The risks are mostly the strengths seen from the other side. Being deeply designed into a few big customers is what produced the share gains, and it is also the Stakeholder Concentration risk: Tata alone carries Pricol clusters on 75-80% of its cars, two-wheelers are about half of revenue, and cost recovery runs through the top 10-12 customers. The same closeness invites the New Entrants risk, where an analyst's channel check flagged an OEM group company moving into instrument clusters in the segment worth roughly 60% of revenue — a competitor of a structurally different kind, since it would be making the part in-house. The one ELEVATED risk, Good Enough Goods, sits directly against the M&A and product-mix record: chain tensioners at ₹75, oil pumps and a 'little more commoditized' vertical are openly low-margin, and the newly bought P3L polymer business earns 9.33% EBITDA against a group figure of about 12.4%, so a deal that should double turnover in three years dilutes margin rather than lifting it. The offset in every case is the deliberate climb up the value chain — mechanical clusters at roughly ₹300 replaced by TFT at up to ₹8,000, commodity lines exited, a propulsion-neutral product that makes the electric-vehicle shift a LOW risk. Growth itself is the weakest quality area (0.33): about 12% a year on average, but lumpy. Add it up — 68 for business quality, 51 for pattern fit, 70 for risk safety — and the blended score is 63 out of 100, a Decent three-star business with SOUND resilience: well run and well financed, but not yet one that sets its own price.

### Cost to Replicate — PARTIAL
*"Could a newcomer copy this?"*

### Good Enough Goods — ELEVATED
*"Cheaper rivals that are good enough"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** A meaningful slice of the portfolio is openly described as commoditised and low-margin — chain tensioners at Rs.75, oil pumps, and the ACFMS vertical as 'a little more commoditized' — while the newly acquired P3L polymer business earns only 9.33% EBITDA on plain moulding and painting; with 100% commodity pass-through and OEMs unable to absorb FY27 cost inflation, pricing power sits with the customer, not the brand.

### Cyclicality — WATCH
