# CreditAccess Grameen Ltd. (CREDITACC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

CreditAccess Grameen (CA Grameen) is an Indian microfinance lender that gives small, mostly unsecured loans to low-income households in rural and semi-urban India, traditionally to women organised into groups ("centers") who meet and repay together — management describes it as serving "the bottom of the pyramid" and notes it has historically financed "only one woman per household". It earns money the way a lender does: it borrows wholesale from banks, domestic markets (including NCDs) and foreign lenders at around 9.2-9.8%, lends out at a portfolio yield of roughly 21% (which it calls the lowest in the microfinance industry), and keeps the spread — an interest spread of about 11% and net interest margin of 14%, less operating costs (cost-to-income ~29-30%) and loan losses (credit cost, which swung from ~2.2% in good years to 6.74% in the FY26 stress year). It runs a branch-and-field-staff model: about 2,276 branches across 457 districts and ~22,000 employees serving roughly 44 lakh borrowers, with a loan book (AUM) of about INR 30,319 Crore as of Q1 FY27, up from about INR 15,615 Crore in mid-2022. It is concentrated in rural India with Karnataka and a small group of "top 3 states" historically dominant, and it is deliberately expanding into newer states and into larger, longer-tenure retail loans for its older customers.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Group Loans (GL) / microfinance** — The core business — small joint-liability loans made to groups of rural women for income-generating activities, collected largely in cash at weekly/periodic center meetings; it was essentially the whole company (INR 25,542 Crore of a INR 26,304 Crore book in Q1 FY25) and even after diversification still makes up roughly 80% of AUM, with growth driven by adding ~1 lakh new borrowers a month, about 35-38% of whom are new-to-credit.
- **Retail Finance (RF)** — Larger, individually underwritten loans sold to long-vintage microfinance customers who "graduate" upward — this is the fastest-growing part, going from a tiny INR 762 Crore book in Q1 FY25 (and 5.9% of AUM in March 2025) to 18.1% of AUM in March 2026 and 20.6% by June 2026.
- **Individual business loans (incl. "Unnati")** — Unsecured business loans to individual borrowers rather than groups, given to seasoned customers — management cites Unnati borrowers averaging 7.7 years of relationship history and a 732 credit score; it is one of the three retail products launched in the last ~3 years and one of the company's three customer-acquisition channels.
- **Mortgage-backed loans** — Secured loans against property to existing customers moving up the ladder (average vintage 6.2 years, average credit score 714), part of the push into "higher ticket secured retail products".
- **Two-wheeler financing** — Vehicle loans, the newest of the retail products named by management; the calls give no separate size for it, only that it sits inside the retail finance expansion.
- **Grameen Mahi (customer app / digital channel)** — A mobile app used both to collect repayments digitally and to source business — 15.4 lakh active users (34.5% of borrowers) by Q1 FY27, with digital collections rising from 14% to 24.2% of total collections; management intends it to become a channel for instant eligibility checks, small-ticket loans and lead generation.
- **MMFL (Madura Micro Finance)** — A separate microfinance subsidiary being integrated into the parent in the earlier calls — in FY23 it ran on two technology platforms (73% of its book on CA Grameen's system, 27% on its own) and had weaker collection efficiency (93% vs 97% at CA Grameen); it is not mentioned as a distinct unit in the later calls.
- **Borrowing / liability franchise** — Not a customer business but central to how it makes money — it funds itself from banks, private NCD issues (e.g. INR 425 Crore in Q1 FY27) and foreign lenders (24% of the liability mix), backed by an 'AA-/Stable' rating, INR 3,536 Crore of cash, capital adequacy of ~24.9% and equity of INR 8,347 Crore, with management stressing that 86% of net-worth growth since FY20 came from retained profits.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

CreditAccess Grameen's core engine is cheap money turned into cheap loans, delivered through a very large physical network. The company borrows at the industry's lowest cost (9.2% by Q4 FY26, helped by an AA- upgrade in August 2022 and 24% foreign borrowings), keeps its cost-to-income ratio near 29-30%, and lends at the industry's lowest yield of about 21%. That combination is what earns the 'Low Price Plus' pattern a strong fit, and it rests on a distribution base that also scores at the top of the quality framework: 2,276 branches across 457 districts, roughly 22,000 field staff, doorstep centre meetings and a vernacular app used by 15.4 lakh borrowers. Both the distribution advantage and convenience checks score the maximum, and the same network underpins the partial fit on 'Cost to Replicate' — 44 lakh customers with six-to-eight-year repayment histories that a new lender cannot simply buy. The second pillar is management, the highest-scoring quality area at 1.33. The 'Corporate Culture' pattern is a strong fit for a specific, checkable reason: the July 2024 call raised credit-cost guidance before the cycle broke, and the May 2026 call stated plainly that FY26 credit cost of 6.74% overshot the 5.5-6.0% guidance and that returns missed. That candour sits alongside conservative leverage of 3.0x, a 24.9% capital ratio, growth funded from internal accruals, and an internal promotion pipeline with attrition falling from 33.5% to 20.6%.

The awkward part is that the very traits producing the strengths also produce the weaknesses, so the record is not a list of pluses offset by unrelated minuses. Deliberately being the cheapest lender is why 'Pricing Power' is a clear no-fit — management has repeatedly committed to passing funding-cost gains back to borrowers rather than keeping them, in August 2022 after the rating upgrade and again in July 2026 with a planned cut — and it is why the 'Intangible benefits' check scores negative: there is no image-driven premium to charge. It also leaves the company exposed on 'Good Enough Goods', since a microfinance loan is a simple, commoditised product with little customer loyalty and competition runs on price. Similarly, the branch build-out that creates the moat is precisely what makes the balance sheet heavy — INR 30,319 Crore of assets on INR 8,347 Crore of net worth — which is why 'Asset turn' scores negative and Return on Capital is the worst quality area at -1.0. The clearest overlap is the cycle itself. 'Cyclical market growth' scores a full two points because the company is visibly coming out of the trough, with credit cost guided down from 6.74% to 3-4% and profit up 720% year-on-year. Those are the same numbers that make Cyclicality a high risk: return on equity was 23.5% in Q1 FY25, 10.7% for FY26, and 24.4% in Q1 FY27. One reading calls it recovery, the other calls it volatility; they are the same fact.

Outside factors do much of the driving here, which is what caps the overall score at 47 out of 100 and the grade at Mixed. Government dependency is rated high risk not because of subsidies — there are none — but because rule changes reset the business directly: RBI's March 2022 harmonised guidelines suppressed disbursements for two months, and industry-body MFIN guardrails capping how many lenders one borrower can hold reshaped the FY25-26 cycle. That cycle is also the New Entrants risk in another guise: too many lenders chasing the same borrower created the over-leverage that blew up credit costs, which is why Industry Structure scores negative at -0.17 even as Growth scores 0.8. Management's answer to several of these at once is the shift into retail finance, which went from 5.9% to 20.6% of assets in about two years. That single move drives the partial fit on 'Innovation Dominance', earns a point on research and development, softens the shifting-consumer-preferences risk as borrowers move beyond the classic group-lending model, and reduces reliance on the most heavily regulated segment. What it does not yet do is answer the question of whether returns hold through a full cycle — resilience is recorded as unknown, market-share claims are never backed by explicit percentages against named competitors, and roughly half the book still sits in Karnataka and Maharashtra. The scoring reflects that split: 59 for business quality and 44 for risk safety, but only 30 on multibagger pattern fit, because four of the eleven patterns are outright no-fits and the cheap-lending model rules several of them out by design rather than by accident.

**What the calls show:** Regulator and industry-body policy repeatedly and directly reset the business: RBI's March 2022 harmonised microfinance guidelines forced a two-month process transition that suppressed April-May 2022 disbursements and borrower additions; MFIN guardrails (INR 2 lakh limit, lender-count cap) then reshaped the entire industry's growth path and drove CAGL's AUM share of >3-lender borrowers from 25.3% to 3.3%; pricing itself moves under a board-approved pricing policy in a rate-sensitive regulatory glare, and general-election code-of-conduct restrictions materially impaired collections in Q1 FY25.

*Silver lining: No subsidy or government-contract revenue, no fixed-infrastructure anchor, and diversification into retail finance (20.6% of AUM) outside the tightly-regulated MFI perimeter; guardrails have also cut sector over-leverage, so policy has been two-way rather than purely punitive.*

## What to watch

- Whether FY27 credit cost actually lands in the guided 3-4% range — the Cyclicality risk turns on this, and FY26's 6.74% already overshot its 5.5-6.0% guidance once.
- Any further RBI or MFIN rule change on lender counts, ticket sizes or process — the Government Dependency risk shows past changes hitting disbursements and the loan book directly.
- Whether retail finance keeps growing past 20.6% of assets at equal or better risk-adjusted returns — this is the single lever behind the Shifting Consumer Preferences and Innovation Dominance verdicts.
- The Karnataka and Maharashtra share of the book, currently about half — the Stakeholder Concentration watch depends on new borrower additions continuing to come from outside the top three states.
- What happens to margins after the planned 50 bps price cut in H2 FY27 — with Pricing Power a no-fit and Good Enough Goods on watch, protection has to come from the funding-cost and cost-to-income advantage, not from price.
