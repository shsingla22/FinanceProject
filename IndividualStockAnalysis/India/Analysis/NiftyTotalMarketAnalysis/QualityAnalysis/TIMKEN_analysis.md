# Timken India Ltd. (TIMKEN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

Timken India Limited makes and sells bearings — the precision metal parts that let wheels, shafts and rollers turn — mainly tapered roller bearings, with newly added spherical and cylindrical roller bearings from its Bharuch plant, plus plain bearings via the Timken GGB business the Board approved merging in. It sells to Indian Railways and wagon builders, truck and off-highway vehicle makers, and heavy process industries such as steel, cement and power, and also to aftermarket buyers through a distributor network; it manufactures at plants including Jamshedpur (its railway centre of excellence) and Bharuch, imports some finished bearings for trading, and exports roughly a fifth of sales, largely to group companies serving North and South American rail and heavy-truck markets. Revenue comes from selling bearings by volume at a spread over steel and other input costs, so margins swing with steel prices, currency and product mix, and management passes cost increases on to customers with a lag. It also runs service work inside customer plants — staffing 26–27 roll shops at Indian steel companies 24x7 for mounting, demounting and de-choking. Standalone revenue was INR3,148 crore in FY26 (up 8.6%), with a record INR1,000-crore-plus quarter in Q4 FY26 at a 19.3% PBT margin.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Rail** — Bearings for railway wagons, locomotives, metro and Vande Bharat trains, sold to Indian Railways and wagon builders and also exported; it was 23% of FY24 revenue (INR128.6 crore in Q3 FY26), management claims around 50% market share, calls it seasonal quarter to quarter but a multi-year growth story, and is expanding rail capacity at Jamshedpur.
- **Mobile / Others** — Bearings for commercial vehicles — heavy trucks, on-highway and off-highway equipment — running about 20% of revenue (INR157.1 crore in Q3 FY26, up 20% year-on-year on volume rather than higher content per vehicle).
- **Distribution** — Replacement and aftermarket sales through auto and industrial distributors, steady at roughly 18% of revenue (INR138 crore in Q3 FY26); management says price increases are easier to pass on here than to OEM customers.
- **Process industries** — Bearings and on-site services for steel rolling mills, cement, power and other stationary heavy plant, about 19% of FY24 revenue and the largest single segment in Q3 FY26 at INR167 crore, underpinned by India's steel and infrastructure capacity build-out.
- **Exports (including intercompany)** — Sales out of the Indian plants to Timken group companies and overseas markets, mainly rail and heavy-truck bearings for the Americas; it fell from 28% of revenue in FY23 to 20% in FY24 as global truck demand went soft, then rebounded to INR222 crore (21% of sales) in Q4 FY26, up about 40% sequentially.
- **New Bharuch plant — spherical and cylindrical roller bearings (SRB/CRB)** — An roughly INR600 crore investment announced in 2022 to make in India the SRBs and CRBs (up to 400mm) previously imported, targeting a combined domestic market management sized at about INR4,000 crore; all lines are now capitalised but sales were only INR12–15 crore in Q3 FY26 as customer PPAP approvals and ramp-up proceed, and ramp-up costs are currently weighing on margins.
- **Plain bearings (Timken GGB)** — A separate plain-bearing business being merged into Timken India for operational synergies and lower cost, with capacity expansion at Bharuch described as broadly on track; the calls give no revenue figure for it beyond consolidated numbers slightly above standalone.
- **Traded / imported bearings and services** — Bearings imported finished and resold in India (exposing the company to currency moves), alongside in-plant service work such as running 26–27 roll shops inside steel customers' facilities around the clock; the calls treat these as supporting activities rather than reported segments.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Timken India earns its keep on a single, well-evidenced idea: it sells a small part that customers cannot afford to have fail. The quality work scores full marks on assurance benefits (zero-failure aerospace and defence requirements, longer bearing life, rail safety as the stated purchase criteria), on convenience (on-site roll shops running inside steel plants around the clock, senior-level direct selling), and on switching costs, where customer PPAP and qualification cycles plus decades of incumbency make it the 'natural supplier' on critical applications. That same trait is exactly what makes the Toll Roads pattern a strong fit across the Dec-2022, May-2024 and May-2026 calls — a mission-critical component sold on life and engineering rather than price. It also shows up in the numbers: ₹18 of operating profit on every ₹100 of sales, with margins moving only about ±0.7 points a year, which the framework reads as rational pricing in an industry few new entrants can crack. The second strong pattern, Corporate Culture, rests on the same file: one leader running the calls from 2022 through FY26, the CRB/SRB plant promised in Nov-2022 tracked on schedule in May-2024 and shipping by FY26, capital committed only against an explicit asset-turn test above 2x, and management that quantifies each margin headwind to the basis point and admits missing its own utilisation target rather than deflecting.

The awkward part is that the traits producing those strengths also produce the three high risks. The ~50% share of Indian railway bearings that scores as a mini-monopoly is the same rail business that makes Government Dependency a high risk: rail is the largest front-end at 23% of FY26 sales (34% in Q4FY24), and its demand is set by Indian Railways budgets, mega wagon tenders, electrification and Vande Bharat programmes, with management framing the outlook around the Rail Ministry's intent. The parent relationship that supplies the continuously refreshed sealing, greasing and metallurgy technology — and keeps technological disruption a low risk — is also why Innovation Dominance is only a partial fit (no R&D spend or pipeline disclosed at the Indian entity) and why Stakeholder Concentration is high: exports are intercompany sales to the parent at 21% of FY26 sales, ₹707 crore, with volumes decided by the parent's global sourcing and pricing set through a BAPA. And the industrial end-markets that give durable secular growth — rail build-out, low per-capita steel consumption, defence, aviation MRO — are cyclical by construction: exports fell from 28% of the mix in FY23 to 14% in Q4FY24 before jumping 66% year on year in FY26, swinging with steel prices, US Class 8 truck and China wind cycles, and customer capex. The offsets are real rather than decorative: distribution and MRO at 17-19% of sales every year, rail replacement demand described as slow and steady, five front-ends each at 15-26% of the pie, and counter-cyclical investment funded from operating cash through the FY24-25 export downturn.

The middle of the picture is where the pattern screen and the risk review agree, and it is why the combined score lands at 50 out of 100 — Decent, or three stars, from 72 on business quality, 30 on multibagger fit and 36 on risk safety. Six of eleven patterns are only partial: the replacement annuity exists but is never discussed as contracted revenue; the brand carries specification pull and 40 years of incumbency but no distributor leverage; leadership is asserted in tapered roller bearings and rail but no share series against named competitors is given; and the time-based barriers — application knowledge, multi-year approvals, ₹720 crore state-of-the-art plants — are barriers an already-funded global peer could also clear. Pricing power is the clearest example of a strength that stops short: price realisation is a standing management effort, pushed even when steel softened, but the May-2026 call shows recovery negotiated, lagged and incomplete, with OEMs resisting and only about 10% of cost inflation recovered. That is the same evidence behind the Good Enough Goods watch, where management concedes the auto aftermarket is commoditising toward cheap bearings and that some part numbers cannot be won without killing margins. Its answer — refusing volume bought with price and tilting toward critical applications where the buyer measures bearing life — is what makes Low Price Plus and Friendly Middleman clear non-fits and keeps the discipline intact, at the cost of ceding the price-driven end. On the framework's own scoring, the weakest areas are capital allocation and competitive advantage breadth, with a 15% cash return on capital and asset turns of only about 2x; resilience is nonetheless judged sound.

The story, in one line: a genuinely toll-road-like business run by candid, disciplined stewards, whose durability comes from the very customers and parent that concentrate its fortunes, and whose margins hold steady only as long as it can keep walking away from the cheap end of the market.
