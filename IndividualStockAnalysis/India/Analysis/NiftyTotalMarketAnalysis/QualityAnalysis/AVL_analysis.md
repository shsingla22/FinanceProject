# Aditya Vision (AVL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aditya Vision Limited (AVL) is an Indian consumer-electronics and home-appliance retail chain that started with a single showroom in Patna, Bihar in 1999 and ran 207 stores across four states as of March 31, 2026. It buys finished goods from original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) and resells them to walk-in retail customers in smaller, under-penetrated 'Hindi heartland' towns and district headquarters — management stresses it is 'pure retailing' with no private labels of its own. It makes money on the gross margin between what it pays OEMs and what customers pay (about 15.6% gross margin and 8.5% EBITDA margin in FY26, on revenue of INR2,672 crores and PAT of INR117 crores), so growth comes from opening more stores plus same-store sales growth (8% in FY26) rather than from manufacturing. Its markets are Bihar (its dominant home state, over 50% market share per a Crisil report cited in 2023 and 75% of FY26 revenue), Uttar Pradesh (13%), Jharkhand (12%) and, newly, Chhattisgarh, with Madhya Pradesh named as the next state to enter.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Retail stores (consumer durables showrooms)** — The entire business — company-operated showrooms selling branded electronics and appliances to retail customers, growing from 105 stores at the FY23 call to 207 stores at FY26, with 102 of those added in just the last three years and a stated discipline of store breakeven within six months and a three-year payback.
- **Cooling category (air conditioners, refrigerators)** — Historically the biggest and highest-margin demand driver, tied to the summer season — so important that a weak summer in FY26 dragged H1 growth to 11% and management calls out cooling-led demand as the reason Q1 was traditionally the make-or-break quarter.
- **Panel televisions and washing machines** — Other core appliance categories; in Q3 FY26 washing machines and panel TVs were the standout performers, each growing over 30% year-on-year.
- **Mobiles and laptops** — A meaningful but lower-priced category also sold in the same stores — mobiles grew about 20% in Q3 FY26, and management flagged expected price rises of 4%–6% from January 2026 on memory-chip costs without expecting demand to fall.
- **Bihar (core market)** — The original and dominant geography — 118 stores across 38 districts and roughly 74%–75% of revenue in FY26, described as fortified and consolidated over 1999–2021 before the company expanded outward.
- **Jharkhand** — First state outside Bihar, entered in FY22; 33 stores covering 22 of 24 districts and about 12% of FY26 revenue.
- **Uttar Pradesh** — Entered at the end of FY23 with four stores in Eastern UP, later pushing into Central UP; now 53 stores across 30 of 75 districts and about 13%–14% of revenue, and the focus of heavier marketing spend in bigger cities like Lucknow that pressured Q3 FY26 margins.
- **Chhattisgarh (and planned Madhya Pradesh)** — The newest state, entered ahead of plan with 3 stores in 2 of 33 districts as the fourth target state, with Madhya Pradesh flagged as the next entry in FY27.
- **Aditya Seva (customer service call centre)** — An in-house grievance-handling call centre described in the FY23 call as having over 30 employees serving customers across the then-three states, part of the customer-stickiness pitch behind the tagline 'Sambandh Bharose Ka'.
- **Inventory buying / stocking strategy** — Not a separate segment but repeatedly described as central to how AVL earns: it deliberately builds inventory ahead of festive and summer seasons and ahead of price hikes (INR439 crores at Sept 2024; about INR840 crores at March 2026, stocked early on BEE norm changes and feared supply disruption), so it can buy cheap, stay in stock and protect margin.
- **OEM relationships and third-party consumer finance** — Long-term tie-ups with branded manufacturers supply the goods and support store expansion, while NBFC/consumer-finance companies — described as aggressive and competitive in this space — fund customer purchases, though the calls do not present financing as AVL's own revenue line.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Aditya Vision is, first and foremost, a share gainer, and almost everything good in these records traces back to that single engine. It sells consumer durables through 207 company-owned stores in small towns and district headquarters across Bihar, Jharkhand, Uttar Pradesh and Chhattisgarh, having doubled its store count from 105 and added 102 stores in three years against 105 built in the prior 23. The quality framework gives full marks to its geographical expansion, its market-share capture (Bihar above 50% in FY23 rising to roughly 60% by FY25, Jharkhand about 15% within two years), and its use of capital for growth rather than upkeep, with stores said to break even in six months and pay back in three years, alongside a 28% return on capital and 60% return on equity on a leased-store model where each rupee of assets produces ₹1.82 of sales. The multibagger screen agrees on exactly one thing without reservation: Market Share Gainers is a strong fit, and there is visible room left, with the company present in only 24 of Uttar Pradesh's 75 districts. The convenience of being physically close to customers who want to see and touch the goods, and 27 years of trust built under the 'Sambandh Bharose Ka' banner with zero store closures since inception, are the reasons that share keeps moving its way.

The awkward part is that the same trait that wins the share also caps what the company can earn from it. AVL is a reseller of other people's brands, and it stays cheaper than small local dealers by buying at scale — year-end deals with manufacturers, stocking air conditioners ahead of energy-norm price increases — which is why the screen calls Low Price Plus a likely fit. But that is a volume advantage, not a price advantage: gross margin sits around 15.6%, average selling prices fell 1.3–3% across categories in the first half of FY25, and in the fourth quarter of FY26 mobile and laptop price increases squeezed gross margin by about 100 basis points instead of lifting it. So Pricing Power is a clear no-fit, and the risk review's 'good enough goods' concern is the mirror image of the Low Price Plus pattern — the same buying scale that beats the small dealer is what leaves the company competing on price and availability rather than on anything a customer would pay extra for. The pre-bought inventory that funds that price edge is why the balance sheet carries ₹840 crores of stock by design, and why the business leans entirely on manufacturer relationships it does not control: no private label, centralised manufacturer billing, and manufacturers helping clear unsold stock. Innovation Dominance, Toll Roads and Friendly Middleman are all no-fits for the same underlying reason, and the quality framework scores Competitive Advantage at zero, which is what pulls the multibagger fit down to 33 out of 100 even as business quality scores 68.

The third thread is that the moat and the main danger are the same fact: geography. Being deep in under-penetrated Tier 2–4 towns — 118 stores across 38 Bihar districts, no organised competition, prime high-street locations that brand stores and online sellers have not taken — is what makes the position hard to replicate and gives the Bihar business its mini-monopoly character. It is also management's entire answer when analysts press on online players, quick commerce and manufacturer-owned brand stores: insulation by location, plus a claim about regional shopping habit ('a family affair'), rather than a channel of its own. Management has repeatedly declined to build online capability and remains non-committal on artificial intelligence, which is why New Entrants and Shifting Consumer Preferences both sit on watch. On top of that, demand here is driven by weather, weddings and festivals, and FY26 showed how sharply that bites: the weakest summer since inception, a first quarter described as a total washout, same-store sales growth falling from 17% in the first half of FY25 to 5% for the first nine months of FY26, and cooling categories dragging gross margin down around 100 basis points, with delays in GST 2.0 implementation slowing the recovery further. Every one of the eight risk channels is on watch except fashion risk, and resilience is judged strained. Taken together the records describe a well-run, founder-led, disciplined regional retailer with a genuine and continuing share story, but thin economics per sale and a defence that rests on where it is rather than on what it uniquely offers — which is how it lands at 56 out of 100, or decent.

*"Winning customers from rivals"*

**What the calls show:** Explicit, rising share numbers across the calls — Bihar >50% (FY23) rising to ~60% (FY25), Jharkhand ~15% within two years — alongside store count doubling from 105 to 207 and management claiming share gain through the FY25 consumer slowdown and the washed-out FY26 summer, with expansion replicated into culturally similar Hindi-heartland states (UP, Chhattisgarh, MP next).

> "Bihar, we have already, as per manufacturers, we have already captured around 60% of markets. And in Jharkhand, in only 2 years of operation, we have captured nearly 15%." — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Sales compounding at 28% while margins held or improved (1% → 9%) — growth that was WON, not bought with price cuts.

### Low Price Plus — LIKELY FIT
*"Cheapest — and still the best"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits and the only computable check supports the pattern.

**What the calls show:** The founding premise is quality goods at competitive prices, and the FY26 calls show scale buying (year-end OEM deals, opportunistic AC stocking ahead of BEE norm price rises) deliberately used to stay cheaper than smaller dealers, with thin ~15-16% gross margins but 28% ROCE / 60% ROE (FY23). What is missing is an itemised cost-obsession culture — opex and other expenses grew ~50-60% YoY in FY26 and margins compressed.

> "for smaller dealers, they may be having those inventories of earlier pricing. So we want to remain competitive all the time" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Keeps ₹16 of every ₹100 of sales after input costs and turns its assets 1.8× a year with 22% returns on capital — thin margins made up on volume, the low-price-winner profile.

### Brand Strength — PARTIAL
*"The name itself sells"*

## What to watch

- Same-store sales growth: the Cyclicality risk is live after the fall from 17% (H1 FY25) to 5% (9M FY26) — a normal summer that does not restore growth would mean the weakness is demand, not weather.
- Gross margin around 15.6%: the Low Price Plus pattern only works if scale buying holds the price gap; further erosion from product price increases, as in Q4 FY26, would confirm the Good Enough Goods risk.
- Share gains outside Bihar: the Market Share Gainers pattern rests on new states repeating the Bihar outcome — Jharkhand beyond ~15%, and progress past 24 of 75 UP districts, are the tests.
- Any organised competitor — online, quick commerce or a manufacturer-owned brand store — actually opening in these towns would remove the geographic insulation that is management's only stated answer to the New Entrants and Shifting Consumer Preferences risks.
- Manufacturer terms: with no private label, centralised manufacturer billing and year-end deals driving margin, any tightening of those relationships hits the Stakeholder Concentration risk and the inventory-heavy model behind the 1.82 asset turn at once.
