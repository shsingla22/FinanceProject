# Motherson Wiring (MSUMI) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Motherson Sumi Wiring India Limited (MSWIL, ticker MSUMI) makes automotive wiring harnesses — the bundles of electrical cables, connectors and terminals that carry power and signals through a vehicle — and sells them to vehicle manufacturers (OEMs) in India, with Maruti Suzuki and Tata named as examples of customers whose expansion drives its own. It was demerged from Samvardhana Motherson International and listed in 2022, and is backed by two partners, Sumitomo Wiring Systems and Motherson. It earns money by supplying harnesses fitted onto customers' production lines: copper is the main raw material and copper and currency movements are contractually passed through to customers with a three-to-six-month lag, so profits wobble in the short term but the cost is recovered. Revenue is essentially all India-based across a growing number of plants (Chennai, Kharkhoda, Pune, Navagam/Mandal in Gujarat), rising from Rs 8,330 crores in FY24 to over Rs 10,000 crores in FY26, run debt-free with ROCE of roughly 48% versus a group target of over 40%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Wiring harnesses for passenger vehicles** — The core business — harnesses for cars and SUVs, where management says growth comes as much from 'content per car' as from volumes, because more features and premiumisation/SUV mix put more wiring into each vehicle.
- **Wiring harnesses for commercial vehicles, two-wheelers, off-highway and agricultural equipment** — The same product supplied to buses, trucks, two-wheelers and farm/off-road equipment; management cites this breadth as a key strength but gives no separate revenue split.
- **Low-voltage harnesses (conventional/ICE)** — The traditional, higher-volume part of the product — roughly 75-80% of the wiring in a passenger car stays low voltage regardless of powertrain.
- **High-voltage harnesses (EV/hybrid)** — A newer, more specialised line started with a dedicated Chennai facility in 2022, needing special machines, testing and safety handling because currents run at 120-180 amps versus 12 volts in a normal car; management says the high-voltage portion alone can cost as much as all the low-voltage wiring in an EV, and orders are now being won across ICE, EV and hybrid platforms.
- **Greenfield plants (Kharkhoda, Pune, Navagam/Mandal)** — Three new factories reported separately from the established base: by Q4 FY26 they were doing about Rs 443 crores a quarter (targeting roughly Rs 2,000 crores annualised) at 50-80% utilisation, still loss-making at the EBITDA level as some customer volumes — especially EV programmes — ramp up slower than planned.
- **Established ('ex-greenfield') plants** — The mature factory base that carries the profits, running at about 80% capacity utilisation, with management adding capacity only once that level is hit and only against firm customer orders; capex is modest (about Rs 190-220 crores a year) and mostly land and buildings.

### The story in depth

Motherson Wiring earns a rating of Decent — 64 out of 100, three stars — built from a strong business-quality score of 71, a much weaker multibagger-pattern fit of 41, and a comparatively safe risk score of 78, with overall resilience judged sound. The quality work is genuinely good where it counts on returns: every rupee of assets produces ₹2.42 of sales, and every ₹100 of capital in the business generated ₹32 of operating cash this year, with return on capital rising from 44% to 48%. The company has been debt-free for four years, has grown entirely by building its own new plants out of its own cash flow with no acquisitions at all, and only commits to expansion against firm customer orders. The weak spots in the quality framework are not the returns but the breadth of the moat and the way capital gets deployed — capital allocation and competitive advantage both score lowest of the seven areas, while return on capital scores highest.

The same handful of traits keep showing up on both sides of the ledger, which is the heart of this story. Being asset-light and assembly-led is what produces the very high returns and lets ₹10,000 crore of revenue run on roughly ₹200 crore of annual capital spending — but management itself concedes that 'wiring harness is more assembly', and that concession is exactly why the cost-to-replicate pattern only partially fits and why the 'good enough goods' risk sits on watch, with an analyst flagging gross margins 500-600 basis points above peers and customers pushing annual cost reductions and recoveries. Building plants right next to customer assembly lines — 26 today, heading past 29 — is scored as a real convenience and distribution advantage, and it is also the direct cause of the elevated stakeholder concentration risk: those greenfield plants at Kharkhoda, Navagam and Pune are effectively built for named customers, Maruti is conceded to be the largest customer, the mix is not disclosed, and one customer's volume shortfall already left the Pune plant running at about half utilisation. The contractual pass-through of copper and yen costs is what makes customers accept price changes without pushback and supports the partial toll-road read, yet the same mechanism is precisely why the pricing-power pattern is judged no fit at all: prices move with costs, not above them, and the three-to-six month lag knocked gross margin down about 600 basis points from the first to the fourth quarter of FY26 before mechanical recovery brought it back.

What lifts the case is growth and the people running it. Revenue grew about 33% in FY26 against single-digit market growth, the third quarter grew 25% against a mid-teens industry, and FY24 outgrew the industry by 11% — profitable share gain sustained across the whole period, which is why market-share gainer is a likely fit. The end market is expanding on two separate axes: Indian carmakers building out capacity, and more electrical content going into every vehicle regardless of powertrain. Corporate culture is the one outright strong fit — the same three leaders on every call for four years, a return-on-capital hurdle above 40% actually delivered, capital spending guided and broadly met, and bad news disclosed. Notably, the shift toward electric vehicles cuts both ways here too: it is scored as a low risk on the consumer-preference side because more features and new powertrains add wiring content rather than remove it, but it stays on watch as a technology risk because carmakers are described as trying to innovate where they already do assembly work. What is simply absent is the rest of the multibagger toolkit — no consumer brand, no recurring service or subscription revenue, no dealer or installer channel, no direct-to-customer business, and no disclosed research spending — which is what pulls the pattern score down to 41 and caps the overall rating at Decent rather than something better.
