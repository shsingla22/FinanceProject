# Oswal Pumps (OSWALPUMPS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Oswal Pumps, set up in 2003, is an Indian manufacturer that builds and installs complete solar-powered water pumping systems for farmers' irrigation, along with regular grid-connected submersible and monoblock pumps and the electric motors that run them. Almost all of its money comes from turnkey orders won through government tenders — mainly the central PM-KUSUM subsidy scheme and state versions of it such as Maharashtra's Magel Tyala — where the farmer pays about 10% and the state and central governments pay the rest, so its customers and receivables are effectively government bodies. It is unusually backward-integrated: it makes the pumps, motors, castings, windings and cables itself, the solar panels through a wholly owned subsidiary, and the mounting structures and balance-of-system kits through an associate company, which management credits for its high margins. FY26 operating income was INR2,064 crore with PAT of INR376 crore; it has installed over 1,06,000 solar pumping systems across 15 states and is now branching into rooftop, utility and commercial & industrial solar to depend less on a single scheme.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Turnkey solar pumping systems under PM-KUSUM (direct and indirect)** — Supplying and installing complete solar irrigation pump sets for farmers under the central government's PM-KUSUM programme — the company's main business, with about 48,900 systems supplied directly as of June 2025, a 31% live-to-date market share including supplies to EPC players, and each order to be executed within roughly 120-150 days.
- **State-level schemes, chiefly Magel Tyala (Maharashtra)** — The same solar pump systems sold through state-run tenders that run ahead of PM-KUSUM 2.0 — Maharashtra's Magel Tyala targets 5 lakh pumps and is described as the largest and most aggressive state programme, though it is also the source of the delayed receivables that pushed receivable days to around 155.
- **Pumps and motors manufacturing (in-house, backward integrated)** — The core factory operation making solar and grid-connected submersible and monoblock pumps plus motors, covering casting, injection moulding, aluminium die-cast, winding wire, cable and stamping in-house; IPO money is funding a capacity expansion and automation programme due to finish by Q3 FY27.
- **Solar modules — Oswal Solar Structure Private Limited (wholly owned subsidiary)** — Makes the solar panels that go into each pumping system, with capacity being expanded in phases — 1 GW by Q1 FY27 and a further 0.5 GW by Q3 FY27.
- **Structures and BOS kits — Walso Solar Solutions Private Limited (38.5% associate)** — Supplies the mounting structures and balance-of-system kits that complete the pump installation, filling out the last piece of the fully integrated supply chain.
- **Rooftop solar — Oswal Solar Energy Private Limited and the HAM SPV** — A new line entered in FY26 with a first order under the government's PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana, plus a 60%-owned special purpose vehicle (Oswal Doon Baran Bundi Solar Projects, with Doon Infrapower) to put rooftop solar on Rajasthan state government buildings under the Hybrid Annuity Model.
- **Utility and commercial & industrial (C&I) solar projects** — A deliberate diversification away from a single government scheme; together with rooftop solar it carries a combined pipeline of roughly 300 MW and is expected to start contributing meaningfully from Q3 FY27.
- **Exports** — Mentioned only in passing as one component of the roughly 24,500-pump order book at January 2026, so it appears small relative to the domestic government business.

### The story in depth

Oswal Pumps is a low-cost manufacturer that has built a real, if narrow, set of strengths. It makes almost everything in-house, from castings and winding wire through to solar modules, structures and balance-of-system parts, and the quality work credits this backward integration and a repeated "frugal operating mindset" with letting the company survive tender price cuts better than rivals. Around that sit a 55-year-old farm-equipment name, a roughly 500-person service network and a genuine execution record: cumulative installations went from 48,900 in June 2025 to 1,06,122 by May 2026 across 15 states, on a stated 31% share of the PM-KUSUM scheme, and the multi-state expansion check credits this as credible and executed. The balance sheet is conservative (net debt to equity of 0.08 times), overall resilience is judged sound, and the money raised at listing is going into clearly growth-oriented capacity: pumps from 2 lakh to over 5 lakh units and solar modules from 600 MW to 2.1 GW, with about Rs 350 crore more planned in FY27. This is why three of the multibagger patterns — low cost done well, hard-to-copy assets, and market-share gains — all come back as partial rather than absent.

The problem is that the very thing that powers those patterns is also the thing the risk review flags hardest. Being the cheapest bidder is the whole defence, and it is a defence in an industry with almost no walls: the industry-structure score is the weakest area of the entire framework, the business is explicitly judged not an oligopoly, and management itself says barriers are low and pricing is irrational as new bidders keep arriving. So the same cost culture that makes the low-price pattern partly work is what forces four patterns to fail outright — pricing power, toll-road economics, the friendly-middleman effect and recurring revenue — and it is what makes new entrants a high risk. The numbers show it plainly: tender realisations fell 13–14% over two tenders, operating margin slid from 27.4% in the first quarter of FY26 to 25.4% and then 23.2%, with FY27 guided down to 22–23% from an earlier 27–29%. The brand tells the same double story. It is old and widely known, which supports the partial brand verdict, but roughly 90% of revenue comes through lowest-price government tenders where a state agency, not the farmer, picks the supplier, so the heritage buys no price premium — hence the separate warning that the product risks being treated as merely good enough. Growth, likewise, is entirely volumes and scheme awards with price working against the company, which is why the pricing-mix-and-volume check is one of the worst in the set.

Cash and concentration complete the picture, and they are the reason the combined rating lands at 24 out of 100, or weak — 43 for business quality, 10 for multibagger fit and 8 for risk safety. About 218 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, receivables run about 155 days, and for every Rs 100 of capital in the business the company produced negative Rs 4 of operating cash this year. That is the financial expression of the same dependency the risk review names three separate ways: the customer is the government, the receivable stress comes from one state counterparty (Maharashtra's MSEDCL), and local-content rules force cells from a two-supplier base. Because the model is one-off turnkey supply rather than anything repeating, the company funds its customer instead of the other way round — exactly why the recurring-revenue pattern does not fit at all. The cycle now turns down: PM-KUSUM 1.0 ends on 31 March 2026, the company has guided to an outright revenue decline in the first half of FY27, and growth guidance has already been cut from 50–60% to 20–25%, which fits a sales record that has averaged roughly 55% growth a year but very unevenly. There is an identifiable upturn ahead in PM-KUSUM 2.0, said to come at a larger size, and the stated fixes — rooftop and commercial solar with a pipeline of about 300 MW, a 60%-owned vehicle to build and own rooftop assets for Rajasthan state buildings, and exports at roughly 3–4% of sales — point the right way. But the timing of the next scheme has already slipped, the diversification is still mostly plans, and the delivery record against past promises is weak in the one case tracked, where a new helical pump missed its stated date.

> "the first two quarters likely to witness a moderate but temporary revenue decline due to the timing of project award and execution schedules" — *management, on an earnings call*
