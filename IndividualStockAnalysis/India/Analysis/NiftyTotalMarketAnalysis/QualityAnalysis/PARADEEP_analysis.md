# Paradeep Phosph. (PARADEEP) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Paradeep Phosphates (PPL) is an Indian fertilizer maker that manufactures and sells crop nutrients — mainly phosphate-based products like DAP, a wide range of NPK grades, Triple Super Phosphate (TSP), plus some urea and newer nano fertilizers — to farmers across India. It buys imported raw materials (ammonia, sulphur, rock/phosphoric acid, much of it shipped from the Middle East), converts them into finished fertilizer at its plants at Paradeep (Odisha), Goa (acquired June 2022) and Mangalore/MCFL, and sells through a network of about 6,800 dealers and over one lakh retailers reaching 15 million farmers in 18 states. It earns money on the spread between raw material cost and the sale price, where a large slice of the realisation comes from the government as subsidy under the Nutrient Based Subsidy (NBS) scheme rather than from the farmer, so subsidy rates, receivable timing and imported input prices drive profitability; margins are effectively capped by the government at roughly 10–12% PBT for manufacturing and 8% for trading. By FY2026 it did about Rs.21,826 crores of revenue, Rs.2,259 crores of EBITDA and Rs.1,000 crores of profit, selling 42.1 lakh tonnes at close to 100% capacity utilisation, and it is spending heavily (a capex programme of roughly Rs.3,600 crores) to make its own phosphoric and sulphuric acid so it depends less on imports.

**The parts of the business:**

- **NPK complex fertilizers (including flagship N20)** — Multi-nutrient granulated fertilizers made in over 10 different grades that management can switch between as market demand shifts; this is now the core and fastest-growing part of the business — NPK including TSP grew 22% to 24.64 lakh tonnes in FY26, and management is deliberately pushing volume from lower-margin products into these higher-margin grades.
- **DAP (di-ammonium phosphate)** — The traditional flagship phosphate fertilizer, still a major seller but shrinking in relative importance — management said it had fallen to about 25% of the product basket by late 2024 — and its economics swing with special government DAP subsidy top-ups and the fixed Rs.1,350-per-bag retail price.
- **Triple Super Phosphate (TSP)** — A newer, more value-added variant of DAP that the company launched and is marketing hard, selling 63,000 tonnes in H1 FY25 and contracting up to 1,50,000 tonnes for that season; small today but treated as a strategic growth category.
- **Urea** — Nitrogen fertilizer produced at the acquired Goa unit (0.4 million tonnes of the unit's 1.2 million tonne capacity) and at Mangalore/MCFL, where capacity was expanded by about 30,000 tonnes a year in Q3 FY26; a secondary product line alongside the phosphate core.
- **Nano fertilizers (nano DAP and nano urea)** — New liquid, bottle-packed 'climate and soil friendly' products sold alongside the bagged range — over 600,000 bottles in H1 FY25 — still tiny in revenue terms but presented as part of the innovation and soil-health story.
- **Phosphoric acid and sulphuric acid (captive intermediates)** — In-house acid plants that feed the fertilizer granulation lines rather than being sold; this 'backward integration' is the company's main margin lever — sulphuric acid capacity was raised 45% (0.6 million tonnes) in FY26 and phosphoric acid is being taken from 0.5 to 0.7 and eventually 1.0 million tonnes so that Paradeep, Goa and Mangalore all become 100% self-sufficient in acid.
- **Granulation capacity at Paradeep** — The physical fertilizer-making trains (four revamped lines, being debottlenecked from 1.8 to 2.0 million tonnes and with a further ~1 million tonne expansion costing about Rs.800 crores) that give the company flexibility to change product mix between DAP and NPK depending on demand.
- **Trading / bought-in product** — Buying finished fertilizer to resell, which management explicitly keeps small — 'we are a manufacturer, so trading will only be to fulfil our overall portfolio' if a demand spike appears in key markets — partly because the government allows only an 8% PBT margin on trading versus 10–12% on manufacturing.
- **Distribution and farmer advisory network** — The sales arm — 6,800 dealers, over one lakh retailers, digital outreach to 12–15 million farmers in 18 states plus an on-ground advisory network — described as a core capability that lets the company place its volumes and push farmers toward balanced, higher-value nutrient products.

### The story in depth

The clearest thing Paradeep Phosphates has built is reach. Its distribution scores the maximum in the quality framework — a coast-to-coast plant footprint feeding over one lakh retailers and 6,800 dealers across 18 states, backed by an on-ground advisory network and 15 million farmers engaged — and that same asset is what earns the only outright pattern fit in the multibagger screen, Market Share Gainers. The numbers move together: farmer NPK sales up about 35% against industry growth of 18% in FY25, the NPK category up 22% in FY26 to 24.64 lakh tons, volumes up 7%, 17% and 10% across the years, and new territory opened in Uttar Pradesh and Punjab served from Goa — all from a base where the company is still only number two or three in NPK. The spending behind it looks like growth spending rather than upkeep: phosphoric acid capacity stepping from 0.3 to 0.5 to 0.7 and on toward 1.0 million tonnes, 0.6 million tonnes of new sulphuric acid, granulation debottlenecked from 1.8 to 2.0 million tonnes, an INR 3,600 crore programme delivered largely on schedule. That delivery record is also what keeps the culture pattern alive as a partial fit, and the industry itself is quiet — one acid complex costs about INR 1,500 crore, no new entrants are discussed anywhere, and operating margins have moved only about two points either way.

The trouble is what travels through that distribution network. The products are near-commodity nutrients sold through third-party retailers to farmers with limited brand attachment, which is why the risk review flags Good Enough Goods as elevated and why the competitive advantage area of the quality framework scores a flat zero. Ten of the eleven multibagger patterns come back partial or no fit, and the reason is the same in almost every case: the government, not the company, sets the economics. Urea and DAP prices are fixed — 'Rs.1350 per bag' — subsidy rates run through the NBS mechanism, pre-tax margins are regulated at 8% on trading and 10% elsewhere, and management concedes it cannot pass sulphur and ammonia inflation through. So pricing power is only partial despite repeated MRP increases on N20 and other NPK grades in October 2024, February 2026 and May 2026; brand strength is only partial despite the 'Captain' 20:20:0:13 flagship at 14.5 lakh tons, because there is no disclosed advertising spend and the premium is set by subsidy economics; and the cost programme, real as it is, feeds a regulated price rather than the company's own pocket. Toll Roads is a clear no fit — fertiliser is one of the biggest line items in a farmer's cost base, and management itself describes the business as an import-cost pass-through. Backward integration cuts both ways here: 80 to 90% integration into phosphoric and sulphuric acid plus the OCP rock partnership is exactly what protects margins where brand cannot, and it is also the source of the supplier concentration warning, since roughly 70 to 75% of ammonia and sulphur arrives from the Middle East through the Strait of Hormuz.

The financial consequence shows up in cash. For every ₹100 of shareholder funds and borrowings in the business, this year produced negative ₹7 of operating cash — the worst score in the framework — and about 115 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Much of that is the government's side of the bargain: INR 3,780 crore of subsidy receivables outstanding at December 2025, with the ad-hoc DAP subsidy of INR 3,000 to 3,500 per ton recognised only to the extent it was actually disbursed. In effect the company funds its largest counterparty, which is precisely why the recurring-revenue pattern registers as the opposite of a fit. Growth has averaged about 26% a year but arrives in lumps, and earnings swing with imported input cycles — management refuses to discuss margin percentages at all, insisting on EBITDA per ton, because imported raw material is such a large share of turnover. Against that, several risks are genuinely quiet: no technology threat across fourteen calls, no fashion or trend exposure in what is repeat seasonal demand, and the one real shift in farmer preference — away from DAP, which fell from 31% to 25% of the basket, toward balanced NPK nutrition — is a shift the company is leading rather than absorbing. Netting it out, the combined rating lands at 45 out of 100, a Mixed two-star verdict built from 57 on business quality, only 18 on multibagger fit, and 54 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged strained. This is a company winning share in a market where winning share does not yet convert into cash returns.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Fortunes are set by policy in every call: NBS subsidy rates, an ad-hoc DAP subsidy of INR 3,000-3,500/ton recognised only to the extent government disbursed it, MRP on DAP and urea effectively fixed ('Rs.1350 per bag'), regulated PBT margins of 8% trading / 10-12% manufacturing, INR 3,780 crores of subsidy receivable at Dec-2025 driving working capital and debt, and a urea energy-norm/incentive change that cut MCFL EBITDA by ~INR 3,000/ton.
