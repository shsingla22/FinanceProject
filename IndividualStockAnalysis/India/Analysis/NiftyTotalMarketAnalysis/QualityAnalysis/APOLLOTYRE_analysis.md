# Apollo Tyres Ltd. (APOLLOTYRE) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: AUTOMOBILE*

Apollo Tyres is an India-headquartered tyre manufacturer that makes and sells rubber tyres for passenger cars, trucks and buses, farm/tractor use and two-wheelers, under its own brands Apollo and the premium European brand Vredestein. It earns money by selling tyres in three channels: to vehicle makers as original equipment (OEM fitments named on the calls include Mahindra, Maruti Suzuki, Toyota, VW, Skoda, BMW, MINI, Genesis and KIA), to the much larger 'replacement' market where existing vehicle owners buy new tyres through dealers, and via exports to overseas markets. Profit essentially comes from the gap between tyre selling prices and raw-material costs (mainly natural rubber), so management constantly talks about passing on commodity cost inflation through price increases, plus mix improvement toward premium/high-performance tyres. It operates in two reporting geographies — India Operations (about INR 52 billion of revenue in the March 2026 quarter, with plants including Chennai and an Andhra Pradesh plant) and Europe Operations (about EUR 170 million in the same quarter, with a Hungary plant being expanded and the Enschede plant being closed) — total consolidated revenue of roughly INR 73–77 billion a quarter with EBITDA margins around 14–15%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **India Operations (standalone)** — The core business: manufacturing and selling tyres across India plus exports, delivering roughly INR 51–52 billion of revenue per quarter (about two-thirds to three-quarters of the group) at ~14.5% EBITDA margin, and running at high-80s to 90% capacity utilisation.
- **Europe Operations** — The European manufacturing and selling arm, generating around EUR 170–180 million of revenue a quarter at 14–18% EBITDA margins; demand there has been muted/flattish recently, but margins are helped by a rising mix of premium ultra-high-performance (UHP) tyres, now 52% of the mix.
- **Replacement segment** — Selling tyres to existing vehicle owners through the dealer network — described as the core, most profitable channel, with double-digit volume growth in truck-bus radial (TBR) and passenger car radial (PCR) in recent quarters.
- **OEM segment** — Supplying tyres as factory fitment to vehicle manufacturers; smaller and more volatile than replacement (it was a drag in FY25 when truck OE demand fell), but management values it for brand credibility and premium positioning.
- **Exports** — Selling India-made tyres into overseas markets; a growth channel (20%+ growth in one quarter) but repeatedly disrupted by freight rates, shipping and geopolitical events.
- **Truck & bus radial (TBR)** — Tyres for commercial vehicles — one of the three main product categories, and a focus of the newly approved INR 5,800 crore capacity expansion at the Andhra Pradesh plant.
- **Passenger car radial (PCR) / consumer tyres** — Tyres for cars and SUVs, including EV-specific ranges; the other major expansion category, with capacity being added both in Andhra Pradesh and in Hungary.
- **Farm / agricultural tyres** — Tractor and farm-equipment tyres sold in India under sub-brands such as 'Virat'; a smaller but distinct category that management flags separately when demand improves.
- **Vredestein (premium brand)** — The group's premium tyre brand, used mainly in Europe but also gaining traction elsewhere; it hit its highest-ever quarterly volumes in Q3 FY26 and is the vehicle for the company's 'premiumisation' push.
- **Adjacencies / services** — Occasional ventures alongside the core tyre business — the calls cite Trumigo, a doorstep car-servicing venture meant to connect with car tyre customers, which was shut down in FY25 because financial performance fell well short of expectations.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

The most reliable thing about Apollo Tyres is the people running it. Management scores at the top of the quality framework on both discipline and candour: they cut net debt from 3.2 times earnings before interest, tax and depreciation down to 0.4 times over five years, avoided big acquisitions, held spending to small debottlenecking projects rather than new plants, and killed a failing venture quickly. They also volunteer bad news without being asked — admitting they underperformed peers, writing off the Trumigo doorstep-servicing business and the Enschede plant, and conceding they hit their own 15% return target in only two of five years. This is exactly why the only pattern in the multibagger screen to score a full fit is Corporate Culture. It is also why the returns look good today: sweating existing assets instead of building new ones is a large part of why the business earns ₹18 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital, and why sales have compounded at a steady ~8% a year at a consistent 15% operating margin.

The same discipline, though, is now turning into its opposite. Holding back growth spending for four to five years has left plants running at 90% of capacity, so the company is committing ₹5,800 crore to add roughly 20% more capacity, with FY27 spending of ₹35 billion of which about 80% is for growth. That capital intensity is genuinely double-edged: the quality framework credits it as a real barrier keeping new competitors out, and the multibagger screen counts it under Cost to Replicate — but the very same spending is what will consume the cash the deleveraging freed up. It sits alongside a cash cycle of about 93 days between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, which already ties up capital. Put together, this is why the overall picture is rated as strained: the balance sheet is in good shape today, but it is about to be asked to fund a large expansion in a business whose profits swing with commodity prices.

The weak spot runs through the patterns and the risks alike, and it is the same single trait: the company cannot set its own prices. The screen finds no fit at all for Pricing Power or Toll Roads — price increases are reactive attempts to pass on raw material costs and they chronically lag, with 6-8% of increases taken against roughly 20% cost inflation and more rounds still needed. That directly produces the one high-risk finding, Cyclicality: natural rubber moved from about ₹180 a kilo to ₹200 and then ₹250, and margins swung between 11.6%, 16.4% and 13.7%. It also explains the negative scores on competitive advantage and industry structure, the -2 on network effects (tyres go out through ordinary original-equipment, replacement and export channels with nothing self-reinforcing), and the -2 on obscurity (this is a heavily covered large company sponsoring the national cricket jersey, not an overlooked one). The response — premiumisation through Vredestein in Europe, ultra-high-performance mix rising from 48% to 52%, and approvals from BMW, MINI, Genesis and KIA — is real and is the silver lining behind the Good Enough Goods risk, but it is only partial: it cost deliberate market share in FY25, advertising spend is only about 2% of sales, research spend is never quantified, and management still says competitors and the raw material cycle set the price. Hence a score of 50 out of 100 — solid business quality and manageable risk, but a weak fit with the traits that make a stock multiply.
