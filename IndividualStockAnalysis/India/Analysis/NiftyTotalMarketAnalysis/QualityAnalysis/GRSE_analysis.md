# Garden Reach Sh. (GRSE) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

GRSE is a Kolkata-based shipyard owned by the Government of India and run under the Ministry of Defence, and its core business — roughly 98% of its order book — is building warships and other vessels to order. Its main customer is the Indian Navy (frigates, anti-submarine craft, survey and patrol vessels), alongside the Indian Coast Guard, other government bodies such as the Ministry of Earth Sciences, DRDO and the Geological Survey of India, the Government of West Bengal, and export clients including Bangladesh, Guyana and a German buyer. It makes money from long-dated construction contracts booked as work progresses, with cash coming in as stage payments are earned at milestones like keel laying, launch and delivery — so revenue tracks how fast ships are physically built. Alongside shipbuilding it runs smaller engineering lines (portable steel bridges, deck machinery, naval guns, an engines unit at Ranchi) and a ship-repair business; FY26 revenue was ₹7,002 crore with profit after tax of ₹748 crore, against an order book of ₹15,324 crore as of March 2026.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Shipbuilding (including commercial shipbuilding)** — Designs and builds warships and other vessels to order — P-17 Alpha frigates, Anti-Submarine Shallow Water Crafts, Survey Vessels Large, Next-Generation Offshore Patrol Vessels for the Navy and Coast Guard, plus research vessels for civilian ministries — and management repeatedly calls it the core business at about 98% of order book and revenue; the March 2026 book of ₹15,324 crore covers nine projects and 39 platforms.
- **Exports** — The same shipyard building for overseas buyers — a passenger-cum-cargo vessel for Guyana, patrol boats and a 1,000 m³ dredger for Bangladesh, and a 12-ship multipurpose vessel order for a German client that grew from 4 to 8 to 12 — contributing over ₹270 crore of revenue in FY26 and described as a focus area.
- **Green shipping / hybrid and electric ferries** — Builds battery-electric and hybrid passenger ferries, starting with a 150-passenger fully electric ferry for the Government of West Bengal and now a 13-ferry hybrid order (seven 100-seat, six 200-seat); small in rupee terms but management calls it a foothold in a market with 'huge potential' for inland water transport.
- **Ship repair** — Refits and repairs of vessels, mainly Coast Guard ships; started from scratch around 2022 at about 1% of revenue and had grown to roughly 5% of revenue by August 2024.
- **Engineering Division — Portable Steel (Bailey) Bridges** — Makes prefabricated steel bridges sold to the Border Roads Organisation, the National Highways Infrastructure Development Corporation and state PWDs; GRSE calls itself the Indian market leader, and turnover rose from around ₹60 crore to ₹140 crore by 2024 and crossed ₹200 crore for the first time in FY26 on 110 bridges delivered, 30 of them exported.
- **Engineering Division — Deck Machinery** — Supplies deck equipment for ships; grouped with the bridges business in a segment that carried only about ₹102 crore of order book in June 2022, so it is a minor contributor.
- **Naval guns** — Builds and delivers naval surface guns — seven delivered in FY26, four of which cleared Sea Acceptance Trials — a small but distinct product line outside shipbuilding.
- **Engines Division, Ranchi** — A separate engine-making unit that held ₹138 crore of order book in June 2022, a small share of the company total.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Garden Reach is a Government of India shipyard riding a genuine, multi-year upswing in its end market, and the quality work picks that up clearly: the demand pool is expanding on two fronts (roughly ₹1.55 lakh crore of naval and coast guard projects already approved, plus about 207 commercial platforms worth over ₹1 lakh crore aggregated by the Ministry of Shipping), and the company is taking a bigger slice of it — lowest bidder on the ₹33,000 crore Next Generation Corvette programme covering 5 of 8 ships, with a German commercial customer raising its order from 4 vessels to 8 to 12 after inspecting the yard. It is spending ahead of that demand rather than merely maintaining what it has: a Goliath crane, capacity taken from 24 to 28 and targeted at 32 concurrent platforms, three brownfield port sites in Kolkata and greenfield yards in Gujarat. The returns on that capital are real — ₹30 of operating cash for every ₹100 of shareholders' funds and borrowings, a 30% cash return. Two patterns fit outright: Corporate Culture, where the same managing director has set specific targets across sixteen calls and then reported them delivered (the corvette win he named as his own goal in 2024 became lowest bid by February 2026; the German contract promised 'by our next meeting' was signed), and Cost to Replicate, because a rival would need a defence licence from a deliberately small pool, scarce waterfront and dry-dock land, and years of build history. Market Share Gainers is a likely rather than certain fit, mostly because the share numbers are asserted more often than measured.

The catch is that the single feature which makes the growth so visible — a government buyer awarding fixed-price contracts to the lowest bidder — is also what caps what the company can keep, and it shows up on both sides of the ledger. On the pattern side it produces one partial fit (Low Price Plus: the model genuinely is built to win on price, with margins management guided at 7.5-8% of profit after tax from 2022 through 2024 and 10.7% delivered in FY26, and with capacity flexed through a public-private outsourcing model rather than owned assets) and three outright non-fits. Pricing Power fails because contracts are competitively tendered and management says plainly that margin cannot rise in step with revenue. Toll Roads fails because a warship is the largest line in the customer's capital budget, not a small input paid without argument. Friendly Middleman fails because the navy, coast guard, ministries and foreign governments buy directly, with high price sensitivity and no intermediary recommending the product. The quality checks agree from the other direction: growth is scored as volume and execution driven rather than price driven — eight warships delivered in FY26 against a handful earlier, at prices the tender sets — and the Competitive Advantage area is the one negative area in the whole framework at -0.33. The risk review names the same thing as Good Enough Goods, rated elevated, and adds the detail that the company itself subcontracts up to 75% of construction on some platforms, which raises the question of how much of the value is uniquely its own.

The concentration risks are the growth story seen from its downside, not a separate problem. Government dependency is rated high risk precisely because every step of the revenue chain — approvals, tenders, the lowest-bid award, and now the ₹69,725 crore revitalisation package with 15-30% subsidies that the expansion is timed around — sits with the state; that same package is what makes the cyclical outlook favourable in the quality checks. Buyer concentration is rated elevated for the same reason the order wins look impressive: one customer, the Indian Navy, accounted for 15 of 18 platforms in FY25 and about ₹11,361 crore of the ₹15,324 crore FY26 book, and a single ₹33,000 crore corvette contract would deepen that. Cyclicality is high risk because warships are lumpy capital purchases and revenue depends on where each hull sits in its build curve, with management itself noting revenue peaks between 40% and 65% physical progress — and the company is already four years into an upswing with revenue quadrupled, which is why the cycle check scores only mildly positive. The working capital position is the weakest single check at -2: about 205 days of cash sits between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, a long tie-up for a business expanding on several sites at once. Management is broadening deliberately — non-defence at 22.5% of the book, ship repair from about 1% of revenue in 2022 to about 5% by August 2024, Bailey bridges from ₹60 crore to ₹140 crore to over ₹200 crore, exports past ₹270 crore, an electric ferry that led to a 13-vessel follow-on — but the New Entrants risk flags that commercial shipbuilding is exactly where private Indian and foreign yards compete and the field is wider. That is the whole tension in one line, and it is why the combined score lands at 46 out of 100, a Mixed two-star verdict with resilience judged strained: high-quality growth (Growth area 0.83, Return on Capital 0.67) bought with weak protection of what that growth earns.

*"Could a newcomer copy this?"*

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.0× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ⚠️ Only about -68% of recent operating profit became cash (was 614%) — profits are getting stuck in paper, an early stress signal.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 12% to 36% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Warships are the customer's capex, not its consumables, so revenue is a function of lumpy multi-decade defence procurement cycles and of where each hull sits in its build curve — management itself explains revenue peaks between 40-65% physical progress. The company is now expanding hard at the top of the cycle (24→28→32 platforms, two brownfield and two greenfield yards) on subsidy-backed commercial demand, the classic over-investment setup, while the order book has fallen from ₹25,231 crore to ₹15,324 crore pending the NGC signature.

> "maximum revenue generation occurs when the project moves from a stage of physical progress of almost 40 to 65%" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Long-dated contracts give multi-year visibility and PAT margins have been remarkably stable (7.5-8%) rather than swinging with the cycle; ship repair and Bailey Bridges are small operations-linked flow verticals*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 2 of the last 11 years with swings of ±32 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between -17% and 11% (±7.2 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 14% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 3% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** GRSE is a Government of India undertaking under the Ministry of Defence whose entire revenue base is set by government processes — DAC 'AoN' approvals, RFPs, L1 tendering, and now a ₹69,725 crore government revitalisation package with 15-30% subsidies that is explicitly underwriting its capacity expansion; an analyst in May 2026 flagged the Standing Committee report showing two-year contract signings falling from ~₹42,000 crore to ~₹18,900 crore, and management could only answer that government 'intent' is unchanged. Every order discussed across all 16 calls is a Navy, Coast Guard, PSU/ministry or state-government platform.

> "the government has already promulgated, publicized a slew of initiatives... the total financial outlay what they have provided is about 69,725 crores" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Non-defence order book built to ~22.5% and exports (German multipurpose vessels, Guyana, Bangladesh) growing — but these are still largely government/policy-anchored*

### Stakeholder Concentration — ELEVATED
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*
