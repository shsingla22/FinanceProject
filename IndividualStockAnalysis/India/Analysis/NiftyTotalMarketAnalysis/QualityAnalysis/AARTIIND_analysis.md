# Aarti Industries (AARTIIND) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aarti Industries is an Indian speciality-chemicals maker that runs chemical plants (Vapi, Jhagadia, Dahej SEZ and a new greenfield site called Zone IV) and turns basic petrochemical feedstocks — mainly benzene and toluene, plus inputs like aniline, methanol and sulphur — into a wide range of intermediate chemicals such as nitrochlorobenzene, nitrotoluene, dichlorobenzene, MMA and hydrogenated/ethylated derivatives. It sells these in bulk to industrial customers who use them to make agrochemicals (crop protection), pharmaceuticals, polymers, dyes and pigments, paints and additives, and fuel/energy products; roughly 65% of FY26 revenue came from exports (up from about 50% in FY23). It makes money on the spread between raw-material cost and selling price on large volumes, with cost inflation largely passed through to customers, plus multi-year contracts with global chemical and agrochemical majors that give committed volumes and margins (including a $150 million supply deal running to March 2030). FY26 revenue was about Rs. 9,018 crore with EBITDA of Rs. 1,172 crore and profit after tax of Rs. 419 crore; a separate pharma business was demerged and listed as Aarti Pharmalabs in December 2022.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Energy applications (led by MMA)** — Supplies chemicals used in fuel/energy applications where it says it holds a market-leading global position — the single biggest piece at roughly 40% of revenue, with capacity being expanded from about 290 KT to 360 KT, though margins swing with crude, gasoline and naphtha cracks.
- **Agrochemicals** — Makes intermediates that go into crop-protection products; volumes have been steady and it is a focus for long-term contracts, but pricing has been squeezed by Chinese oversupply.
- **Pharmaceuticals** — Supplies intermediates into pharma end-uses; described as stable but smaller since the separate pharma manufacturing arm was demerged into Aarti Pharmalabs in 2022.
- **Polymers** — Products like PDCB/DCB and PDA/MPDA feeding polymer makers, currently in a strong growth phase on EV-related demand (PPS), though MPDA is under heavy Chinese competition; DCB capacity is being debottlenecked from 120 to 140 KTPA.
- **Dyes, pigments and paints** — An older end-market for its benzene-chain products; demand was weak in FY23 and is described in recent calls as broadly stable rather than a growth driver.
- **Benzene and Toluene value chains (core integrated chemistry)** — The backbone of the company — NCB, nitrotoluene, chlorotoluene, hydrogenation and ethylation chains where it calls itself the 'go-to integrated player', with production tracked quarterly in tonnes and about 78% of sales described as value-added products.
- **Long-term contracts / custom manufacturing** — Multi-year dedicated supply arrangements with global chemical and agrochemical companies, sometimes with capex built specifically for the customer (e.g. Rs. 200-250 crore backward-integration project on a 15-year contract), giving visible volumes and margins.
- **R&D and Advanced Materials (future growth)** — A 250+ scientist/engineer R&D team with 50+ products in the pipeline, now steering the company toward higher-value 'advanced materials' and sunrise areas like circularity, renewables and battery chemistries — a stated strategy rather than a large revenue contributor today.
- **Joint ventures (Augene/Superform with UPL, and RESL)** — A 50-50 JV with UPL for downstream amine derivatives used in agrochemicals, paints and coatings (about Rs. 150 crore invested by each side, commissioning around H1 FY27, targeting Rs. 400-500 crore of peak annual revenue), plus a separate JV with RESL on circularity initiatives.
- **Zone IV greenfield site** — A new manufacturing site with multipurpose plants, chlorotoluene and PEDA blocks being commissioned in phases using in-house technology — the main capex project, with total FY26 capex of Rs. 1,125 crore falling to a guided Rs. 700-800 crore in FY27.

### The story in depth

Aarti Industries earns a mixed overall rating of 44 out of 100, built from a business-quality score of 59, a multibagger-pattern fit of only 25, and a risk-safety score of 40 — and its financial resilience is judged strained. The genuine strengths are real and specific. The company has decades of integrated chemistry off benzene and toluene, in-house process technology, customer qualification cycles that slow switching, and global volume leadership in MMA, which together give it the highest score of any quality area (competitive advantage, 1.0). It has expanded geographically with intent — exports reached a record 65% of revenue in the third quarter of FY26, spread across the US, Europe, the Middle East and Africa, and management demonstrably rerouted volumes when Middle East shipments stopped. Sales have compounded at roughly 10% a year, right inside the steady band the framework prizes, and R&D is well staffed and sustained: 250-plus scientists, a 50-plus product pipeline, a pilot plant, and Zone IV blocks commissioned on indigenous technology. Growth has been bought rather than only earned, though: the company is a clear share gainer, but management concedes the share was taken partly with margin concessions.

The single trait that explains most of this report is that growth has been volume-led while prices have fallen. That one fact scores the worst of any individual check (pricing, mix and volume, at minus 2), and it is also what turns almost every promising pattern into a partial rather than a yes, and what pushes the risk review to its harshest verdict. On the pattern side: the toll-road test fails because two and a half years of Chinese dumping forced price deflation across the portfolio despite the switching frictions being real; pricing power is a flat no fit, because the record shows pass-through mechanics rather than price increases, portfolio-wide margin compression from FY24 through FY26, and Aarti absorbing part of US tariffs itself to hold volumes; and market share gains are only a partial credit for the same reason. On the risk side, the same Chinese overcapacity is rated the highest risk in the review — a structurally different low-cost entrant that reset industry economics, pushed the company toward smaller Tier-2 and Tier-3 customers to move volume, and left a large bulk-chemistry portion of the book (NCB, DCB, PDA, MPDA) exposed to being substituted on price alone. The same pressure shows up in the numbers as swings of plus or minus 4.4 points in profit margin (barriers to entry, minus 1) and a cash return of only about ₹7 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital employed (return on capital, minus 1). That weak return is also why the low-price-plus and innovation-dominance patterns stall: the cost culture and the research pipeline are both real, but neither has yet shown up as the high returns or high gross margins the patterns require.

The second recurring theme is that the company's biggest concentration is simultaneously its cushion and its exposure. MMA, guided to settle at 30-40% of the portfolio and currently around 40% of revenue, is where the global volume leadership sits — and it is a gasoline blending and refinery product whose demand base is tied to internal-combustion fuel consumption, even as the company cites EV adoption as the growth driver for its other chemistries. That single position feeds three separate risk verdicts: cyclicality (elevated, with feedstock prices up 60% in the fourth quarter of FY26 and EBITDA guidance repeatedly withdrawn or missed), shifting consumer preferences (watch), and stakeholder concentration (watch, with one long-term contract terminated outright and Middle East and US placement of this one product driving a quarterly volume decline). Likewise, the 65% export mix that earns a positive mark for geographic expansion is exactly what leaves pricing and volumes to be reset by policy the company does not control — US tariffs, the India-US trade deal, the India-EU FTA, China's VAT-rebate removal — which is the government-dependency risk. Against all this, the cycle check is the one clearly forward-looking positive: the company appears to sit near the bottom of a two-to-two-and-a-half-year pricing downcycle, with China's supply discipline, the trade deal and the FTA already showing early price effects, and this same catalyst list is the stated silver lining for the top-rated risk. Management's conduct supports taking that at face value — they withdrew guidance rather than defend it, disclosed the Zone IV delay and the ₹15 crore labour-code charge, cut capex, and have avoided large acquisitions in favour of small 50-50 JVs of about ₹150 crore each that move downstream. The honest reading is a capable, candid, well-integrated operator whose economics are currently set by someone else's capacity decisions, holding volumes and market position while it waits for prices to turn.

The verdict therefore rests less on execution than on whether the price cycle actually turns. If it does, the volume share taken at thin margins converts into real returns and several partial patterns could become genuine ones; if it does not, strained resilience, a 7% cash return on capital and a bulk-chemistry book with no price protection make the current rating generous rather than harsh.

*"Owns the shop, not just the product"*

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

### New Entrants — HIGH RISK
*"A new player changes the rules"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 2 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Chinese overcapacity acts as a structurally different low-cost entrant that reset industry economics for 2-2.5 years — management says pricing pressure is 'across the board', forced them onto Tier-2/Tier-3 customers to move volume, and they admit pushing utilisation 'even at the expense of a slight compromise on the margins'.

> "pressure on margins remains and it is mostly driven by Chinese competition which continues to dump products in global markets due to their domestic overcapacity situation" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: China's anti-involution supply discipline is beginning to reverse deflationary pricing in the NCB chain; integration and downstream/advanced-materials pivot*

- ⚠️ Operating margin drifted down from about 19% to 14% — steady profit erosion, the mark of rising competitive pressure.
- ✅ Growth is holding up: 8% a year earlier, 13% recently — no structural stall.

*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

## What to watch

- Whether the down-cycle actually turns — the cyclical market growth check is credited on China's anti-involution discipline, the India-US trade deal and the India-EU FTA showing early price effects; if realised prices do not follow, that positive and the silver lining on the new-entrants risk both fall away.
- Whether volume gains start converting into margin — the pricing, mix and volume check (minus 2) and the market-share-gainers pattern both hinge on share that management admits was bought with margin concessions and Tier-2/Tier-3 customers.
- The cash return on capital — currently about ₹7 of operating cash per ₹100 of capital employed (return on capital, minus 1); this is the specific gap keeping both the low-price-plus and innovation-dominance patterns at partial rather than confirmed.
- MMA's share of the book, guided to 30-40% — it drives the cyclicality, stakeholder-concentration and shifting-consumer-preferences risks at once, since its demand base is gasoline blending while the company's stated growth story is EV-linked.
- Commercial payoff from the Zone IV blocks, the R&D pipeline and the downstream JVs — the R&D check notes the payoff is still largely ahead, and the forward-integrators pattern is only directional today; visible high-margin revenue from these would be the first evidence that the pivot away from price-taking bulk chemistry is working.
