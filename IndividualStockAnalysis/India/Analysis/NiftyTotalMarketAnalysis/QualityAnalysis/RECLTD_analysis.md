# REC Ltd. (RECLTD) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

REC Limited is a government-owned Indian lender (a Maharatna public sector company, registered as an NBFC since 1998 and holding RBI's Infrastructure Finance Company status) that makes long-term loans to power projects across the whole electricity chain — coal-based and renewable generation, transmission lines and grid corridors, and state electricity distribution companies — and, since 2022, also to non-power infrastructure and logistics such as metro rail, roads, ports, airports, hospitals and a refinery/petrochemical complex. Most of its borrowers are state-sector entities (about 86-87% of the loan book, with private borrowers 13-14%), and management says lending to state governments carries no NPAs; new areas like renewables and green hydrogen bring more private-sector customers. It makes money the way a lender does: it borrows at scale in domestic and international markets (about Rs. 5.07 lakh crore of borrowings, AAA domestic ratings and sovereign-level international ratings) and lends at a higher rate — yield on loans around 10%, cost of funds around 7.1%, leaving a spread of roughly 2.9% and a net interest margin of about 3.6-3.7%. It operates only in India, headquartered in New Delhi/Gurugram with regional offices across the country; its loan book was about Rs. 5.82 lakh crore in September 2025, half-year profit Rs. 8,877 crore, and it is 52.6% owned by Power Finance Corporation.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Distribution** — Loans to state electricity distribution companies (discoms) — the biggest single part of the business at about 40% of the loan book, 43% of Q1 FY26 sanctions and 69% of half-year FY26 disbursements.
- **Conventional generation** — Loans for coal and other fossil-fuel power plants, about 27% of assets under management; management expects coal to supply base-load power for the next five to six years and is targeting roughly 20% of the Rs. 6.6 lakh crore of debt needed for India's planned 94 GW of new thermal capacity.
- **Renewable energy** — Loans for solar, wind, hybrid and storage projects plus emerging areas like green hydrogen and green ammonia — about 12% of the loan book (Rs. 63,850 crore as of June 2025, up 49% year-on-year) and the fastest-growing segment, which management wants to take to roughly 30% of assets, around Rs. 3 lakh crore, by 2030.
- **Transmission** — Loans for power transmission lines and evacuation infrastructure, including India's green energy corridor, at about 8% of assets under management, against a national transmission investment need management cites at roughly Rs. 2.5 lakh crore.
- **Infrastructure and logistics (non-power)** — Newer diversification into metro rail (e.g. Mumbai Metro under MMRDA), roads and highways, ports, airports, hospitals and a refinery complex — about 10% of the loan book, started with fully state-government-guaranteed projects, and capped by the government at a maximum 33% of sanctions in any year and 33% of the loan book, so at least 67% must stay in power.
- **Nodal agency role for Government of India schemes** — REC acts as the government's implementing arm for flagship power programmes including RDSS, Saubhagya, Deen Dayal Upadhyaya Gram Jyoti Yojana, the late payment surcharge scheme, discom ratings, NEF and the rooftop solar scheme — a role the company presents as a core strategic strength rather than a separately sized revenue line.

### The story in depth

REC is a government-owned power-sector lender whose strengths nearly all trace back to one source: the state. It is a Maharatna company with PFC holding 52.6%, and that ownership gives it the highest AAA rating from all four domestic agencies plus sovereign-parity international ratings, which in turn gives it the cheapest rupee borrowing among comparable lenders (cost of funds held near 7.05-7.17% across FY25-FY26) and access to statutory capital-gains-exemption bonds. The government also hands it exclusive jobs — sole implementing agency for PM Surya Ghar and national implementing agency for RDSS — which act as small mandated monopolies, and it runs the ratings of the state distribution companies it lends to. Sitting under all of this is the one thing scored best in the whole quality framework: an end market in durable secular expansion, with a 500 GW non-fossil target, per-capita power use at a third of the world average, and an estimated INR46 lakh crore of sector investment over four to five years. The results so far look good — loan assets compounded from INR3.85 lakh crore in FY23 to INR5.85 lakh crore, return on equity around 22%, gross bad loans down from 3.28% to 1.06%, and capital adequacy of 23.74% against a 15% requirement.

The multibagger screen picks up exactly those state-derived advantages, but keeps grading them 'partial' rather than full, and the reason is instructive. The cheap-funding advantage (Low Price Plus) is real but deliberately passed on to borrowers, not kept; the un-replicable pieces (Cost to Replicate) are the bond-issuance rights, the ownership and the nodal-agency status — that is, things granted rather than built; the toll-road-like position is anchored in policy rather than in customer lock-in. Pricing power scores as a likely fit on the numbers (yields rising from 9.82% to 10.08% and margins from 3.28% to 3.74% across the last three June quarters), yet management openly says it accepts lower spreads to win better credit and prices renewable loans down because that space is crowded — so the improvement looks more like mix and interest-rate movement than genuine ability to charge more. Two patterns simply do not apply: there is no research spend or product pipeline to speak of, and there is no recurring-revenue model, since this is a wholesale lender funded by bonds, foreign borrowings and bank loans. That is why the pattern score is the weakest of the three inputs, 19 out of 100, and why the framework separately flags a halo effect: the impressive headline outcomes come largely from state ownership and mandates rather than from demonstrated management skill.

The risk review is largely the same story read backwards. Government dependency is rated high risk for the identical reasons that create the moat — policy targets set the loan growth, government rules cap the mix (infrastructure limited to a third of the book), and the chair rotates among civil servants. Customer concentration is also high risk: 86-87% of the loan book is state sector, and single names visibly move results, from Andhra Pradesh accounts slipping between overdue categories to the INR11,400 crore Kaleshwaram irrigation exposure. Three further risks sit on watch — the loan flow depends entirely on customers' own construction programmes, with a legacy pile of stressed projects as the visible damage; public and private banks, IREDA and other lenders are crowding into precisely the segments REC is expanding into; and credit is an undifferentiated product, so it concedes margin to win good assets and loses the strongest borrowers to cheaper money altogether. Only technology risk is low, because a lender can finance whichever generation technology wins. Set against genuine positives — candid disclosure (management volunteered that headline 7% loan growth would have been about 16% without prepayments, and named the source of the INR576 crore derivative loss), no acquisitions, and steadily rising dividends with bonus issues — the combined score lands at 42 out of 100, a Mixed two-star verdict: business quality 53, pattern fit 19, risk safety 48. Notably, growth is already decelerating from 17% to a guided 11-12%, the diversification into infrastructure was launched before the in-house appraisal capability existed, and the resilience assessment is recorded as unknown.
