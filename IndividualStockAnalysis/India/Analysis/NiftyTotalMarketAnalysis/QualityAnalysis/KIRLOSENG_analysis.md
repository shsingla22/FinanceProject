# Kirloskar Oil Eng Ltd. (KIRLOSENG) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

Kirloskar Oil Engines (KOEL) is an Indian engineering and manufacturing company that designs and builds diesel and alternate-fuel engines, and the products built around them — mainly diesel generator sets for power backup (its range goes up to 1500 KVA, with newer CPCB 4+ emission-compliant and natural-gas models) and engines sold to other manufacturers who put them into tractors, construction equipment, self-loading mixers, fire-fighting pump sets, marine and defence applications. It also makes and sells water pumps and farm mechanisation equipment for households and farmers, and separately runs a lending business, Arka Fincap. Money comes from selling engines, gensets and pumps to builders, real estate and hospitality projects, data centres, industrial OEMs, dealers and retail customers, plus a steady stream of spare parts, service and annual maintenance contracts, and interest income at Arka. It is centred on India (its market share and unit volumes are quoted against the Indian diesel generator market) but is deliberately pushing overseas — the Middle East, South Africa, UAE — with international gross sales crossing INR1,000 crores in FY26 against standalone net sales of INR5,604 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Power Generation (Power Gen)** — Sells diesel and gas generator sets for power backup to real estate, services, hospitality, data centres and retail buyers; it is the single biggest business line, at INR603 crores in the December 2025 quarter (up 44%), and KOEL says it sold upwards of 50,000 units in FY26 in a roughly 179,000-unit Indian DG market.
- **Industrial** — Supplies engines to other equipment makers — construction and mining machinery, tractors, self-loading mixers, fire-fighting pumps — and to defence, nuclear, marine and railway customers; it generated INR1,444 crores of revenue in FY26, growing 22% for the year.
- **Distribution & Aftermarket** — Sells spare parts and service through channel partners and is moving from just supplying parts to managing the whole ecosystem via annual maintenance contracts; roughly INR238 crores in the December 2025 quarter and growing steadily in the low-to-mid teens.
- **International business** — Exports and locally-built operations in markets such as the Middle East, UAE (fire-fighting) and South Africa (industrial); management distinguishes exporting from genuinely 'being international' (hiring and possibly assembling locally), and wants international to reach 30% of the total, having crossed INR1,000 crores of gross sales in FY26.
- **B2C — Water Management Solutions (later renamed Fluid Dynamics / Kirloskar Fluid Dynamics)** — Makes and sells water pumps and electric motors under the KOEL and LGM brands to households, farmers and dealers — over 1 million pumps sold in FY26 — and was transferred into subsidiary LGM by slump sale in October 2025; it runs at roughly INR249 crores a quarter, far smaller than B2B.
- **B2C — Farm Mechanization** — Sells farm equipment to agricultural customers through channel partners and some exports; it is the smallest and most inconsistent line, described as growing in FY23 but in degrowth by FY25.
- **Financial Services — Arka Fincap** — A separate lending arm that earns interest income, now pivoting toward small-ticket retail loans (used vehicles, loans against property) through 137 branches; assets under management were INR7,947 crores with INR69 crores of profit in FY26, contributing about INR227 crores of revenue in the December 2025 quarter.
- **Kirloskar Advanced Systems Private Limited** — A newly incorporated subsidiary announced in May 2026 to pursue systems-integration work beyond the traditional engine business, aimed at key accounts in sectors like defence; it is a start-up effort with no revenue disclosed.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Kirloskar Oil Engines comes out of the three reviews as a genuinely good operator in a business that does not fully protect it, which is why the combined score lands at 61 out of 100, or Decent. The strongest evidence is on growth and management: the company grew 41% in FY26 against an industry that grew 18%, taking its share of the diesel generator market past 50,000 units out of roughly 179,000, and lifting its high-horsepower share from almost nothing to close to double digits. International sales have compounded for years and crossed INR 1,000 crores gross in FY26, up 37%, built through local offices and hiring rather than shipping through agents. Management set a doubling target, largely delivered it, and rolled straight into a new five-year ambition, while openly reporting the things that went wrong. Those same facts are what make Market Share Gainers and Corporate Culture the two strongest pattern matches in the multibagger screen — the patterns are not a separate finding, they are the same evidence read a second way.

The awkward part is that this operational strength has not turned into pricing power or a wide moat, and the scores say so plainly: competitive advantage and return on capital both score only about a third of the available marks, and the screen rates Pricing Power a clear NO FIT because gross margin has stayed flat at roughly 35% and the margin improvement came from a better product mix, operating leverage and passing costs through, not from charging more. Management itself describes the industry as aggressively priced, which is the same sentence that drives the Good Enough Goods risk in the lower-horsepower generator and pump lines. Emission regulation cuts both ways in exactly this manner. The successive CPCB 4+ and CEV BS-V regimes raise the cost of certification and are the reason the barriers-to-entry check scores positively and Cost to Replicate registers as a partial fit — but the same rules force full portfolio re-engineering and 18-month OEM re-approval cycles, which is the Technological Innovation risk, and a shifted CPCB 4+ deadline created a pre-buy that distorted the FY24 and FY25 comparisons, which is the Government Dependency risk. The 18-month design-in cycle likewise makes the company a sticky partner to generator and construction OEMs, supporting the Friendly Middleman fit, while making it dependent on a small set of those same OEMs, whose destocking alone was enough to make one quarter subdued.

The timing question sits on top of all of this. Cyclicality is the only risk rated ELEVATED and the overall resilience reading is STRESSED, and the quality framework separately marks the cyclical position negatively at minus one: record quarters for four years, power-shortage and infrastructure tailwinds since 2022, and the regulatory transitions already banked mean the company is well into an upswing rather than near a low point, with early softness already visible. The genuine offset is the annuity layer, and it is the same fact in the risk review and the pattern screen — distribution and aftermarket hit its highest-ever quarter at INR 238-281 crores, growing 14-20% and up 15% for FY26, shifting from one-off parts toward maintenance contracts across a roughly 15-year generator life. That is what earns Recurring Revenue a likely fit and what management is leaning on against the cycle. The supporting mechanics are sound: 18 paise of every rupee of sales is operating profit with margins moving only about three points either way, only 28 days of cash is tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and capital is going into new capacity rather than repairs, with FY26 profit up 33% on 25% higher sales. So the reasonable reading is a well-run share gainer riding a favourable cycle, where the returns depend more on execution and volume than on any structural pricing advantage.

### Friendly Middleman — LIKELY FIT
*"The expert sells it for you"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits and every one of the 2 computable checks supports the pattern.

**What the calls show:** A large part of B2B revenue is specified by genset OEMs and construction OEMs rather than end customers, with an 18-month joint approval/proto-testing cycle and service channel partners being trained up; but there is no trade-training programme or professional-prescriber economics of the classic pattern.

> "customer acquisition is a long-drawn process, involving technical alignment, product fit assessments and often engineering tweaks" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Growing operations-linked flow revenue — Distribution & Aftermarket (+15% FY26, +20% in Q4) shifting from parts to AMC/annuity contracts, plus pumps (1 million units) and spares that ride customer operations rather than capex; continued R&D and capacity investment through the cycle.*
