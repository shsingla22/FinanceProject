# NMDC Ltd. (NMDC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: METALS*

NMDC (National Mineral Development Corporation) is an Indian government-owned mining company whose core business is digging up iron ore and selling it to steel makers. It mines from its Bailadila deposits in Chhattisgarh (Kirandul, Bacheli) and from Donimalai and Kumaraswamy in Karnataka, and sells mainly to domestic steel and pellet producers — customers named on the calls include NMDC Steel's Nagarnar plant, AMNS's Vizag pellet plant, JSW Dolvi, RINL and NSL — with some scope to export through Visakhapatnam or Krishnapatnam. It makes money on the spread between a low mining cost and the ore price it sets, and management says the standalone iron ore business runs at about a 42% EBITDA margin, though roughly 40%–43% of sales revenue goes out as royalty and additional royalty to the states. In FY26 it produced about 53 million tons and reported sales revenue of about INR31,000 crores, and it is spending heavily on mines, a slurry pipeline, rail and port-side land to reach 100 million tons by 2030.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Iron ore mining and sales (the core business)** — Mining lump and fines iron ore from Bailadila in Chhattisgarh and Donimalai/Kumaraswamy in Karnataka and selling it to steel plants and pellet makers; this is essentially the whole company — about 53 million tons produced in FY26 against a 60 million ton target for the next year and a 100 million ton goal by 2030, at roughly 42% EBITDA margin.
- **NMDC-CMDC Limited (NCL) — joint venture with the Chhattisgarh government** — A 51%-owned JV that is opening new merchant iron ore mines in Bailadila — Deposit 4 opened after 50 years and starts commercial mining with about 1 million ton this year rising to a rated 7 million tons, and Deposit 13 which starts small but is planned to ramp to 20–21 million tons over 4–5 years.
- **Coal (new mineral, new geography)** — Two coal blocks in Jharkhand — Tokisud, already operational with overburden being removed and guidance of about 0.75–1 million ton of coal this year against a 2.3 million ton rated capacity, and the Rohne coking coal block, expected to open around end-Q3 with no production this year.
- **Pellets** — A pellet conversion arrangement with KIOCL that has added to the top line but little to the bottom line so far, with a shift to higher-value DRI-grade pellets planned, plus an envisaged 8 million ton pellet plant on the land taken near Gangavaram port.
- **Logistics and evacuation infrastructure** — Doubling of the KK railway line, a 135 km slurry pipeline from Kirandul/Nagarnar toward Vizag, and a planned 20–30 million ton blending yard on 1,167 acres bought from RINL next to Gangavaram port — the point being to lift how much ore it can actually move (management spoke of 70–75 million tons of capacity) and to shift from mine-head selling to selling from blending yards and stockyards.
- **Gold — "Legacy" operations** — A gold mining operation that lost money in its first year, was stabilised over the following few months, and which management expected to turn profitable; small relative to iron ore and barely quantified on the calls.
- **Rare earths and critical minerals subsidiary** — A newly opened subsidiary dedicated to rare earths and other critical materials for the country; described as early-stage with a hoped-for breakthrough, no revenue mentioned.
- **Overseas asset acquisition** — An effort to buy mining assets abroad, coordinated through a new Dubai office aimed mainly at Africa, with a couple of deals said to be at advanced stages but undisclosed; no earnings contribution yet.
- **Steel trading (NMDC Steel HR coils)** — A one-off arrangement where NMDC bought hot-rolled coil from NMDC Steel and resold it to help that company's cash flow; low-margin trading that pulled group EBITDA down from 42% to 33% in a quarter, and management says it is essentially discontinued.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

NMDC's one genuinely strong card is the ground it sits on. The 'Cost to Replicate' pattern is the only strong fit in the whole screen: statutorily granted high-grade Bailadila orebodies plus dedicated evacuation infrastructure that a funded newcomer simply cannot rebuild — Deposit 4 was the first new Bailadila mine opened in 50 years, Deposit 13 is still waiting on clearances, and the KK rail line is part of the same package. That asset shows up in the numbers: after direct input costs the company keeps about Rs 83 of every Rs 100 of sales, with 42% iron-ore EBITDA, and the quality framework scores 'Intangible benefits' positively while noting plainly that this is ore-body economics rather than anything management chose or built. It also sits in a market that is genuinely expanding — customers doubling pellet and steel capacity, and management sizing Indian coking coal imports rising from 55-60mt to 150-160mt — which earns the highest single check score in the review. Volumes have followed, from roughly 40mt in FY23 to 53mt in FY26. The risk review agrees the asset is not going away: 'Technological Innovation' is the one LOW risk, with nothing across the calls suggesting a platform shift that threatens iron ore mining, and resilience overall reads SOUND.

The trouble is that almost every strength here is the same fact viewed from a different angle, and from most angles it reads as a risk. Because the orebody is granted by the state rather than won in a market, 'Government Dependency' is a HIGH RISK: royalty plus additional royalty runs at roughly 40-47% of sales revenue, is computed off government-published prices, and sales volume itself is capped by environmental clearance. Because the product is an undifferentiated commodity, three separate patterns come back empty — 'Pricing Power' is a NO FIT (realisations track international benchmarks, and blended EBITDA fell from 42% to 33% on sluggish prices), 'Low Price Plus' is a NO FIT (management prices at par or slightly above other domestic suppliers), and 'Toll Roads' is a NO FIT because iron ore is the single largest raw-material line in a steelmaker's cost base, not a small critical ingredient — which is precisely why customers have built or bought captive mines instead, feeding the ELEVATED 'New Entrants' risk alongside Lloyds Metals' expanded Maharashtra mine. That same price-taking shows up as HIGH RISK 'Cyclicality', with results across the calls explained by ore and steel price swings. The company's own answer to the commodity problem — port-side blending yards, a Rs 3,000 crore Vizag facility, India's first branded consistent-quality ore — is why 'Brand Strength' and 'Forward Integrators' score only PARTIAL: the intent is real and repeated, but as of June 2026 it is board-sanctioned and three years out, not delivered. The quality framework says the same thing from the other side, marking 'Pricing, Mix and Volume' negative because growth is almost entirely volume-led while the promised mix upgrades remain promises.

The volume growth itself carries a string attached, and this is where the three analyses converge most tightly. 'Market Share Gainers' is only PARTIAL because part of the offtake is sustained by extending credit to RINL and NMDC Steel — the same fact that makes 'Stakeholder Concentration' a HIGH RISK (around 70% of revenue from three clients, and the entire Rs 7,800 crore receivable balance sitting in just two accounts), the same fact behind the worst check score in the framework, 'Cost of working capital' at -2 with roughly 247 days of cash stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and the same fact the 'Recurring Revenue' pattern flags when it says working capital runs the wrong way. Stewardship compounds it. Capex for growth is rising and scores positively, but execution slips against guidance repeatedly, which is exactly why 'Corporate Culture' lands at PARTIAL — management does own bad news candidly, yet targets keep moving. With Rs 8,000-9,000 crore of net cash the company gave no coherent distribution rationale and refused the buyback question outright, then redirected the money into a Rs 50,000 crore capex programme and a self-described hyper-aggressive push across 10 minerals plus overseas M&A with an explicit willingness to overpay — the three negative checks that pull Management and Capital Allocation to -0.5 each. Competitive Advantage and Return on Capital both sit at zero. Net of all this, the combined rating is 36 out of 100 — Mixed, two stars — built from a middling business-quality score, a weak multibagger fit, and the lowest mark of the three on risk safety. The asset is durable; the question is what management does with the cash it throws off.

### Forward Integrators — PARTIAL
*"Owns the shop, not just the product"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (0 of 2 checks supporting).

**What the calls show:** There is a genuine and repeated forward-integration intent — moving off mine-head sales to port-side blending yards and stockyards, pellet plants at Vizag, slurry pipelines and the Nagarnar steel plant — but as of Jun 2026 the blending yard is only board-sanctioned, the pipelines are at DPR stage, and the downstream steel exposure has so far drained cash rather than added control.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration, New Entrants, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.2× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 58% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 21% to 30% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 2 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across all four calls results are explained by iron ore/steel price swings and demand cycles — FY22 described as a one-off 'exceptional year', Q3FY23 turnover/EBITDA/PAT dips blamed on prices and a weak steel industry, and FY26 PAT growth achieved 'despite slightly sluggish prices' with blended EBITDA falling 42% to 33%; meanwhile the company is committing ~INR50,000-75,000 crores of capex to double to 100mt by 2030, the classic expansion-at-the-top pattern.

> "If you look at the previous financial year, I mean, it was an exceptional year for or for the industry steel industry and for the mining sector as a whole. So that's a one-off" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Iron ore is a consumable feed for customers' running operations rather than a capex item, so volumes are tied to steel output not to customer capex cycles; NMDC also keeps investing (record capex, net cash ~INR9,000 crores) through the down-leg.*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 3 of the last 11 years with swings of ±33 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from 29% to 63% (swinging ±11.0 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 55% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 14% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** A Government of India enterprise whose economics are set by policy at nearly every level: royalty plus additional royalty is ~40-47% of sales revenue and is computed off IBM-published prices, sales volume is capped by environmental clearance ('55.4 million tons... this is the EC capacity'), Kumaraswamy was throttled by an MPAP limit, export duty changes swing realisations, and new mines wait on Section 7/9/11 notifications, forest diversion (INR282 crores paid for land held since 1968) and ministry-sanctioned mineral policy; it was also directed to take a 10% stake in the demerged Nagarnar steel plant and to carry receivables from state-owned RINL/NSL.

> "Royalty and additional royalty taken together would be around 40%. In fact, it will be 43% approximately." — *management, on an earnings call*

### Stakeholder Concentration — HIGH RISK
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 1 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** An analyst noted in Feb 2023 that ~70% of revenue comes from three clients (RINL, SAIL, JSW), and by FY25-FY26 the entire INR7,800 crores receivable balance sat in just two accounts — RINL and NMDC Steel — with management conceding RINL takes 7-8 million tons and 'we cannot afford not to supply' it, i.e. the company keeps funding a distressed single customer because losing it would immediately hit production.

> "they consume 7 to 8 million tons of our product and are located in a very strategic place... that is one company we cannot afford not to supply" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Management claims the customer base is 'growing very hugely' as existing large customers expand capacity, and the planned Vizag blending yard/stockyard model is intended to shift sales away from a few mine-head offtakers to a broader market.*
