# Saregama India (SAREGAMA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Saregama India is an Indian music company that owns and licenses a huge library of songs — a catalogue of roughly 180,000 recordings, from decades-old hits by singers like Lata Mangeshkar and Kishore Kumar to newly acquired film and non-film music, growing by about 5,500 new releases a year. It makes most of its money by licensing this music to audio and video streaming platforms (Spotify, YouTube, JioSaavn and similar), which pay Saregama a share of their advertising and subscription revenue; it buys the music rights to films and albums up front and targets getting its money back within five years, after which the same songs keep earning for decades. Alongside music it manages a roster of artists and influencers (about 270 by early 2026) whom it makes famous through its own releases and then monetises via live shows, weddings and brand endorsements, taking a cut, and it produces video content — films, digital series and short-form videos. It operates almost entirely in India across many languages (Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Punjabi, Bhojpuri, Gujarati, Marathi, Bengali, Kannada, Haryanvi and more); FY26 revenue ran at roughly INR 260-287 crores per quarter, with the music vertical alone at INR 814 crores for the year.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Music licensing** — The core business: licensing Saregama's owned song catalogue and freshly acquired film/non-film music to streaming platforms, YouTube, radio, TV and brands — by far the biggest and most important part, and management's stated goal is to capture 25-30% of all new music releases in India while growing 21-23% a year.
- **Artist management** — A newer arm inside the music vertical that makes singers and influencers popular through Saregama's own releases and then earns a share of their fees from live events, weddings and brand endorsements — grown from ~150 artists in 2024 to ~270 with over 300 million social followers by end-2025.
- **Video (Yoodlee films, Pocket Aces / Dice, FilterCopy, TV serials)** — Produces films under the Yoodlee brand, digital series and short-format content through acquired company Pocket Aces, and TV serials for Sun TV — described as small and early-stage, valuable mainly for guaranteeing music supply and marketing muscle, and by Feb 2026 management said it is winding down the in-house movie business (after buying a minority stake in Bhansali Productions) while keeping short and medium-length video.
- **Carvaan / retail products** — A hardware line of pre-loaded music players (plus trial variants like a karaoke sound bar and a mobile phone) sold to consumers — meaningful in FY23 at ~98,000 units a quarter, but deliberately scaled down from FY25 onward and now folded into the music vertical as 'retail'.
- **Catalogue and content acquisition** — Ongoing spending to buy new music rights (about INR 235 crores in FY26 plus INR 105 crores on buying small and mid-sized existing catalogues), including strategic deals such as the Nav Haryanvi partnership and the minority stake in Bhansali Productions that locks in top Hindi film music at pre-agreed prices.

### The story in depth

Saregama scores 63 out of 100 — "Decent" — built from a business quality score of 62, a multibagger-pattern fit of 56 and a risk-safety score of 74, with overall resilience judged sound. The core of the story is one asset: a catalogue of 180,000 songs accumulated since 1946. That catalogue is the reason the "Cost to Replicate" pattern is a strong fit — no funded newcomer can go back and re-record the Lata/Kishore-era masters — and it underpins the "Toll Roads" reading, since music rights are a necessary input for every streaming platform and management has deliberately avoided leaning on any one buyer. The economics of owning it are unusually attractive: the check on intangible benefits scores a maximum 2 because the product has essentially no direct input costs, so nearly all of every ₹100 of sales is available for people, marketing and profit. Sitting behind this is a genuinely long runway — India is under 2% of the $32bn global recorded music market despite having 18% of the world's population, and paid streaming reaches under 3% of Indians versus 57% in the US. Management scores the maximum on quality (1.0 as an area): candour rates a 2 for repeatedly owning misses — the twice-failed Punjabi strategy, the 44% video decline, the Carvaan scale-down — and independence rates a 2 for a decade-long rebuild since 2015 run for a 30-40 year horizon rather than harvesting the catalogue under visible shareholder pressure.

The same behaviour that produces the best pattern scores also produces the worst check scores, and this is the central tension. Saregama is buying new music aggressively — ₹1,000cr over three years, ₹235-300cr a year — to hit a stated target of 25-30% of all new Indian music releases and to move from "no. 2 label to the no. 1 label", which is what makes "Market Share Gainers" a strong fit. But that same buying is why the industry structure area scores zero, why the barriers-to-entry check scores -2 (profit margins swing by ±13 points and producers are demanding prices management calls visibly irrational), and why "New Entrants" is a live risk: institutional capital from Bain, Apollo and KKR is bidding for the same catalogues and new music. It also explains the weakest area, return on capital at -0.33 — every ₹100 of capital produced ₹6 of operating cash this year, and management concedes that incremental returns on the new spending have so far only "just about matched" what came before. The company's defence is a stated 5-year payback rule followed by 55-75 years of returns, which is exactly the annuity logic behind the "Recurring Revenue" pattern; the honest position is that this is a promise about the future rather than something the current 6% cash return demonstrates. Growth itself scores well as an area (0.83) but the persistence check is negative — roughly 16% a year on average, arriving lumpily.

The risks mostly describe things Saregama does not control, which is the flip side of monetising through other people's platforms. "Good Enough Goods" is the one risk rated elevated: free ad-supported listening is the classic cheap substitute, management's own cited study says Indians default to free whenever it is available, and AI-generated tracks now sit next to licensed music on the same apps, with the platform deciding what gets shown. That platform dependence also drives the technology risk — free-to-paid transitions, short-format economics and platform consolidation all reset revenue lines — and the stakeholder-concentration risk, where a single partner's exit (Airtel Wynk closing in November 2024) dragged music growth for more than four quarters and pulled reported growth from about 23% to 18%, while platform-level revenue splits stay undisclosed. It is the same dependence that keeps pricing power at only a likely fit: realisation rises mainly when platforms push free users behind paywalls and raise their own prices, not because Saregama sets the price. Brand strength is likewise only a partial fit — real heritage, but no consumer premium, because the money flows through platforms. Finally, the advertising check scores well precisely because pushing Dhurandhar with Pocket Aces' digital reach sustained 6mn daily streams for five months — but reliance on individual trending hits (Tauba Tauba, Stree 2, Dhurandhar) is also the fashion risk, with management conceding hits cannot be produced at will. The offsets are real: 1,200 releases a year across nine-plus languages, 300+ managed artists, and demand tied to everyday consumption rather than customer capex, which is why cyclicality and shifting consumer preferences both rate low.
