# Biocon Ltd. (BIOCON) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: PHARMA*

Biocon is an India-based biopharmaceutical company, headquartered in Bangalore and listed on the BSE and NSE, that makes affordable versions of expensive medicines and sells them worldwide. It earns money three ways: selling biosimilars (lower-cost copies of complex biologic drugs such as insulins, ustekinumab, denosumab, bevacizumab, trastuzumab and aflibercept) to markets including the US, Europe, Canada and emerging markets; selling generic drugs, both bulk active ingredients (APIs like statins and immunosuppressants) and finished formulations (including GLP-1 peptides such as liraglutide and semaglutide); and providing contract research and manufacturing services to other drug companies through its listed subsidiary Syngene. It manufactures at its own plants in Bangalore and Visakhapatnam, which are inspected by regulators such as the US FDA, UK MHRA and Brazil's ANVISA, and sells either directly in key countries or through local partners elsewhere. Group operating revenue was around INR 4,173 crores in the quarter ending December 2025 (up 9% year-on-year), and in 2025-26 it merged its biosimilars arm, Biocon Biologics, into the parent to run as a single listed biopharma company.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Biosimilars (Biocon Biologics)** — Develops, manufactures and sells lower-cost copies of biologic drugs — insulins, and oncology, immunology, bone-health and eye-care biologics — across the US, Europe, Canada and emerging markets; it is by far the largest part of the group, at INR 2,756 crores of revenue in the January-March 2026 quarter and the main driver of group growth and margin improvement.
- **Generics** — Makes and sells bulk active pharmaceutical ingredients (statins, immunosuppressants) and finished generic pills and injectables, increasingly focused on GLP-1 peptides like liraglutide and semaglutide for diabetes and obesity; it is the second-largest segment (about INR 659 crores of revenue in the June 2024 quarter) and swings between price-erosion pressure and strong growth from new launches.
- **Research Services / CRDMO (Syngene International)** — A separately listed subsidiary that does contract research, development and manufacturing for other pharmaceutical companies; it is the smallest of the three segments and has recently been the weak spot, declining 3% year-on-year in the December 2025 quarter and growing only 2% in the March 2026 quarter.

### The story in depth

Biocon's case rests almost entirely on growth, and that growth is real and verifiable. Growth is the strongest area of the quality work (+0.67), and the two best individual checks say why: the company keeps taking share from a small base — insulin glargine from 3% to the mid-teens, Ogivri and Fulphila to roughly a quarter of the US market, Yesintek to about a fifth of the ustekinumab market — inside end markets that are expanding on their own, through a multi-year wave of biologics losing exclusivity plus diabetes and obesity drugs, sized by management at about $75bn in oncology alone. This is also the only pattern in the eleven-pattern screen rated a strong fit, and the share numbers have been quoted and then delivered call after call, which is why the culture pattern gets partial credit for promises kept and bad news disclosed promptly. Supporting evidence lines up behind it: expansion beyond the US into direct European launches, Turkey, Canada, Australia and partnered markets; research spending held steady at 7-9% of revenue through a heavily indebted period and converting into named approvals; and operating profit of ₹21 on every ₹100 of sales that has moved only about three and a half points a year.

The problem is that the very thing producing those share gains is also the source of the biggest risks. Biocon wins by being the cheaper copy of someone else's drug, so share is taken on price and formulary bids rather than on preference — which is why pricing power, the friendly-middleman pattern and the toll-road pattern are all rated no fit, and why 'good enough goods' is flagged as an elevated risk. Patients have no attachment to the brand; pharmacy benefit managers, hospital formularies and tender committees pick the molecule, management concedes continuous price erosion in both segments, and the best outcome ever described is stable pricing, never an increase. That same dependence on a handful of powerful buyers is the single high risk in the review: advanced-market biosimilar economics once ran entirely through Viatris, where losing one customer cut trastuzumab share from 10-11% to 7-8%, and US access still hinges on three large benefit managers. Buying out the Viatris commercial business and taking full global rights to Hulio is the fix for that concentration, and it is what earns partial credit for the forward-integration pattern — the same act shows up as both a risk mitigation and a strategic pattern. Two further risks sit on the same fault line: new competitors keep arriving on each molecule, including private labels owned by the benefit managers themselves, and as a follower Biocon depends on originator molecules and formats it does not control, with its legacy insulin franchise sitting in a market being reshaped by GLP-1 drugs.

Against that, the returns and the balance sheet are where the record is weakest, and again the cause is shared with the strengths. Every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.27 of sales, and every ₹100 of capital produced just ₹4 of operating cash this year — the direct cost of the asset-heavy, vertically integrated manufacturing base that is also the barrier a copycat cannot rebuild quickly, and the cost floor behind the low-price-plus-quality model. The route to owning the commercial business was a transformational, debt-funded acquisition that took years of repair and about $1bn of equity raising before margins improved; shareholders were diluted through two share sales, there is no dividend or buyback policy, and all free cash is committed to paying down debt. That leaves the scorecard split: growth and competitive advantage positive, return on capital (-0.67), management (-0.5), industry structure (-0.5) and capital allocation (-0.2) negative. Blending 47 for business quality, 41 for multibagger fit and 46 for risk safety gives 45 out of 100 — Mixed, two stars — with resilience judged strained. In plain terms: a genuinely good share-gaining business operating in a growing market, but one that has not yet earned an adequate cash return on the capital it consumed to get there, and whose pricing is set by buyers rather than by itself.

### Forward Integrators — PARTIAL
*"Owns the shop, not just the product"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (1 of 2 checks supporting).

**What the calls show:** A clear multi-year forward-integration trajectory: from Viatris-led profit share in FY23, to buying the Viatris commercial business and going self-led, to a 'direct-to-market' GLP-1 launch in the Netherlands and taking full global rights to Hulio from FKB in FY26.
