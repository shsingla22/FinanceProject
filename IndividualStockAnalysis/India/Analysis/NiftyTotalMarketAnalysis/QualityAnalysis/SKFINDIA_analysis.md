# SKF India (SKFINDIA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

SKF India makes and sells bearings and related mechanical parts that go inside things that spin — vehicles, motors, pumps, gearboxes and heavy plant machinery. It sells partly to manufacturers who build these products (original equipment makers, roughly two-thirds of sales in recent quarters) and partly through a distribution/aftermarket network that supplies replacement parts, with a small export share of about 8-10%. It earns money both by manufacturing parts in its own Indian plants and by reselling 'traded' products sourced from its Swedish parent, SKF Group; the automotive range is about 95% made locally while the industrial range was only about 35-40% localised, which is why traded-product costs and pricing swings move its margins around. It is headquartered at Chinchwad, Pune, is listed on the NSE and BSE, and in 2025-26 it split into two companies — the listed SKF India carrying the automotive business (FY26 sales of about INR 20.3 billion) and a separate SKF India (Industrial) Limited.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Automotive** — Bearings and drivetrain components for cars, two- and three-wheelers, commercial vehicles and powertrains, including wheel hub bearings and parts for electric motors; it was about 42% of sales before the split and is now the whole of the listed company, with management saying it is high-volume, long-sales-cycle, sticky OEM work at slightly lower margins globally.
- **Industrial** — Bearings and solutions for many end-industries — wind/renewables, railways, metals, heavy industry and general machinery such as motors, pumps and gearboxes — which was the larger slice at roughly 47-49% of sales, more traded (imported) and more fragmented into many smaller verticals, and which was demerged into SKF India (Industrial) Limited in 2025-26.
- **OEM (original equipment)** — Direct sales to vehicle and machinery manufacturers, the biggest channel at roughly 52-66% of sales depending on the period, and the main growth driver in FY26 with about 20% growth.
- **Distribution / aftermarket (VSN)** — Replacement parts sold through distributors to end-users servicing existing vehicles and machines, historically around a third of sales and about 20% in the post-demerger automotive company; management called distribution a standout performer in FY25 but noted it dipped in FY26.
- **Exports** — Sales outside India, a smaller and fairly steady slice at roughly 8-14% of revenue across the period covered.
- **Traded products** — Bearings bought in from the SKF group rather than made in India and resold locally — around 43% of revenue in FY22 and concentrated in industrial; the gap between sourcing cost and local selling price is a recurring swing factor in quarterly gross margins, and the company is trying to shrink it by localising more manufacturing.
- **SKF Technologies (unlisted affiliate)** — A separate unlisted Indian entity of the group running technology centres that serve both the automotive and industrial businesses; management said in Feb 2025 there was no plan to merge it into the listed company.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

SKF India earns its "Decent" 51-out-of-100 rating on the strength of what it already owns rather than on what it might become. The business needs little capital to run: every rupee of assets throws off ₹1.81 of sales, ₹12 of every ₹100 of sales is operating profit, and margins have moved only about 1.7 points a year in either direction. Against the money actually tied up — shareholders' funds plus borrowings — the company generated ₹27 of operating cash per ₹100 this year. That is why the return-on-capital and competitive-advantage areas of the quality framework score at or near full marks, while growth and management score zero. Growth is organic, capex is paid for out of the company's own cash, no acquisitions have been made, and the demerger simplified the structure rather than loading it with debt. Underneath sits a real engineering base — an in-house centre feeding hub units, sprockets, EV motor bearings and insulated high-speed bearings, with design wins booked out to 2030 — and named share gains at Bajaj and Honda, roughly half the EV two-wheeler business, and railway class E and class K approvals that open freight wagons.

The multibagger screen is where the story turns, and it scores only 10 out of 100 — not because patterns are absent, but because nine of eleven are half-present and none is complete. Crucially, the same facts that create the partial patterns also create the risks. The replacement business — vehicle aftermarket plus industrial distribution, steadily around 34-36% of sales — is what makes the recurring-revenue and middleman patterns partly fit, because it is a repeat stream sold through distributors and dealers. But that identical channel is exactly where the New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks bite: by FY26 the company was giving discounts to hold its position there, so aftermarket net sales fell even though volumes were flat. That is why pricing power is judged an outright no-fit — pass-through worked during the FY23-FY24 inflation window, but FY25 and FY26 growth came from selling more units, not from charging more, and price increases have lagged steel costs. The share-gain pattern and the new-entrant risk are likewise two readings of one fact: the wins are real and named, but competitors are adding local capacity in the same industrial segments, and management concedes it must localise as much as possible to stay competitive. The brand stretches into greases, belts and suspension parts using existing shelf space, yet the company sells through third parties rather than owning the route to the customer, so forward integration is a no-fit too, and the technology-and-premium positioning rules out any low-cost pattern.

The risk review is the mildest of the three readings — resilience is judged sound, seven of eight channels sit on "watch" rather than alarm, and fashion risk shows no signal at all — but the watches point back at the same pressure points. The most structural is dependence on the SKF parent group: roughly 40-43% of sales are bearings bought in from sister companies, industrial localisation is only about 40%, and reported margins therefore move on intra-group transfer pricing as much as on the market. The answer to that dependence is also the answer to new entrants: a stated ₹500 crore capex programme over FY26-28 with new Haridwar and Pune lines, stepped up from a historical ₹100-150 crore a year. It is self-funded and clearly aimed at the right problem, but the returns on it are unproven, which is why the capex check scores only lightly positive. The EV transition cuts both ways in the same manner — it is one of the genuinely secular end-market drivers alongside renewables, railway wagons and infrastructure, yet each electric vehicle carries fewer bearings than an engine, so volume growth and content loss run together. Policy-driven demand — railway tenders, defence, subsidy-linked wind — is a meaningful minority of sales rather than the whole, and diversification across auto, two-wheelers, tractors and general machinery cushions it. Two further weaknesses sit outside the risk channels: exports have shrunk from about 10% to 8% of sales with overseas customers explicitly deprioritised, so there is no geographic expansion story, and leadership has churned heavily with three managing directors across the period, which is what pulls the management score to zero even though the company's disclosure has been candid about localisation lags, transfer-pricing mechanics and unprofitable wind customers.
