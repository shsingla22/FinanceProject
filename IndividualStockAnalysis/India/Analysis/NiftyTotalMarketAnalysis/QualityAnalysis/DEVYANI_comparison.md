## Step 2 — Where it improved and where it regressed

### Bucket 1 — Business quality (34-check framework)

Of the 34 quality checks, 32 could be genuinely re-tested on both views: 9 improved, 1 regressed, 22 unchanged. 1 check carries the long view's verdict forward unchanged (the window could not re-test them).

**Checks that improved:**

- **Advertisement and promotion** (Capital Allocation): Weak → Good. Now: A&P is visibly converting: the celebrity-led 'Dunked Range' campaign and value/accessibility campaigns preceded KFC's 4.9% SSSG and ~15% year-on-year growth, its best in 14 quarters, though the spend is value-perception led rather than brand-equity building. | The long view had said: A&P was stepped up repeatedly (Pizza Hut 'going back to basics', Melts TV campaigns, celebrity-led Dunked Range) yet Pizza Hut SSSG stayed negative for years and brand contribution went to ~0-negative, so the spend has not durably built equity; only the FY26 KFC value campaigns showed payback.
- **Profit margin** (Return on Capital): Neutral → Excellent. Now: Out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹15 is operating profit. Margins have moved about ±1.4 points a year — very steady, a sign costs are under control. | The long view had said: Out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹15 is operating profit. Margins have moved about ±7.1 points a year — sizeable swings, suggesting key costs are outside management's control.
- **Capex for growth (not maintenance)** (Capital Allocation): Weak → Neutral. Now: Growth capex is real and rising (100-110 new KFC stores planned, 54 net adds in Q3, 20 international), but incremental returns are weak — KFC ADS is flat/declining at ~INR 89-90k with 'deleverage' cited repeatedly and Pizza Hut expansion halted with loss-making stores being shut. | The long view had said: Growth capex has been heavy and sustained (1,008 stores in Jun-2022 to 2,256 by Mar-2026 at ~INR1.25-1.3cr per store) but incremental returns have deteriorated — KFC ADS fell from INR127,000 to ~INR90,000, ~10 quarters of negative SSSG, admitted cannibalization and now closure of loss-making Pizza Hut stores.
- **Security by obscurity** (Industry Structure): Poor → Weak. Now: The opposite of under-the-radar — 18 analyst calls since 2021, a widely covered listed company, and a headline merger explicitly pitched as redefining the sector's competitive map. | The long view had said: The opposite of obscure — a listed company covered by Goldman Sachs, Jefferies, Kotak, IIFL, Axis, Emkay and JM on its calls, with a headline billion-dollar merger and a marquee promoter group.
- **Mini-monopolies** (Industry Structure): Weak → Neutral. Now: KFC holds a strong mind-share position in Indian fried chicken, but Devyani is the operator not the brand owner and Pizza Hut's negative SSSG shows no monopoly-like pull in its second brand. | The long view had said: No monopoly-like hold on the customer's mind — DIL operates franchised brands against larger, better-scaled QSR rivals and is itself subordinate to the brand owner.
- **Distribution advantage** (Competitive Advantage): Neutral → Good. Now: Physical reach and scale are the clearest durable edge — 2,256 stores going to 3,000+ post-merger with pan-India multi-format coverage and explicitly stronger bargaining power with partners and landlords. | The long view had said: Reach is substantial (2,279 stores, non-metro penetration, food courts/airports, in-house supply chain services to subsidiaries) but it is not unmatched — the nearest QSR competitor operates more than double the KFC store base.
- **Partial monopoly / broken competition / switching costs** (Industry Structure): Neutral → Good. Now: Value rests on exclusive Yum! territorial franchise rights, which the merger consolidates into national rights — a contractual regional monopoly, though end-customer switching costs are effectively nil. | The long view had said: Territorial franchise rights for KFC/Pizza Hut give a form of protected regional exclusivity (further consolidated by the Sapphire merger), but consumers face zero switching costs between QSR brands.
- **Merger and acquisition** (Capital Allocation): Weak → Neutral. Now: Sky Gate (Biryani By Kilo/Goila) was a bolt-on turned to breakeven brand EBITDA ahead of guidance, but it diluted group margins for several quarters and the Sapphire deal is a transformational merger, the opposite of small and simple. | The long view had said: Deal-making has escalated from bolt-ons to a transformative scale merger — Thailand (Jan-2024, structurally lower gross margin), loss-making Sky Gate/Biryani by Kilo, and now the Sapphire Foods merger creating a 3,000+ store, ~USD1bn platform — the opposite of small, simple bolt-ons of already-strong acquirees, though BBK did reach breakeven ahead of guidance.
- **People matter (talent development)** (Management): Neutral → Good. Now: Succession was handled from within — CFO Manish Dawar elevated to President & CEO after five years, and EVP Finance Anupam Kumar promoted to CFO — alongside deliberate capability hiring ahead of the merger. | The long view had said: Internal succession worked at the top (CFO Manish Dawar elevated to CEO, EVP Finance Anupam Kumar to CFO), but the simultaneous external hiring of CTO, CMO and COO after a self-diagnosed skills audit indicates a thin internal bench below the top.

**Checks that regressed:**

- **Customer types (retail vs corporate)** (Customer Benefits): Good → Neutral. Now: Purely retail customers, where the framework wants intangible pull, yet the company is competing on value and accessibility rather than image — a partial mismatch. | The long view had said: The benefit type is correctly matched to a retail consumer base — brand-led intangibles plus convenience and accessibility rather than TCO selling — even if the intangible pricing power is only moderate.

*Unchanged: Barriers to entry and rationality; Halo effect (caution); Share donators; Return on capital (headline); Geographical expansion; Pricing, Mix and Volume; Out of limelight; Oligopoly (vs duopoly); Independent, long-term and tenacious; Asset turn; Cost of working capital; Assurance benefits; Candor; Convenience benefits; Disciplined stewards; Structural end-market growth; Cyclical market growth; Network effects; Intangible benefits; Technology advantage; Research and development; Gain in market share.*

### Bucket 2 — Multibagger patterns (11 patterns)

The long view matches 3 of the 11 winning patterns; the one-year view matches 3. Genuinely re-tested pattern verdicts: 2 strengthened, 4 weakened, 5 unchanged.

*Foundation test: PARTIAL on the long view, PARTIAL on the one-year view.*

**That strengthened:**

- **Brand Strength**: PARTIAL → LIKELY FIT. Why now: DIL operates genuinely powerful global brands (KFC, Pizza Hut, Costa) but as a franchisee it does not own them — for KFC even marketing and innovation stay with Yum! — and brand health is mixed: KFC's best SSSG in 14 quarters against Pizza Hut's continued negative SSSG and store closures. (The long view had said: DIL operates powerful brands (KFC, Pizza Hut, Costa) but does not own them — it is a Yum! franchisee paying royalties — and the brand equity failed the durability test, with Pizza Hut in multi-year decline, zero net new units in CY2025 and CY2026 and loss-making stores being closed.)
- **Forward Integrators**: PARTIAL → STRONG FIT. Why now: The entire business model is company-owned stores plus direct delivery — 2,256 outlets globally, 100–110 new KFCs a year, own brands Vaango/BBK/Goila acquired outright, and a proprietary DIL Commerce app/web stack replacing reliance on third parties. (The long view had said: DIL controls its own store estate and is building direct digital reach (80%+ KFC kiosk penetration, in-house app/tech stack, own brands Vaango and Biryani by Kilo), but it is a franchisee rather than a manufacturer integrating forward, and third parties do not fund its growth — it funds every store itself at ~Rs.1.25-1.3 crore capex.)

**That weakened:**

- **Pricing Power**: LIKELY FIT → NO FIT. Why now: The last 12 months show the opposite of pricing power: GST benefits passed on via price cuts, value-led INR 69 launches, Pizza Hut SSSG of -4% and -3.7% with ADS falling to ~INR 30,000, and Costa gross margins down 1.2% because coffee/cocoa input costs could not be passed through. (The long view had said: In FY23 they demonstrated real pricing power — a ~9% KFC hike absorbed inflation with ADS at record highs — but from FY24 onwards the trajectory reverses into value-led promotions, deals and 10-odd quarters of negative SSSG, with KFC ADS falling from Rs.104,000 (Q1 FY25) to Rs.90,000 (Q3 FY26).)
- **Market Share Gainers**: LIKELY FIT → PARTIAL. Why now: The Sapphire merger creates 3,000+ stores and ~INR 8,000 crore revenue, a step-change in scale and bargaining power, and KFC/international are growing, but this is share bought by consolidation and acquisition rather than organic gain, and no market-share percentages versus named competitors are given. (The long view had said: Store count grew from 1,008 (Jun 2022) to 2,256 (Mar 2026) with Thailand and Sky Gate acquisitions and a proposed Sapphire merger, but the growth is store-count-led rather than share-led — SSSG was negative for ~10 quarters, cannibalisation is admitted, and management concedes the nearest QSR competitor has more than twice its KFC store count.)
- **Recurring Revenue (Product Upfront)**: PARTIAL → NO FIT. Why now: Devyani is a QSR operator; across all four FY26 calls revenue is per-transaction store sales (ADS, SSSG, brand contribution) with no license fees, subscriptions, AMC, renewal rates or deferred-revenue funding discussed — it is in fact the franchisee paying royalties to Yum!, not collecting them. (The long view had said: the calls are silent and the numbers are mixed (2 of 3 checks supporting).)
- **Friendly Middleman**: PARTIAL → NO FIT. Why now: Sales are direct-to-consumer through own stores and delivery aggregators, driven by consumer marketing, LTOs and celebrity campaigns — no professional prescriber or installer stands between the product and the payer. (The long view had said: the calls are silent and the numbers are mixed (1 of 2 checks supporting).)

*Unchanged: Innovation Dominance (LIKELY FIT); Low Price Plus (PARTIAL); Corporate Culture (PARTIAL); Cost to Replicate (PARTIAL); Toll Roads (NO FIT).*

### Bucket 3 — Risks (8 channels)

Genuinely re-tested risk verdicts: 1 eased, 2 worsened, 5 unchanged. Financial resilience: Strained on the long view, Sound on the one-year view.

**That eased:**

- **New Entrants**: ELEVATED → WATCH. Why now: Indian QSR is being reset by quick-commerce and cloud-kitchen formats, and the response is visible in the numbers: gross margin given up on 'investments to support transactions growth in the online business', a INR 69 value burger, and 'disruptive value' framing, alongside Pizza Hut store closures and negative SSSG. (The long view had said: Across FY25–FY26 calls management repeatedly frames the structural entry of aggregator/delivery platforms as the thing that reset the industry, forcing them to run online and offline as "two different businesses" with different pricing, and to invest in value/deals to pull footfall back into stores; SSSG was negative for roughly ten straight quarters at KFC and longer at Pizza Hut while the store base tripled.)

**That worsened:**

- **Stakeholder Concentration**: ELEVATED → HIGH RISK. Why now: DIL is fundamentally a Yum! franchisee — KFC and Pizza Hut are the overwhelming majority of stores and revenue, and Yum! retains control of KFC marketing and innovation, the very levers that drive same-store sales; the Sapphire merger deepens rather than dilutes this single-licensor dependence, with Yum! explicitly thanked as an enabling party. (The long view had said: Devyani is essentially a franchisee: KFC and Pizza Hut (Yum!) plus Costa Coffee licences drive the overwhelming majority of revenue, with development agreements dictating store-addition commitments; analysts questioned whether the Yum! agreement's store-opening incentives had been relaxed, and management said the KFC strategy must be presented to "the parent organization" for approval. Licensor dependence persists through the Sapphire merger, and supply is further concentrated via central negotiation/supply-chain services.)
- **Government Dependency**: LOW → WATCH. Why now: Revenue is not regulated, but GST design directly sets the company's pricing and cost economics — it cannot claim input tax credit, so rate changes flow straight through, and management repeatedly attributes demand recovery to 'government support and fiscal measures' and GST 2.0. (The long view had said: Across all seventeen calls, no revenue, pricing or licence is set by a regulator, tariff or subsidy; pricing is taken by management itself (9% KFC price hike in FY23) and demand comes from ~2,256 consumer-facing stores across 200+ cities and four countries. Government appears only as a macro demand tailwind via GST rationalization and fiscal stimulus, plus ordinary approvals (CCI/exchange) for the Sapphire merger.)

*Unchanged: Cyclicality (WATCH); Technological Innovation (WATCH); Shifting Consumer Preferences (WATCH); Fashion Risk (WATCH); Good Enough Goods (WATCH).*

**Financial resilience:** Strained on the long view (1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.) vs Sound on the one-year view (none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.)

## How this comparison was built

Both sides ran the same three analysis engines (BusinessAnalysis, MultibaggerPattern, QualityRisks) through their respective orchestrators:

- **Long view (full AnalystSkill):** statements over ~a decade, the whole call timeline — engines ran with_calls.
- **Last one year (RecentAnalystSkill):** latest fiscal year (prior year as baseline only), last 12 months of calls — engines ran 1 items carried from the full-history analysis, with_calls.

Comparison rules: only verdicts assessed on both views are counted; one-year-lens silences are listed but never scored as regressions; every movement line carries both sides' evidence so the WHY of the change is visible, not just the direction. Research tooling; not investment advice.
