# Atlanta Electricals (ATLANTAELE) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Atlanta Electricals is an Indian manufacturer of transformers — the large electrical equipment that steps voltage up or down so power can travel across the grid and be used by industry. It sells these units mainly to state and central power utilities (such as Gujarat's GETCO, Rajasthan's RVPNL and Punjab's PSTCL), to private power and renewable-energy developers (Adani Green Energy, Tata Power), and to industrial customers, with a small but newly started export business; management calls itself a "pure-play transformer manufacturer" with over three decades of history and 251 customers across 19 states and three union territories. It makes money by winning tenders and orders, building the transformers to order in its own plants, and dispatching them — revenue is recognised as orders execute, so the order book (INR 3,117 crores as of 30 June 2026) drives future sales, and margins improve as its plants run fuller and as it sells bigger, higher-voltage units. It operates five manufacturing units in India — three legacy plants in Anand (Gujarat) and Bengaluru, plus the newer Vadod plant (Unit 4) and the Atlanta Trafo plant at Ankhi (Unit 5) — with total capacity of 63,060 MVA and seven in-house NABL-accredited testing labs; FY26 revenue was INR 1,851.5 crores with PAT of INR 201.8 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Power transformers** — The core product — large grid transformers ranging from 33 kV class up to 765 kV extra-high-voltage units, which management says accounted for roughly 79% of revenue in Q1 FY27, with 220 kV class alone about 52% of FY26 revenue and over 55% of the order book.
- **Inverter duty transformers** — Transformers built for solar and other renewable plants (for example a INR 134 crore order from Adani Green Energy), grouped by management with auto transformers and other products in the roughly 21% of revenue outside power transformers; a dedicated new inverter-duty facility is being built out of internal cash.
- **Auto transformers, reactors and special/furnace/generator duty transformers** — The rest of the six-category product range — including auto transformers (a 220 kV, 150 MVA unit in the GETCO order), 400 kV bus reactors, and furnace, generator and special duty transformers for industrial users — individually small, reported together with inverter duty as the balance of revenue.
- **Transmission & distribution (end market)** — Supplying state and central transmission utilities, described as the largest end market at about 66% of Q1 FY27 revenue and underpinned by the government's INR 9.6 trillion planned transmission spend through 2032.
- **Renewable energy (end market)** — Transformers and solar pooling substations for solar and wind projects (INR 116 crores of solar pooling substation orders across Bikaner, Bijapur and Pugal), about 19% of Q1 FY27 revenue.
- **Thermal power and industrial applications (end market)** — The remaining roughly 15% of revenue, covering thermal generation and assorted industrial customers, which management says adds diversification rather than scale.
- **Atlanta Trafo Private Limited (formerly BTW-Atlanta), Unit 5 at Ankhi** — A wholly-owned subsidiary acquired in FY26 that brings extra-high-voltage design and testing capability up to 500 MVA / 765 kV; it started production only in Q3 FY26 and produced 580 MVA in FY26, so it is still small but is the platform for the highest-value orders.
- **Vadod plant (Unit 4)** — The big new 30,000 MVA plant for transformers up to 500 MVA / 400 kV that began production in July 2025, contributed about a third of Q3 FY26 revenue and 6,960 MVA of FY26 output at ~39% of nameplate capacity, and received PGCIL approval for 400 kV manufacturing in April 2026.
- **Exports** — A newly started activity — management flagged its first significant export order of INR 20 crores in Q3 FY26 as a milestone, so it is currently a very small part of the business.
- **Backward integration (tanks and radiators)** — An ongoing in-house capex programme, funded from internal cash, to make its own transformer tanks and radiators rather than buy them — aimed at cost and margin rather than being a separate revenue line.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Atlanta Electricals scores 50 out of 100 — a "Decent" three-star rating built from 58 for business quality, 33 for multibagger fit and 58 for risk safety, with overall resilience judged SOUND. The story behind that middling number is a company that is unusually good at execution and unusually short on protection. Management is the strongest area of the whole file (1.17), and the record backs it: they pre-empted the awkward question about low year-end inventory, disclosed the mineral-oil shortage that hurt output, admitted there is still no 765 kV breakthrough, refused to give guidance until the new capacity was proven, and then beat their own ~40% growth target with 48.8% revenue growth. All INR340cr of term debt was repaid ahead of schedule, ongoing capex comes out of internal accruals, and the Vadod plant — roughly INR200cr of spend — produced INR495cr of revenue in seven months on a claimed three-to-four year payback against an industry norm of five to seven. That consistency is why Corporate Culture is the single STRONG FIT among eleven patterns tested: promises made on one call were delivered on a later one, inside the same window.

The growth is real and it is share gain, not purchase. Capacity went from about 16,000 to 63,060 MVA, the order book roughly doubled from INR1,584cr to INR3,117cr with a record INR972cr single quarter, customers went from 200 to 251, and Q1 FY27 revenue was up 48% — all against an industry growing something like 6-15%, and achieved with widening margins (EBITDA 15.6% to 18.6%) and no term debt. Hence Market Share Gainers as a LIKELY FIT and a Growth area score of 0.83. But the same traits that create the growth also create the risks. The PGCIL and state-utility approvals, the seven NABL-accredited labs, the mandatory short-circuit tests and the 4,700-transformer track record are exactly what wins repeat orders like the INR298cr GETCO award — and they are also the reason Government Dependency is the one HIGH RISK in the file: about 70% of the order book is utility or PSU, the demand case rests on the government's INR9.6 trillion transmission plan to 2032, and the right to sell 400/765 kV at all is gated by PGCIL. The qualification record is simultaneously the moat and the dependency. Likewise, the capacity build that drove the share gain is happening across the industry — roughly 60,000 MVA of new domestic supply is flagged under New Entrants — and it sells into customers' annual capex budgets, which is why Cyclicality is ELEVATED and why management itself explained a soft quarter by budget timing.

What the company does not have is any of the durable pricing or lock-in structures the screen looks for, and this is where Competitive Advantage lands at -1.0. Seven of eleven patterns are outright NO FIT: prices are set by tender with commodity pass-through clauses and the last quarter's growth was explicitly volume-led with no price change, so there is no Pricing Power; the model is one-off tendered equipment with no service, spares or AMC layer and positive working capital (105 days of inventory, 88 days of receivables, an 83-day cash conversion cycle), so no Recurring Revenue; sales go direct to utilities, EPCs and IPPs with no channel partner; there is no advertising spend or premium price, only a supplier reputation; and the only integration move is backward into tanks and radiators. Toll Roads, Cost to Replicate and Innovation Dominance are all merely PARTIAL, and for a consistent reason — Atlanta itself went from groundbreaking to a 400 kV PGCIL approval in about two years, which proves the barrier is climbable, and the 765 kV capability still leans on an outside technical partner with no R&D disclosure. Network effects score -2, and so does obscurity: this is a recent IPO in the most crowded theme in Indian capital goods, with capital pouring into the same capacity. The strategy management has chosen — climbing the kV ladder into less competitive segments — is the direct answer to the Good Enough Goods risk they concede in lower kV classes, but it means the whole thesis rests on continuing to move up a technical ladder they have not finished climbing, while earning a headline 19% cash return on capital (Return on Capital area: 0.33).

The honest read is that this is a well-run, candidly managed, fast-growing supplier riding a genuinely expanding end market — grid build-out, renewables, and emerging demand from batteries, data centres, EV charging and railways — whose returns depend on staying ahead of a crowd rather than on owning anything the crowd cannot copy. The upgrade path is narrow and specific: prove 765 kV, land the targeted ~15% export share across Europe, Africa and the US, and hold margins while the new domestic capacity arrives. The downgrade path is equally specific: a slip in the government's transmission spending, or a price war that reaches the higher kV classes.
