# Alkem Laboratories Ltd. (ALKEM) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: PHARMA*

Alkem Laboratories is an Indian drug maker that sells prescription medicines — mostly generic pills and formulations sold under its own brand names — to doctors, chemists and distributors in India, and generic medicines to customers in the US and other overseas markets. It earns money by manufacturing and selling these medicines at a mark-up over raw-material (API) cost, with a large field sales force promoting brands to Indian doctors; management says it ranks among the top five companies in the Indian pharmaceutical market and is a leader in acute therapies like anti-infectives and gastrointestinal. India is roughly two-thirds of sales (FY26 total revenue about INR147 billion, India about INR98.5 billion, international about a third and growing faster at ~22-25%), with EBITDA margin around 20% in FY26. Beyond plain pharma it is building three newer legs — biosimilars, a biotech contract-manufacturing arm (Enzene, including a US plant), and a recently acquired medical-devices business (Alkem MedTech) — plus a separately carved-out wellness business.

**The parts of the business:**

- **India branded generics (domestic formulations) — acute** — Branded prescription medicines promoted to Indian doctors, where the company says it holds a leading position in anti-infectives and gastrointestinal; this is described as the company's core and the biggest single driver of the India business, though anti-infective market growth has been sluggish in some years.
- **India branded generics — chronic** — Long-term-therapy medicines including antidiabetic, dermatology, cardiac, respiratory, pain and vitamins/minerals, repeatedly called the faster-growing part of India — management says it grew 16.1% versus the market's 13.6% in Q4 FY26 and outperformed the market in six therapies, and it includes the March 2026 day-one launch of semaglutide (GLP-1), which already had about 11% unit market share.
- **Trade generics** — Unbranded generic medicines sold through the trade channel rather than promoted to doctors; management says returns on capital are decent but lower than branded generics, and that a large majority of the products are outsourced (made by third parties) rather than made in-house.
- **US business** — Generic medicines sold in the United States, requiring ANDA filings and approvals from the US FDA and subject to price erosion (a heavy drag in FY23, easing to low single digits by FY24); it swung from a decline in FY22-23 to double-digit growth in FY24 and strong growth again in FY26.
- **Other international / non-US markets** — Sales in markets outside the US, with Australia specifically called out as a good performer; combined with the US, international revenue crossed INR4,000 crore in FY24 and grew about 22-26% in FY26.
- **Biosimilars** — Copies of biologic (complex, protein-based) drugs sold in the Indian market, built from three launched products in FY22 to a portfolio of seven by FY24, and repeatedly described by management as a future growth engine rather than a large current revenue contributor.
- **Enzene (biotech / bio-CDMO)** — The biotech subsidiary that makes biologic products for other pharmaceutical companies on contract (CDMO), with deals signed with international and Indian players and a US plant built out around FY25; management calls it a growth area and expects it to reach a 25% EBITDA margin within four to five years.
- **Alkem MedTech (medical devices)** — A newly acquired, separately run medical-devices business announced in February 2026, focused on orthopaedic and cardiac implants with a global ambition; it is small today but management targets roughly INR1,000 crore of revenue and 20-25% EBITDA margin in three to five years, needing another INR200-300 crore of investment on top of what has already been spent.
- **Alkem Wellness** — A consumer/wellness business carved out as a separate entity, described in Q3 FY26 as facing headwinds and dragging on reported India sales, in contrast to the prescription business which was growing strongly.
- **R&D and manufacturing** — In-house research and a network of plants (including St. Louis in the US, Indore and a Taloja bioequivalence centre) that are regularly inspected by the US FDA; R&D runs at roughly 4-5% of revenue and is fully expensed, covering generic filings and clinical trials for the biosimilar portfolio.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Alkem earns its "Strong" 69-out-of-100 rating on the strength of one engine: selling branded generic medicines in India through doctors who prescribe and a distribution network built over decades. That single engine is what powers the two patterns the screen judged a strong fit. Because doctors choose the product and patients pay for it, Alkem sells through a friendly middleman rather than to a price-negotiating buyer, which is why the company keeps about ₹65 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs and why the quality framework scored both competitive advantage and customer benefits at the top of their range. And the same field force and stockist relationships are what let it take share year after year — rank gains in antidiabetic and derma in FY23, and by FY26 growing faster than the wider Indian market in six therapies at 1.2 to 2 times the market rate, from a top-five but not dominant position. Sales have compounded around 13% a year with unusually little variation, sitting right inside the steady band the framework prizes, and the end-markets themselves are expanding — chronic therapies, GLP-1 drugs, and an Indian medical devices market where spending is $7 per person against $50 in China.

The catch is that the very channel that creates the advantage also creates most of the risk. Because the chemist and the stockist sit between Alkem and the patient, they can also capture the margin — which is precisely the "good enough goods" threat the risk review flags: unbranded trade generics and pharmacy private labels attacking branded generics, with the chemist doing the choosing. Alkem's defence is that it owns that channel itself as the country's largest trade generic player, which is why the screen rated its cost-to-replicate barrier only a partial fit rather than a strong one — management concedes peers are entering trade generics, and the segment's own growth slowed to about 4.3% in FY26 after a carve-out. A second consequence of the model is that Alkem does not set its prices. Roughly 30% of the domestic book is under government price control with no price lever, the US business went through years of price declines, and in June 2026 the managing director said plainly that pricing is set by regulation. That is why two patterns — pricing power and toll roads — came back as clear non-fits, and why government dependency stays on watch: growth here has to come from volume, mix and new launches, not from raising prices. The distribution reach also has a cash cost — about 152 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, the framework's single worst check.

Where the story gets genuinely uneven is capital allocation, the one quality area that scores below zero. Alkem is spending well ahead of its current business — ₹600-700 crore of capital spending guided for FY25 including a US contract-manufacturing plant, plus ₹200-300 crore for medical devices — and R&D has been held at 4-5% of sales for years with visible results: a biosimilar range grown from three products to seven, and a day-one launch of semaglutide that took about 11% of units. But the ₹1,100 crore purchase of 55% of Occlutech is the largest deal in a decade, cross-border, into an unrelated devices business, expected to contribute only 10% of operating profit by FY27 on a roughly ten-year payback — the opposite of the small, simple, already-understood acquisition the framework rewards. That same spending pushes on two risks at once: the shift to GLP-1 drugs is re-basing the diabetes market Alkem is trying to grow into, to the point where management now strips GLP-1 out to show its own outperformance, while the company diversifies into devices at the same time. Reported profitability has also swung far more than the annual numbers suggest — operating margin of 7.9% in one quarter of FY23 against 22.2% in a FY26 quarter, driven largely by raw-material price cycles and US pricing — which is why cyclicality is the only risk rated elevated even though the annual margin line looks steady at around ₹20 of operating profit per ₹100 of sales. Set against that, the balance sheet has stayed conservative (net cash rising from ₹950 crore in FY23 to about ₹3,550 crore in FY24) and bad news has been disclosed promptly, and no single customer, supplier or licensor drives results — which is why the overall resilience reads as sound.

## What to watch

- Trade generics growth: the ~4.3% FY26 rate is the clearest early sign of the "new entrants" and "good enough goods" risks turning real — a second weak year would undercut the cost-to-replicate barrier that already scored only a partial fit.
- Therapy-level share gains: the "market share gainers" pattern rests entirely on growing faster than the wider market in six therapies; if that outperformance narrows once GLP-1 is stripped out, the strongest single support for the rating weakens.
- Occlutech's contribution against the 10% operating-profit-by-FY27 marker: this is the test of the M&A check that scored -1 and of the negative capital allocation area — slippage makes the ten-year payback harder to defend.
- Regulatory price action: any widening of price control beyond the ~30% of the domestic book, or the recurring trade-margin cap, would deepen the government dependency risk and confirm the no-fit verdicts on pricing power and toll roads.
- The working capital cycle and margin swing together: 152 days of cash tied up alongside quarterly margins ranging from 7.9% to 22.2% means a turn in raw-material prices would show up in cash faster than the steady annual growth record implies.
