# Alkem Laboratories Ltd. (ALKEM) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: PHARMA*

Alkem Laboratories is a Mumbai-headquartered Indian drugmaker that makes and sells prescription medicines — mostly generic pills and injections sold under its own brand names — to doctors, chemists and distributors. Its biggest business by far is India, where it is one of the top five pharma companies and is best known as a leader in anti-infectives (antibiotics) and gastro medicines, while pushing harder into long-term 'chronic' therapies like diabetes, heart, respiratory and skin. It earns money by manufacturing (and in some cases outsourcing production of) these medicines and selling volume through a large field force of medical representatives, plus a growing export business selling generic drugs in the US and other countries such as Australia. In FY26 total revenue was about INR 14,712 crore, roughly two-thirds from India (INR 9,851 crore) and the rest from international markets, with an EBITDA margin of about 20%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **India / domestic branded generics** — Sells branded generic prescription medicines in India through doctors and pharmacies; the core of the company at about INR 9,851 crore in FY26 (growing ~10%), and management repeatedly calls domestic its 'top most priority'.
- **Acute therapies (anti-infectives, gastrointestinal, pain, vitamins & minerals)** — The traditional strength — Alkem says it holds the leadership position in anti-infectives and, per IQVIA data, grows faster than the Indian pharma market in anti-infectives, vitamins/minerals, pain and GI.
- **Chronic business** — Long-term-treatment medicines (antidiabetic, cardiac, respiratory, dermatology) that management describes as the key growth driver, consistently outperforming the market and gaining ranks; in Q4 FY26 chronic grew 16.1% versus the market's 13.6%.
- **Trade generics** — Unbranded generics sold through the trade channel, largely made by outside manufacturers rather than in-house; management says it earns a decent return on capital but less than the branded generic business.
- **GLP-1 / semaglutide** — A newly launched diabetes/weight drug launched on day one of patent expiry in March 2026, already at roughly 11% unit market share and called out by the MD as doing 'extremely well'.
- **US business** — Sells generic drugs in the United States via ANDA filings and approvals from the US FDA (with plants at St. Louis, Indore and a Taloja bio-equivalence centre subject to FDA inspection); it went through years of heavy price erosion before stabilising and returning to double-digit growth.
- **Non-US international** — Sales across other geographies including Australia; together with the US, total international sales grew about 22-26% recently and crossed INR 4,000 crore for the first time in FY24.
- **Biosimilars and Enzene (bio-CDMO)** — Complex biological medicines sold in India (a portfolio of seven products by FY24) plus contract development and manufacturing for other drug companies through subsidiary Enzene, including a new US plant; management calls it a future growth engine targeted at 25% EBITDA margins in 4-5 years.
- **Alkem MedTech** — A newly acquired medical-devices arm (announced February 2026) focused on orthopaedic implants and cardiology, run as a separate company with its own CEO; management targets roughly INR 1,000 crore of revenue in 3-5 years with INR 200-300 crore of further investment.
- **Alkem Wellness** — A consumer/wellness business carved out as a separate entity, which management said in Q3 FY26 is facing headwinds while the core prescription business grows strongly.
- **R&D** — Spending on new product development and clinical trials, including for biosimilars, running at roughly 4-5% of revenue and expensed through the P&L each year.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Alkem earns a Strong rating (70 out of 100, built as 45% x 66 on business quality, 30% x 74 on multibagger fit and 25% x 74 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound), and the single thread running through all three analyses is reach rather than price. The quality framework's top marks go to distribution (pioneer status and stockist relationships in trade generics plus a top-five prescription sales force that a new rival would have to build from scratch), to accessibility as the clearest customer benefit, and to steady share capture from a non-dominant top-five position. Those same facts are what make the two strongest multibagger patterns fit: Friendly Middleman, because the doctor prescribes and the stockist stocks while the patient pays, and Market Share Gainers, the most consistently evidenced claim across the 2022-2026 record (rank gains in antidiabetic and derma, six therapies growing 1.2x to 2x the Indian pharmaceutical market by the third quarter of FY26, and roughly 11% unit share within weeks of the semaglutide launch). Sales have compounded at about 13% a year with unusually little year-to-year variation, and the end markets themselves are expanding, which is why growth and competitive advantage are the highest-scoring areas of the quality work.

The catch is that the very structure that delivers those wins is also where the risk review finds its live problems. Because the chemist and stockist choose the molecule and keep the extra margin, the branded-generic price premium is attacked from below by trade generics and pharmacy private label, which is the Good Enough Goods risk, and it is the same doorway large branded peers are using to launch their own trade-generic divisions, which is the New Entrants risk. Both showed up in the numbers: the carved-out trade-generic entity grew only about 4.3%. Alkem also has no ability to defend itself with price. Two multibagger patterns, Toll Roads and Pricing Power, are marked no fit on direct contrary evidence: roughly 30% of the India portfolio sits under government price control with no price lever, US prices have eroded through most of the period, and FY24 growth was explicitly banked on volume rather than price. That combination of price control, a hanging threat of a cap on trade margins, and US revenue riding on inspection and approval outcomes is what keeps the government-dependency risk on watch and holds the industry-structure score at zero. With no price lever, margin has to come from costs and raw-material luck, and that is exactly the elevated cyclicality finding: operating margin fell to 7.9% in one quarter of FY23 on raw-material spikes and recovered to 20.4% by FY26, with management itself attributing most of that swing to interim ingredient prices rather than to its own cost programmes, which is why the Low Price Plus pattern is only a partial fit.

The third strand is what the company is doing about all this, and it is both the most encouraging and the least proven part of the record. The technology risk is real but not ignored: GLP-1 drugs are reshaping the antidiabetic market to the point that management has to strip them out to state its own growth, and the small-molecule generic model is being flanked by biologics. Alkem's answer is a day-one semaglutide launch, a biosimilar portfolio taken from three products to seven, and heavy spending on new engines including the Enzene contract-manufacturing plant in the US and the Occlutech medical-device purchase. Research spending is disclosed, steady at 4.2-4.3% of revenue and repeatedly hits its guidance, which is why Innovation Dominance rates a likely fit even though gross margins of roughly 57-60% are not outsized. But that spending is also the reason capital allocation scores zero and return on capital scores low: capex is guided at INR 600-700 crore plus INR 1,100 crore for Occlutech, the return on the incremental money is unproven, and management has stated a payback of about ten years. On top of that, about 152 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, the framework's single worst mark. What makes the case still hold together is the track record and the balance sheet: promises made in FY23 on chronic growth, biosimilar timing and margins were delivered over four years, bad news such as the weak trade-generic growth and the long Occlutech payback was disclosed plainly, and the company has stayed in net cash throughout, moving from roughly INR 950 crore to about INR 3,550 crore. Concentration, consumer-taste and fashion risks are all low, so the story rests almost entirely on whether volume-led share gains keep outrunning a channel that can switch away from you and new money that has not yet earned its keep.

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**What the calls show:** Revenue is flow/consumption-linked (prescription pharma, no customer-capex exposure), but the calls show margins swinging hard on API commodity cycles and US price erosion — EBITDA fell to 7.9% in Q1FY23 on cephalosporin/PenG raw-material spikes, recovered to ~20% by FY26 largely on 'favorable API prices', and FY27 guidance was already being walked back on renewed API/packaging inflation.

> "the current geopolitical environment and evolving global supply chain dynamics have resulted in increased logistics costs along with some pressure on APIs and the packaging materials" — *management, on an earnings call*

## What to watch

- Trade generics: whether the carved-out entity recovers from about 4.3% growth, and whether the threatened cap on trade margins actually lands - the pivot point for both the New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks and for the Cost to Replicate barrier
- Margin durability through the next raw-material cycle: the elevated Cyclicality finding and the partial Low Price Plus fit both hinge on how much of the move from 7.9% to 20.4% operating margin survives when ingredient prices turn
- Early evidence on the new engines - Enzene's US plant, the seven-product biosimilar range and Occlutech's roughly ten-year stated payback - since the zero score on Capital Allocation and the low Return on Capital score rest entirely on returns that have not yet shown up
