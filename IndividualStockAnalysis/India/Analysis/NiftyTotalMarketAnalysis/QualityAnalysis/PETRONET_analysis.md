# Petronet LNG Ltd. (PETRONET) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

Petronet LNG Limited imports liquefied natural gas (LNG) into India, turns it back into gas at its own port terminals, and either sells that gas or charges a fee for processing gas that belongs to someone else. It buys LNG under long-term supply contracts (management cites a Qatar/RasGas contract of about 7.5 MMTPA after assigning 1 MMTPA to offtakers, plus a 2017 Exxon contract whose first 0.5 million tonne cargo arrived in April 2026) and spot cargoes, and sells the regasified LNG to Indian oil and gas companies such as GAIL, IOCL, BPCL, GSPC, Torrent, ONGC and MGL. Money comes two ways: the margin on LNG it buys and resells, which drives most of the roughly Rs 14,000 crore quarterly turnover, and regasification/service fees on third-party cargoes, running about Rs 674–879 crore a quarter, backed by long-term booked capacity with "use or pay" penalties if customers don't bring the volumes they committed to. It operates two terminals in India — Dahej in Gujarat and Kochi in Kerala — with quarterly throughput of roughly 219–234 TBTU and yearly profit after tax of about Rs 3,500–3,900 crore.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Dahej LNG Terminal (Gujarat)** — The main import-and-regasification plant and the source of almost all volumes — around 201 to 219 TBTU per quarter out of a company total of 219 to 234 TBTU, running at roughly 90-94% capacity utilisation, with capacity being expanded from 17.5 MMTPA to 22.5 MMTPA by March 2026; management says it wins volumes over rival terminals because of 35 million tonne gas evacuation capacity, among the lowest charges in the industry, and eight storage tanks.
- **Kochi LNG Terminal (Kerala)** — The much smaller second terminal, running at only about 20-29% utilisation, though FY26 was its highest ever year at 68 TBTU of throughput.
- **Own LNG sale (long-term and spot cargoes)** — Buying LNG under long-term contracts (Qatar/RasGas, Exxon) plus spot cargoes and reselling it as regasified gas — this is the bulk of reported turnover, and it also creates inventory gains or losses (Rs 95 crore gain in the March 2026 quarter) and foreign-exchange swings (a notional Rs 124 crore loss in the June 2022 quarter).
- **Regasification / third-party service income (tolling)** — Charging customers a fee to unload, store and regasify cargoes they bring themselves — around 94-95 TBTU a quarter of third-party gas in FY23 and service income of roughly Rs 674 crore (Q4 FY24) rising to Rs 865-879 crore a quarter by FY26; a new master regas framework agreement with ONGC works the same way and management hopes to convert it into a long-term capacity booking.
- **Use or Pay (UOP) charges** — Penalty income billed to customers who booked terminal capacity long-term but failed to bring the agreed volumes — Rs 379 crore for CY21, Rs 844 crore for CY22, Rs 610 crore for CY23 and Rs 49 crore for CY25, with Rs 630 crore of CY22 dues actually collected in the March 2026 quarter; these amounts can make one quarter's profit look unusually large.
- **Gopalpur terminal (under development, Odisha coast)** — A planned third terminal of about 4 MMTPA if built as a floating (FSRU) unit or roughly 5 MMTPA if land-based, described in May 2024 as needing about three years and still requiring jetty and other infrastructure, so it contributes nothing yet.
- **LNG retail dispensing stations** — A small new venture selling LNG directly as fuel at stations, with four stations contracted with IOCL and expected to start up during FY23 — a pilot-scale effort next to the terminal business.
- **Petrochemicals and biogas projects** — Diversification projects raised by analysts on the calls, mentioned as being pursued but with no volumes or earnings attached to them in these excerpts.

### The story in depth

Petronet's core is a toll booth on India's imported gas. The multibagger screen's strongest finding, Cost to Replicate, and its Toll Roads fit both rest on the same physical facts management describes: Dahej has 35 MMTPA of pipeline evacuation capacity against 17.5-22.5 MMTPA of regasification capacity, eight storage tanks that no other Indian terminal matches, and a finite prime coastal site. Customers pay a regasification fee that is only a small slice of the delivered cost of their gas, under long-term use-or-pay capacity contracts with a built-in 5% annual fee increase, which is why the Recurring Revenue and Pricing Power patterns show up at all. The financial record matches the description: 19 rupees of operating cash generated per 100 rupees of capital employed, only about 10 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and a disciplined 40-50% dividend payout that management says it can sustain even through a roughly INR 9,000 crore capital spending year. Capital Allocation is the single best-scoring quality area, at the top of the scale.

The same trait that makes the toll booth work also caps what it can charge, and that is where the strengths and the risks meet. Because regasification is an undifferentiated service, the only axis of competition is the fee itself. That produces the Low Price Plus fit -- management positions Dahej as the lowest-cost operator running at 90-94% utilization -- and simultaneously the Good Enough Goods risk, where cheaper substitutes such as Reliance's domestic gas auction (potentially displacing 3-4 MMTPA of LNG demand) and domestic high-pressure gas compete directly. It also explains why Pricing Power is only a partial fit and why the quality framework marks down intangible benefits: after direct input costs the company keeps only 15 rupees of every 100 rupees of sales, and management concedes there is no image- or taste-driven pricing power. The demand side is equally two-edged. India's gas market is in secular expansion with LNG already around 48-50% of consumption, but demand is highly price-elastic, so volumes track the global LNG price cycle almost mechanically: demand collapsed when spot prices hit $40-50/mmbtu in FY23, throughput jumped 22% when prices fell in FY24, and Dahej utilization fell to 53% in March 2026 when the Gulf crisis interrupted Qatar supply. That is why Cyclicality is flagged as elevated and why the cyclical-position check, read the other way, scores as a company sitting near a trough with an identifiable recovery ahead. Concentration compounds it: capacity is held by exactly three offtakers -- GAIL, IOCL and BPCL -- who are also the promoters, and supply leans on a single 7.5 MMTPA Qatar contract whose 2028 renewal has dominated analyst calls.

The weakest part of the story is growth and who is steering it. Sales have grown about 1% a year on average and lumpily, growth as a quality area scores zero, and persistence of growth is a negative mark. Against that, capital spending is large and rising -- the Dahej expansion from 17.5 to 22.5 MMTPA commissioned in FY26, a third jetty, Gopalpur, and a roughly INR 7,500 crore propane-to-polypropylene petrochemical plant -- yet the returns on that spending are unproven, and management itself says the incremental 5 MMTPA is expected to come from overall market growth rather than share gains, which is why Market Share Gainers is only partial. Execution has a track record of slipping: Gopalpur was under negotiation in August 2022 and was still awaiting environmental clearance in February 2026 with construction starting only in FY27, and Kochi has waited years. Governance sits underneath all of this. Five chairmen and four managing directors in five years, all government nominees, drag down both the independence check and the Corporate Culture pattern, and feed the Government Dependency risk, which is the single highest-rated risk -- the same public-sector ownership that locks in the three offtakers also makes project outcomes hostage to policy. The offsetting evidence is real: candid disclosure of the 53% utilization month, quarter-by-quarter walk-throughs of use-or-pay provisioning, a conservative balance sheet, and a broadening customer base through a new ONGC agreement, servicing Mahanagar Gas, a second ExxonMobil contract from April 2026 and cargoes from new sources. Netted out, the combined rating is 52 out of 100 -- Decent, three stars -- from 56 on business quality, 49 on multibagger fit and 48 on risk safety, with resilience judged sound. A durable, cash-generating asset with a genuine physical moat, priced against a weak growth record and a heavy dependence on forces it does not control.

## Section 2 — Does it look like a long-term winner? (11 multibagger patterns)

### Cost to Replicate — STRONG FIT
*"Could a newcomer copy this?"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** Management articulates a concrete, non-replicable barrier: Dahej's 35 MMTPA evacuation/pipeline connectivity against 17.5–22.5 MMTPA of regas capacity, eight storage tanks unmatched by any Indian terminal, and a finite prime coastal location — which is why competing terminals nearby cannot ramp volumes despite having regas capacity; a well-funded entrant cannot rebuild pipeline grid access and jetty/site rights in a few years.

> "it already has evacuation capacity of 35 million metric tons ... none of the terminals in India are having so much of storage capacity" — *management, on an earnings call*

### Recurring Revenue (Product Upfront) — LIKELY FIT
*"Sells once, earns forever"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits and every one of the 3 computable checks supports the pattern.

**What the calls show:** Revenue is a recurring regas/tolling service stream under long-term capacity agreements with use-or-pay obligations (service income ~INR 865 cr in Q3FY26, INR 879 cr in Q4FY26), but there is no front-end product sale, no attach/renewal-rate framing, and working capital is a drag rather than a funding source — use-or-pay dues from CY21–CY24 remained uncollected for years and required bank guarantees and provisioning schedules.

> "Service income has been INR 865 crores in the quarter." — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Only about 10 days of cash is tied up in the trade cycle.
- ✅ Capital spending is about 6% of sales — growth needs little capital.
- ✅ Operating cash was positive in 12 of 12 years — the reliable cash engine recurring-revenue businesses show.

### Toll Roads — LIKELY FIT
*"Small cost, can't skip it"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern, but the numbers push back (0 of 2 checks supporting) — held one step below a full confirmation.

**What the calls show:** The business is an explicit tolling model: regas charge is a small fraction of the delivered LNG cost while the terminal is indispensable to the customer's gas chain, contracted on use-or-pay with annual escalation, in a rational few-player Indian terminal oligopoly — evidenced consistently from Aug 2022 (use-or-pay protection when Gazprom failed GAIL) through Feb/May 2026 (annual escalation taken, INR 630 cr CY22 dues eventually paid).

> "we have a use or pay contract for tolling contract" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ Operating margin 12%, swings of ±3.5 points, median return on capital 26% — good but not the near-monopoly profile of a toll road.
- ❌ Operating margin of 12% — too thin for a protected franchise.

### Low Price Plus — LIKELY FIT
*"Cheapest — and still the best"*

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Across all four years management explains volumes almost entirely through the LNG commodity price cycle — demand collapsed at $40-50/mmbtu spot in FY23, drove a 22% throughput jump when prices fell in FY24, and Dahej utilisation fell to 53% in March 2026 when the Gulf crisis spiked prices to $25; but this is a tolling/flow business (regas fee per TBTU, 5% annual tariff escalation, use-or-pay), so PBT has stayed in a narrow band (Rs 4,335cr FY23 → 4,757 → 5,275 → 5,158cr FY26) even as volumes swung.

> "demand is price sensitive. It is not possible to consume gas at $40, $50" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/operations-linked demand under long-term tolling contracts with use-or-pay penalties and contractual 5% annual tariff escalation, which decouples earnings from throughput swings*

## What to watch
