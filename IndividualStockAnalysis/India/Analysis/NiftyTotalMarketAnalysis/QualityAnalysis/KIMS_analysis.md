# Krishna Institu. (KIMS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

KIMS (Krishna Institute of Medical Sciences) runs a chain of private multi-specialty hospitals in India, selling in-patient treatment (surgeries and admissions, including complex work like organ transplants, neurosurgery, cardiac, cancer and orthopaedic care) and out-patient doctor consultations to individual patients. It earns money per patient treated — the calls track revenue as beds occupied times average revenue per occupied bed (ARPOB) and average revenue per patient (ARPP) — with bills paid by patients in cash, by health insurers, and by government schemes such as Aarogyasri. It started in and is still anchored in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana (about 3,600 beds across those two states in 2022) and has since spread to Maharashtra, Karnataka and Kerala, reaching 25 hospitals across 5 states by early 2026, with a Chennai hospital under construction. Growth comes from building new hospitals, adding beds at existing ones, recruiting senior consultants, and buying existing hospitals such as Sunshine.

**The parts of the business:**

- **In-patient (IP) business** — Admitted patients having surgery or treatment — the main revenue driver, measured by IP volumes, occupancy and length of stay; volumes were about 61,000 in a single quarter (Q3 FY26) and grew roughly 15% over FY26.
- **Out-patient (OP) / OPD consultations** — Walk-in doctor consultations that also feed admissions and tests; about 4 lakh consultations in FY24, rising to roughly 5.85 lakh in a single quarter by Q3 FY26.
- **Telangana cluster (Hyderabad/Secunderabad, Kondapur, Kompally)** — The original home market and largest cluster, including the flagship Secunderabad unit doing robotic and transplant work, with about 600 beds added at Kondapur and a new 6 lakh sq ft Secunderabad building being built over 2-3 years.
- **Andhra Pradesh cluster (Guntur, Ongole, Nellore, Vizag/Seetamadara)** — The other core home-state cluster of tier-1 and tier-2 town hospitals; two new Guntur units, a Vizag unit, a cancer block at Ongole and a mother-and-child unit at Nellore were added in FY26.
- **Sunshine Hospitals (acquired)** — A majority-stake acquisition completed in FY23 that brought a nationally known orthopaedics franchise; it came in at a lower 17.8% EBITDA margin than the rest of KIMS but management repeatedly says it has since improved as expected.
- **Maharashtra cluster (Nagpur, Nashik, Thane, Sangli)** — The main out-of-home-state expansion: Nagpur was the first and turned around to roughly 20% EBITDA on about INR18 crore monthly revenue, followed by Nashik, a 290-bed Thane (Mumbai) hospital on 99-year leased land, and Sangli.
- **Karnataka / Bangalore cluster (Mahadevapura, Electronic City, PES)** — Newest big-city cluster, commissioned in 2025 with a dedicated cluster CEO; ARPOB is high (above INR76,000) but it is the largest source of current EBITDA losses, expected to turn EBITDA-neutral through FY27.
- **Kerala (Kollam)** — A single new hospital opened in 2025, part of the FY26 batch of seven new units that lifted revenue but dragged margins.
- **Mature units vs newer units** — Management's own split of the P&L: in Q4 FY26 mature hospitals produced about INR862 crore of the INR1,085 crore revenue at roughly 28.5-30% EBITDA margin, while units under one year old contributed about INR224 crore of revenue but eroded EBITDA and pulled the group margin down to 19.9%.
- **Specialty centres of excellence** — High-end clinical programmes management highlights as the brand's pull — organ transplants (over 430 renal, 120 liver and 100 heart-and-lung transplants in 2025), neurosciences (10,000+ brain tumour surgeries), oncology, renal and gastro sciences, robotic surgery (500+ in 2025) and equipment like Gamma Knife and Tulsa Pro.
- **Fertility centres and KIMS Cuddles (mother and child)** — Smaller newer formats — fertility centres opened at Hyderabad and Guntur and a mother-and-child unit at Nellore in FY26; the calls give no separate revenue figures for them.

### The story in depth

The strongest thing about KIMS is the same thing that produces its best pattern scores: genuine clinical depth in procedures where patients and referring doctors are buying certainty rather than a low price. The quality framework gives full marks to assurance benefits (marquee surgeons drawing international patients, more than 10,000 brain tumour surgeries, 650+ transplants in a year) and to intangible benefits, where the company keeps ₹79 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs. That depth is what makes Pricing Power and Innovation Dominance read as likely fits — average revenue per bed rises every year (+14% for FY26), helped by a steady drumbeat of named clinical firsts such as Gamma Knife, MRgFUS, Tulsa Pro and 500+ robotic surgeries in 2025. It is also the main defence against the Good Enough Goods risk: cheaper challengers cannot replicate transplant and complex-surgery programmes built over two decades.

The second thread is expansion, and here one trait drives a pattern and a risk at the same time. Heavy growth spending — seven new hospitals launched in 2025, an 800-bed Kondapur flagship, Thane, a Chennai lease — is what earns the Market Share Gainers fit (from about 3,600 beds in two states in 2022 to 25 hospitals across five states, FY26 revenue up 28.2%, Mahadevapura at ₹17 crores a month within 8-9 months). The same spending is why the returns checks score badly: incremental returns on the new capital are so far negative, with new units eroding ₹32 crores of EBITDA in Q4 FY26, and the business as a whole turned only ₹8 of operating cash on every ₹100 of capital. The build-out also sits directly in front of the two live risks — new entrants (roughly 3,000 beds added in the Kondapur micro-market in 4-5 years, a rival heading past 1,000 beds) and payer concentration, where delayed empanelment with STAR, Medi Assist and GIPSA explicitly held back the ramp at Nashik, Thane, Mahadevapura and Electronic City. Even the pricing story is qualified by the same intermediary: management concedes most of the revenue-per-bed lift comes from case, geography and stay-length mix, and that insurers push back on rates.

What holds the story together is funding and management conduct; what strains it is the shareholder side of capital allocation. Customers effectively finance the business — cash comes back about 50 days before suppliers need paying — and the founder-family team has delivered several promises it made on calls, lifting Sunshine's margin from 17.8% toward roughly 26% and turning Nagpur EBITDA-positive as guided, while owning misses candidly (both candor and working capital score full marks). Against that, there has been no dividend or buyback discussion in five years; the capital action ran the other way, a ₹1,500 crore dilutive share issue to retire debt after FY26 earnings per share fell 37%. That mix — strong customer proposition, weak returns on the newest capital, only three of eleven multibagger patterns rated a likely fit, four risks on watch — produces the 63/100 'Decent' rating (65 business quality, 49 pattern fit, 76 risk safety) and a resilience read of STRAINED. The company is not fragile; it is spending faster than its new assets currently pay back.
