# Aadhar Hsg. Fin. (AADHARHFC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aadhar Housing Finance is an Indian housing finance company that lends small, secured mortgage loans to low-income households — mainly the economically weaker section and lower income group, with an average loan of about INR 10-11 lakhs and roughly 55-56% of borrowers being salaried. It sells home loans for buying a house, self-construction on owned land, and home improvement or extension, plus small loans against property; it does not lend to corporates or developers, so the book is entirely retail and secured against property at about a 59-60% loan-to-value. It makes money on the spread between what it charges borrowers (portfolio yield of about 13.7% as of Dec 2025) and what it pays to borrow (exit cost of funds about 7.7%), funding itself with bank loans (~51%), National Housing Bank refinance (~22%), non-convertible debentures (~19%) and some external commercial borrowings. It operates pan-India through a branch-led sourcing model — 626 branches across 22 states and 550+ districts as of March 2026, with no single state more than about 15% of assets — serving over 3.2 lakh live customers, with assets under management of INR 30,571 crores in FY26 (up from INR 21,120 crores in FY24).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Home loans** — Loans to low-income, mostly first-time buyers for purchasing a home, building on land they already own, or extending/improving a house — the core of the business at 73-75% of AUM throughout the period, with an incremental ticket size of about INR 12.9 lakhs in FY24.
- **Micro loan against property (non-housing)** — Small loans given against a borrower's property as collateral, described by management as 'micro-LAP' with a ticket size of around INR 8.2 lakhs in FY24; it is the balance 25-27% of AUM and management says the split with home loans stays well balanced.
- **EWS / LIG focus segment** — Not a separate product but the customer segment the whole company is built around — economically weaker section and lower income group borrowers, about 71% of AUM in FY24, served in urban and semi-urban locations.
- **Urban and Emerging strategy** — An internal split of the branch network introduced in FY26 — roughly 130 branches in the top 15 cities classed as 'urban' and the rest as 'emerging' — run with different ticket size, yield and manpower targets; management says urban faces heavy bank competition while emerging markets carry the steadier low-income demand.
- **In-house collections** — The company runs its entire collections function itself with no outsourcing across all delinquency buckets, which management links to gross NPA of 1.08% and collection efficiency above 99.8% at FY26 year-end.
- **PMAY 2.0 / government subsidy channel** — Aadhar routes government interest-subsidy benefits under Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana 2.0 to its borrowers — over 10,000 customers had received first subsidy tranches by Q3 FY26 — which management describes as a demand driver rather than a revenue line of its own.

### The story in depth

Aadhar Housing Finance lends small home loans — an average of about INR 10.9 lakh — to roughly 3.2 lakh low-income borrowers, and almost everything good about the business traces back to one thing: it owns the ground it sells on. The branch count has gone from 523 in FY24 to 626 by March 2026, spread over 22 states, 550-plus districts and around 11,000 of India's 18,000 PIN codes, with collections never outsourced and about 2,000 collectors on its own payroll. That network is why the quality framework gives full marks to distribution and to customer convenience, and it is the same fact that makes the multibagger screen call Market Share Gainers a strong fit — the share of the low-income housing finance pool has moved from 16-17% to about 18%, with loan book growth of 20-23% a year to INR 30,571 crores, and it was won by building branches rather than buying anything. The same network is also what the Cost to Replicate and Forward Integrators patterns are pointing at, and it is the main thing standing between the company and the New Entrants risk, where management openly admits banks are pushing down into its urban territory. Sitting on top of it is a management team that scores highly on both candour and prudence: the same MD and CFO across FY24-FY26, guidance on growth, profit, costs and credit losses repeatedly set and then met, an unprompted apology for weak disclosure on an early call, and voluntarily disclosed numbers that do not flatter — which is why Corporate Culture is the screen's second strong fit.

The interesting part is where a single trait shows up as both a strength and a threat. The business earns a portfolio yield of about 13.7% and a return on assets near 4.4% because it serves informal, low-income borrowers that cheaper bank money does not underwrite well — but that same high yield is exactly what makes the Good Enough Goods risk live, since a home loan is a fairly undifferentiated product and the cheapest way for a rival to attack is to refinance the customer away. That is not theoretical: about 5.6% of the book walks out through balance transfers each year, management runs a dedicated retention team, and it keeps customers by cutting their rate rather than by being preferred. This is the direct reason the framework marks image- or reputation-based pricing power as a negative, why the Pricing Power and Brand Strength patterns land at only partial fit, and why the Toll Roads pattern gets no fit at all — management itself describes these borrowers as sensitive to the size of the monthly instalment. The same double-edge runs through policy. Being the number-one player on PMAY 2.0 interest subsidies, with over 10,000 customers, helps explain the share gains and supports the top score on end-market growth. It is also the single highest-rated risk in the review: every earnings call anchors the demand story in government programmes, subsidy schemes, guarantee funds and rate cuts. The honest offset is that the underlying need — a roughly 10 crore unit housing shortfall, 9.5 crore of it in the lowest-income brackets — exists whether or not the subsidy does, and management says plainly that disbursements are not caused by the subsidy. Cost discipline plays a similar balancing role against the branch expansion: costs as a share of income fell from 37.5% to 36.4% to 35.9% even while roughly 46 branches were added in FY26 and 40-50 more are promised each year, which is why the capex being spent is judged growth spending rather than upkeep.

What keeps the risk review calm is deliberate conservatism, and it is the same conservatism the framework credits under disciplined stewardship: no acquisitions, no builder or corporate lending at all, borrowings spread across 42 relationships, no single state above 15% of the book, a capital cushion of 42-44% tier-one against about 3.1 times leverage, and an INR 45 crore provision buffer held back on purpose. Add fully secured lending at 60% loan-to-value, 55% salaried borrowers, collection efficiency above 99% and bad loans in a narrow 1.05-1.38% band, and five of the eight risk channels come out low or merely worth watching. The gap is that the strengths are operational rather than structural. Outside distribution, growth and management, the scorecard thins out fast — returns on capital score neutral, industry structure scores negative, and three of the eleven multibagger patterns simply do not apply: there is no recurring or subscription revenue, no intermediary channel to cultivate, and no position as a small-but-essential cost the customer will not switch away from. Technology investment is real and multi-year, but with no research spending disclosed it reads as sensible modernisation, not dominance. Roughly three-quarters of the loan book reprices with rates, so cuts have to be passed on and yield has already drifted from 13.8% to 13.71%, even though spreads have been held at 5.7-5.97%. That combination — an execution-led, policy-supported, price-exposed lender run by candid and careful people — is what produces the combined score of 53 out of 100 and a Decent, three-star verdict rather than anything stronger, and the resilience question was left unresolved.

## What to watch

- Balance transfer outflows: the 5.6% annual run-rate rising, or retention needing deeper rate cuts, would confirm the Good Enough Goods risk and further weaken the already-partial Pricing Power fit.
- Spread and yield: spreads slipping below the 5.7-5.97% band, or yield falling faster than 13.8%-to-13.71%, would undercut the ~4.4% return on assets that the whole high-yield model rests on.
- Policy support: any change to PMAY 2.0 subsidies or the wider housing-for-all push would test the highest-rated risk, Government Dependency, and the claim that disbursements are not subsidy-driven.
- Cost of the branch build-out: if the guided 40-50 branches a year stop being matched by falling cost-to-income (37.5% to 35.9% so far), the distribution advantage and the growth-capex check both weaken.
- Bank competition in urban markets: visible share loss or slowing growth below the 20% rate would move the New Entrants risk from watch to real and dent the Market Share Gainers pattern.
- Credit quality: bad loans breaking out of the 1.05-1.38% range, collection efficiency dropping below 99%, or use of the INR 45 crore buffer would challenge both the Cyclicality view and the disciplined-stewardship score.
