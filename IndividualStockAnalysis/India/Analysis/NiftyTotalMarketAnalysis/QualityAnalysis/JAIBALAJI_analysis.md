# Jai Balaji Inds (JAIBALAJI) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Jai Balaji Industries is a 24-year-old Indian steel maker, based in Kolkata, that runs fully integrated plants in West Bengal and Chhattisgarh, including a 1.1 million tonne greenfield steel facility. It converts iron ore and coking coal into finished steel products and sells them mainly to infrastructure and industrial buyers — its two flagship, higher-margin lines are ductile iron (DI) pipes used for water supply and drainage schemes such as the government's Jal Jeevan Mission and AMRUT, and specialised ferro alloys sold to Indian and overseas steel customers under long-term contracts, with exports going to more than 40 countries. It makes money on the spread between raw-material cost and the price per tonne of what it sells, so management talks in tonnes shipped, realisation per tonne and EBITDA per tonne (Q1 FY25: about INR21,000/tonne on DI pipes and INR37,000/tonne on ferro alloys versus INR5,000–7,000 on plain steel items). Revenue ran around INR1,700 crore a quarter in FY25 with EBITDA margins of roughly 14%–19%, and the company has been shifting its mix toward the two value-added lines (from about 30% of revenue in FY24 to 45%–55%, targeting 80%) while cutting net term debt from INR871 crore in FY23 to INR221 crore in FY25.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Ductile Iron (DI) Pipes** — Large iron pipes for carrying water and sewage, sold largely into government water-infrastructure projects; this is the single biggest and most important business — roughly 30% of revenue in FY24 rising toward 45%–50%, about a 10% share of the Indian DI pipe market (targeted to reach 18%–20%), with capacity expanded from 3 lakh tonnes to 5.04 lakh tonnes in Q4 FY25 and headed to 6.6 lakh tonnes.
- **Specialized Ferro Alloys** — Custom alloy grades sold to steel makers in India and abroad that fetch a premium over benchmark prices; the second pillar at roughly 20%–25% of revenue (management aimed for 35%) and the highest EBITDA per tonne of any product, with capacity going from 1.66 lakh tonnes to 1.9 lakh tonnes, though the expansion slipped to Q1 FY27 after delays with a Chinese equipment supplier.
- **TMT Bars** — Ordinary reinforcement steel bars for construction — a commodity, lower-margin line (about INR6,000 EBITDA per tonne) whose capacity was raised from 2.6 to 3 lakh tonnes through low-cost debottlenecking.
- **Pig Iron** — Basic iron sold on as a commodity product at roughly INR7,000 EBITDA per tonne; one of the non-core items management says has suffered falling volumes and realisations.
- **Sponge Iron** — Another commodity steel input sold externally at about INR5,000 EBITDA per tonne, the lowest-margin item in the portfolio and part of the mix the company is deliberately shrinking in relative terms.
- **Integrated upstream and cost-saving operations** — Not sold to outsiders but central to profitability — blast furnaces producing hot metal (capacity being revamped from 6.3 to 7.5 lakh tonnes), sinter plants (9 lakh tonnes going to 12 lakh tonnes) that substitute for costlier iron ore, a 6 lakh tonne iron ore beneficiation unit, captive power including a 35 TPH blast-furnace waste-gas boiler, and three owned railway sidings, all aimed at the lowest cost structure.
- **Other Pipes and Tubes (new)** — A diversification the board approved in May 2025 to extend the existing pipe business into additional pipe and tube products; still at the proposal stage with no size given in the calls.

### The story in depth

Jai Balaji is a 24-year steel operator that has spent the last few years cleaning itself up and pushing into ductile iron (DI) water pipes and specialised ferroalloys, and on the balance-sheet side the delivery is real. Net term debt came down from ₹871 crore in FY23 to ₹221 crore, net debt to EBITDA sits at 0.25 against management's own 0.6 guidance, and roughly ₹822 crore of capex — including a ₹1,000 crore programme to take DI pipe capacity from 3 to 6.6 lakh tons plus ferroalloys and a blast furnace revamp — was paid for entirely out of money the business generated, with FY25 return on equity around 26%. That combination is why the framework gives credit for disciplined stewardship and for genuine growth (not maintenance) capex, and why the 'Corporate Culture' pattern registers as a partial fit. The catch is that these are inputs, not proven outcomes: the checks note incremental returns are still unproven because DI EBITDA per ton has been falling, and capacity actually commissioned (3 to 5.04 lakh tons in Q4 FY25) has run ahead of volumes sold — FY25 DI production of 2.82 lakh tons missed the target.

The single fact that drives most of the upside also drives most of the danger: the customer is the government. Management frames FY25 as a trough because ordering slowed after the election and DI EBITDA per ton bottomed at ₹19,000, with a budget-funded Jal Jeevan Mission and AMRUT 2.0 recovery ahead — that is the basis for the positive marks on cyclical market growth and market-share runway from a non-dominant ~10% share. The very same dependence shows up as a HIGH RISK on Government Dependency (management concedes most DI pipe buyers are government bodies, and FY25 was hit directly by the post-election spending swing) and as HIGH RISK on Cyclicality, with EBITDA margin swinging 14% to 19% and back to 14% across six quarters. Selling into tenders to a single class of buyer also explains why so many of the multibagger patterns come back as no fit: no pricing power (management guided to 4-5% realisation pressure in Nov 2024 and reported in May 2025 that realisations fell for almost all products), no friendly middleman (buyer and payer are the same body), no brand, and a scored -2 on oligopoly because tender buyers hold maximum negotiating power. Competitive advantage is the weakest quality area at -1.0, and the growth story and the concentration risk are the same story told twice.

The defences are genuine but thin, and each one has a matching risk entry. The itemised cost programme — three railway sidings, captive power, iron ore beneficiation, sinter saving ₹1.5 per kg, a 35 TPH waste-gas boiler — earns a partial 'Low Price Plus' fit, but it is also management's only answer to the New Entrants risk, where analysts twice pressed on peers adding DI capacity in the East; the review notes the defence is cost leadership rather than any structural barrier, which is also why 'Cost to Replicate' scores no fit. Specialised ferroalloys, where the company claims to be the only organised domestic producer, exports to about 40 countries and earns a premium, is both the partial 'Toll Roads' fit and the partial hedge against government dependency — yet that same line depends on a Chinese supplier whose delay pushed the expansion from FY25 to Q1 FY27, a supply single-point flagged under Stakeholder Concentration. Cheaper substitute pipe materials (PVC, HDPE, OPVC) sit behind both the Good Enough Goods and Technological Innovation watch flags, and the company's own answer — a board-approved OPVC line, itself facing a Spanish equipment supply crunch — is hedging against its own main product rather than deepening a moat. Governance adds the last drag: candor scores -1 because the January 2024 'net debt free in 18 months' promise was quietly rolled forward call after call and segment data is withheld; there is no dividend or buyback policy, only a 1:5 stock split for liquidity; and the board is family-dominated with four Jajodia-family managing director exits and the CFO role passing within the family, with no succession or talent pipeline discussed. The combined rating is 34 out of 100 — Weak, one star — from 44 on business quality, 10 on multibagger fit and 44 on risk safety, with resilience judged SOUND. The fair reading is a well-run, deleveraged cyclical manufacturer, not a compounding franchise.
