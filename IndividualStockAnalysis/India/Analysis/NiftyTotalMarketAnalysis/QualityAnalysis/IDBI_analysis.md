# IDBI Bank Ltd. (IDBI) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

IDBI Bank Limited is an Indian bank, headquartered in Mumbai and listed on the BSE and NSE, that takes deposits from customers and lends the money out as loans. It serves both retail (individual) customers and corporate borrowers — management says the loan book is run to a target mix of roughly 62%–68% retail with the balance corporate — and as of the September 2022 quarter it held about Rs. 2,30,000 crores of deposits against net advances of about Rs. 1,46,752 crores, on a balance sheet of roughly Rs. 3,09,000 crores. It earns mainly from the spread between interest on loans and investments and what it pays on deposits (net interest income of Rs. 2,738 crores in Q2 FY23 at a net interest margin of about 4.37%), plus fee-type 'other income' such as commission, exchange and brokerage, forex income, dividends from its three subsidiaries, and recoveries on previously written-off loans. It operates in India, is majority-owned by the Government of India (about 45%) and LIC (about 49%) — who are running a disinvestment process — and it came out of the RBI's Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) restrictions in March 2021, after which it resumed growing both retail and corporate lending.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Retail advances** — Loans to individual/small customers, which management calls its priority — a 'differentiated granular approach to build up more retail business' — and the main driver of loan growth, adding about Rs. 4,800 crores in the September 2022 quarter; retail is targeted at roughly 62%–68% of total advances.
- **Corporate advances** — Loans to companies, pursued on a 'calibrated approach' with emphasis on mid-corporate relationships (including rebuilding ties with mid-corporates it had to exit during the PCA period) and 'lesser emphasis' on large corporates; corporate makes up the remaining roughly one-third of the loan book.
- **Deposits (CASA, retail and bulk)** — The bank's funding base of about Rs. 2,30,000 crores, unusually skewed to cheap current and savings accounts with a CASA ratio of about 56%, alongside retail term deposits and a small bulk-deposit book (about 5.5%) that management plans to grow toward 8%–10%.
- **Treasury / investments** — The bank's portfolio of securities, largely government bonds, split into AFS (available-for-sale, modified duration about 1.27) and HTM buckets; it earns interest on investments (up about 15% year-on-year) and occasionally books trading gains, such as the G-sec sale gains in the June 2022 quarter.
- **Priority sector lending** — Regulator-mandated lending to designated sectors, where management says the bank has 'achieved all the targets' with no shortfall, so its associated RIDF/PSL deposits are simply running off on maturity.
- **Fee and other income** — Non-interest earnings including commission, exchange and brokerage (up about 10% year-on-year), forex income, dividends from the three subsidiaries, and recoveries from written-off accounts (Rs. 26 crores in the September 2022 quarter); total other income grew about 11% year-on-year.
- **Stressed-asset recovery and resolution** — A significant, actively managed activity of recovering money from bad loans through settlements, upgrades, NCLT/insolvency cases and write-offs — the bank guided to about Rs. 4,000 crores of recoveries and upgrades for FY23, had recovered Rs. 1,136 crores by the June quarter, and wrote off about Rs. 5,200 crores in the September quarter, bringing net NPA down to 1.15% with a provision coverage ratio of 97.86%.
- **Digital banking channels** — ATMs, internet banking, a mobile app and other alternate channels through which over 95% of customer-initiated transactions now flow, with UPI taking 'the lion's share' (about 75%); the bank says it keeps investing in IT to expand this footprint and plans to set up digital banking units eventually.
- **Subsidiaries and joint ventures** — Three subsidiaries that pay dividends into other income; the bank also fully exited its life insurance joint venture with Ageas (Ageas Federal), booking a gain of Rs. 380 crores in the September 2022 quarter on top of an earlier Rs. 140–141 crore stake-sale gain in the June quarter.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

IDBI Bank comes out of this work as a genuine recovery story that has not yet become a genuinely good business, and the combined score of 46 out of 100 (Mixed, two stars) reflects exactly that split: reasonable business quality at 45 and roughly similar risk safety at 48. The bright spots are all about the direction of travel rather than the underlying franchise. Growth scores the maximum, helped by a strong bottom-of-the-cycle reading: the bank has just exited the RBI's prompt corrective action regime, its credit cost has collapsed to 0.18%, bad loans are falling, and management expects the private investment cycle to turn into a multi-year credit upturn. Underneath that, the economics are weak. Return on capital scores the worst possible mark, because a balance sheet of Rs.3.09 lakh crore with a 7.5% leverage ratio is earning only about 1% on assets, and there is nothing protecting that spread. Competitive advantage and industry structure both score badly for the same reason: the bank holds no monopoly-like position anywhere, Indian banking is fragmented, and IDBI is openly a price-taker on both deposits and loans.

The single trait that most shapes this report is the cheap deposit base, and it cuts both ways. A CASA ratio of 55-56% and a 3.44% cost of deposits, alongside a cost-to-income ratio held at 42-43%, are what make the Low Price Plus and Pricing Power patterns come out as partial rather than absent: margins jumped 135 basis points year-on-year to 4.37% because loans repriced faster than deposits and management deliberately chose not to lead the market on deposit rates. That same cheap funding is the stated silver lining under three separate risks - it softens the Good Enough Goods problem of selling commodity products, it cushions cyclicality, and it partly offsets the concentration in a few large corporate exposures. But the records are clear that this is rate-cycle transmission, not durable pricing power, which is why the Pricing, Mix and Volume check earns only a modest positive while the mini-monopoly and oligopoly checks are both negative. The same caution applies to the headline profit: the halo-effect check flags that the record result leans on items that will not repeat - Rs.380 crore and Rs.141 crore of stake-sale gains, interest on income-tax refunds, provision write-backs and the rate cycle itself - so the reported numbers overstate how much the business actually earns from ordinary operations.

The two high risks, cyclicality and government dependency, trace back to the same history that blocks three of the eleven patterns outright. Because the bank was under prompt corrective action until March 2021, it shrank rather than gained share, which is why Market Share Gainers is a clear no-fit - 17% loan growth is described as merely matching the system, while deposits were flat to declining and an investor pushed management on it. The same period cost the bank its corporate relationships, now being rebuilt, and left it without recruitment for roughly four years, which explains both the negative score on talent development and why Cost to Replicate finds nothing here that a competitor could not copy. Results still swing on a handful of named accounts: Rs.868 crore of prior-year income came from Kingfisher and Videocon recoveries, Videocon alone is a Rs.4,500 crore exposure, and a single Future Group relationship accounted for Rs.356 crore of one quarter's corporate slippage. Ownership adds a layer the bank cannot manage - the government holds about 45% and LIC about 49%, and the whole strategic direction sits inside a divestment process management will not discuss, which is the one risk carrying no silver lining at all. The genuine offset is behaviour: management strips out one-off items, warns analysts against comparing to a flattered base, reports guidance against actual line by line, and has repeatedly beaten its own targets while provisioning far above what regulators require, with a 97.86% provision cover and 19.5% capital. That culture is why the Corporate Culture pattern registers as partial and why the stewardship and candour checks are positive, but it is discipline in a business whose long-run resilience the review still marks as unknown.
