### The story in depth

Asahi India Glass comes out as a decent business rather than an exceptional one, scoring 61 out of 100 (three stars) from a blend of 62 for business quality, 31 for multibagger fit and 96 for risk safety. The clearest strength is what customers are willing to pay for: after direct input costs the company keeps ₹70 of every ₹100 of sales, the single best mark in the whole quality framework (Intangible benefits, +2), and out of every ₹100 of sales ₹18 ends up as operating profit. That profitability has been remarkably steady, moving only about ±2.2 percentage points a year, which the framework reads two ways at once — as evidence that costs are under control (Profit margin, +1) and as a sign of an industry where players price rationally and few new competitors have shown up (Barriers to entry, +1).

That same pricing-power trait is what the multibagger screen keeps circling: Brand Strength and Innovation Dominance register as quantitative signals, and Pricing Power, Toll Roads, Forward Integrators, Friendly Middleman and Recurring Revenue all come back as partial fits. Importantly, none of these could be confirmed or denied, because no conference-call judgement was available for any of the eleven patterns — so the low fit score of 31 reflects unconfirmed evidence as much as it reflects absent qualities. Only Low Price Plus and Market Share Gainers are clear non-fits, which is consistent with the picture elsewhere: this is a company that earns from price and position, not from being cheapest or from taking share. The same evidence gap shows up in the quality framework, where Competitive Advantage and Management could not be scored at all.

Where the story turns is in what the business does with that profitability. Good margins are not converting into good returns: every ₹100 of shareholders' funds and borrowings generated only ₹8 of operating cash this year, and every ₹1 of assets produces just ₹0.64 of sales. The reason sits in the working capital check — roughly 120 days of cash stays stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, the only negative mark in the framework (Cost of working capital, −1, dragging Capital Allocation to −1.0). So the pricing power that powers the pattern fits is partly offset by a capital-hungry operating cycle. Growth tells a similar story: about 8% a year on average, but lumpy year to year, which is scored as neither a strength nor a weakness and lines up with the one risk finding — a Cyclicality flag raised by the numbers, with the calls saying nothing either way. Overall resilience is judged SOUND and risk safety is the highest of the three scores at 96, but that partly reflects how little the risk review found to speak to, not a proven absence of danger.
