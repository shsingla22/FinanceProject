# Delhivery (DELHIVERY) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Delhivery is an Indian logistics company that picks up, carries and delivers other companies' goods across India — everything from a single e-commerce parcel to pallets of factory freight — and calls itself the country's largest integrated logistics platform. Its customers are businesses: online marketplaces, direct-to-consumer brands, SMEs, and large manufacturers in autos, electricals, FMCG, paints and engineering, plus some consumer-to-consumer shipping through its own app; it had ~29,000 customers in FY23 and over 51,000 by Q3 FY26. It makes money by charging freight/service fees per parcel or per tonne shipped (and fees for warehousing and supply-chain work), earning more as volumes and per-shipment yields rise while spreading costs over a fixed network. It runs that network itself — about 21.9 million sq ft of hubs, gateways, automated sort centres, processing centres and delivery centres reaching ~18,838 pin codes across India, with a 71,000-strong team, 67,000 partner agents and a 21,000 fleet — and operates almost entirely within India, with cross-border services to and from abroad. FY26 revenue from services was about Rs 10,486 crore with PAT of Rs 347 crore, and it closed the year with over Rs 4,500 crore of cash after acquiring rival Ecom Express.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Express Parcel** — Door-to-door delivery of individual e-commerce and D2C parcels (including same-day and heavier 'heavy goods' items) — the biggest business at roughly two-thirds of revenue, Rs 6,685 crore and over a billion shipments in FY26, at the top of its 16–18% service-EBITDA margin band.
- **Part Truckload (PTL) / freight** — Consolidated business-to-business freight where several customers' loads share a truck, sold by the tonne — about 20–21% of revenue, Rs 2,254 crore and 2 million metric tonnes in FY26, and the segment whose margins management has been steadily repairing since the SpotOn acquisition and integration in FY22–23.
- **Truckload (FTL / full truckload)** — Booking whole trucks for large single shipments, won largely through customer tenders (RFQs); a smaller, more opportunistic line that management says it bids on selectively rather than chasing volume.
- **Supply Chain Services / Solutions (SCS)** — Running warehouses and end-to-end distribution for large manufacturers and retailers (autos, electricals, FMCG, appliances, luxury/lifestyle) under long-cycle contracts — Rs 729 crore of revenue in FY26, small but turned profitable this year with service EBITDA quadrupling to Rs 79 crore (10.9%) after exiting loss-making accounts such as a quick-commerce mother warehouse.
- **Cross-Border** — Shipping goods between India and other countries, including ocean freight and a new 'Delhivery International' air economy product aimed at SME exporters — a small line whose results swing with global freight rates and volumes.
- **Delhivery Direct (local / rapid)** — On-demand intra-city delivery and consumer-to-consumer shipping via the Delhivery app, recently extended to Mumbai and Hyderabad — an early-stage growth bet, not yet a material revenue contributor.
- **Technology, data and engineering (incl. TransportOne SaaS)** — In-house software and data science that runs the network, now also sold externally — the TransportOne SaaS product, a Freight Index One benchmark, patented address/ID systems and R&D in automation, robotics and delivery drones; described as a competitive moat rather than a large standalone revenue line.
- **Financial services (new vertical)** — One of the 'next growth pillars' management says it is now funding from core transport cash flows, alongside local, cross-border and rapid; described as a long-cycle bet with no revenue detail given in the calls.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Delhivery earns a Strong rating of 70 out of 100, built from 66 on business quality, 71 on multibagger fit and 74 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound. The core of the story is a physical network that is very hard to copy: 18,838 pincodes, 4,730 delivery centres, 21.9 million square feet, 49 automated sorting centres and 123 gateways assembled over fifteen years. That reach is what the framework credits under convenience benefits, and it is the same asset behind the Cost to Replicate pattern and the Low Price Plus pattern, where scale has translated into itemised network savings, capital spending falling from 7.8% of revenue to 4.7%, receivable days down to 11 and return on invested capital reaching 16%. Sitting on top of the network is a steady stream of technology work — US patents, proprietary sorting and dispatch, auto-docking that saves 20 to 30 minutes per truck, robotics, drones and AI — which scores full marks on both technology advantage and research and development, and which is the direct evidence for the Innovation Dominance pattern. Crucially, that same innovation cadence is what keeps the technological-change risk at merely watch rather than elevated: the company is on the innovating side of the shift, and it funds the work out of its own cash flow rather than from outside money.

The share-gain story and the biggest single-customer risk come from exactly the same source. Express volumes grew 43% year-on-year in one quarter and 72-73% in another, full-year volumes rose about 40% to a billion parcels, and customers went from 29,000 to more than 51,000 — all against an end market growing 15-20%. The framework scores both market share gain and share donators at full marks, pointing to a distressed Ecom Express that was bought outright, struggling smaller rivals, and expensive in-house delivery arms whose volumes are moving to outsourced players. But those large platforms are also the concentration risk: Shopee's exit alone removed 22 million parcels and roughly ₹60 crore in a single quarter, management concedes lumpiness at the platform level, and growth is habitually described as coming from the non-large-marketplace segments. The New Entrants risk is the mirror image of the same relationship — Amazon and Flipkart opening their captive logistics arms to outside customers would turn today's donors into tomorrow's competitors, and management's answers on that point are described as defensive. The broadening customer base, the newer D2C, SME and channel-partner segments, and the Ecom Express consolidation are the offsets, which is why both risks sit at watch rather than higher.

The clearest weakness is that all of this scale converts into only a thin slice of profit, and that weakness ties several findings together. Out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹6 is operating profit, and margins have swung by roughly 34.5 points a year, which points to key costs — fuel above all — sitting outside management's hands. That is the same fact behind the elevated cyclicality verdict, alongside first-quarter troughs, festive third-quarter peaks and an e-commerce market management itself calls volatile. It also explains why pricing-related patterns stop short of a strong fit: management openly describes logistics as a commodity where customers actively negotiate shipping cost, so Toll Roads is a clear no-fit, Pricing Power is only a likely fit resting on yield mix and diesel pass-through clauses rather than genuine price increases, and Brand Strength is partial because the name carries no premium. The Good Enough Goods risk follows directly — with no premium to defend, the threat is a rival cheap enough rather than better, and the only real defence is being the lowest-cost operator, evidenced by 18% Express service margins achieved without raising prices and by rivals reportedly losing money at Delhivery's price points. Against that, the framework rates management highly: a founder-chief executive since 2011 who stayed with the parcel-truckload business through a badly handled integration until it reached 11%-plus margins, disclosure of the SpotOn failure with a line-by-line cost bridge and an admitted timeline miss, flagging of flattering accounting items, and a visible internal promotion pipeline — together the basis for the Corporate Culture pattern and the candor score. Demand itself is functional and repeat-purchase across 51,000 customers, so fashion risk and shifting-preference risk both rate low, though revenue is transactional rather than contracted, with only the small software and data layer counting as genuinely recurring.
