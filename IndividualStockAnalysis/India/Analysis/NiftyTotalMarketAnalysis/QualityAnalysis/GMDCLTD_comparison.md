## Step 2 — Where it improved and where it regressed

### Bucket 1 — Business quality (34-check framework)

Of the 34 quality checks, 28 could be genuinely re-tested on both views: 10 improved, 2 regressed, 16 unchanged. 2 checks carry the long view's verdict forward unchanged (the window could not re-test them).

| Area | Long view | Last one year | Change |

| Return on Capital | Weak (-0.67) | Neutral (-0.33) | +0.33 |
| Growth | Weak (-0.67) | Neutral (-0.33) | +0.33 |

**Checks that improved:**

- **Share donators** (Industry Structure): Weak → Good. Now: Imported coal is the identified share donator that GMDC targets when seaborne prices are elevated, and management frames volume capture explicitly against it. | The long view had said: The competitor from whom share must be taken is imported coal — a globally cost-competitive, price-setting alternative, not a weak donator; the weak assets described are GMDC's own.
- **Profit margin** (Return on Capital): Neutral → Good. Now: Out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹17 is operating profit. Margins have moved about ±3.5 points a year — very steady, a sign costs are under control. | The long view had said: Out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹17 is operating profit. Margins have moved about ±10.8 points a year — sizeable swings, suggesting key costs are outside management's control.
- **Barriers to entry and rationality** (Industry Structure): Neutral → Good. Now: Profit margins have barely moved over the years (±3.5 points) — consistent with rational pricing and few new competitors. And from the calls: Regulatory barriers are high — blocks come only via national auction or government allotment and require years of statutory clearances — and government ownership makes the structure stable, but pricing rationality is externally imposed by seaborne coal rather than chosen. | The long view had said: Profit margins swing by ±10.8 points — a hint of price wars, cost shocks, or new entrants disturbing the industry. And from the calls: Mineral-lease licensing is a genuine barrier, but the MMDR amendment opened mining to private operators and pricing is reactive/discount-driven against imports rather than rational.
- **Distribution to shareholders** (Capital Allocation): Neutral → Good. Now: An analyst explicitly credits an improved dividend payout in FY'25, indicating distributions are rising, though no buyback or articulated distribution policy is presented by management. | The long view had said: Distribution policy is imposed by the state owner (30% of PAT / 5% of net worth) rather than self-designed, with no buybacks, though management frames it positively and payout improved.
- **Gain in market share** (Growth): Neutral → Good. Now: Explicit customer-acquisition targets — Mission 2000 to exceed 2,000 active customers and Mission 18.0 to triple monsoon-quarter lignite sales from 6 lakh to 18 lakh tons — show intent and runway to take share from imported coal. | The long view had said: Genuine runway exists (Gujarat solid-fuel demand ~26mt vs GMDC ~8mt) and customer-count initiatives are underway, but volumes stagnated at 8mt against repeated 10mt targets, so share capture is not evidenced.
- **Structural end-market growth** (Growth): Weak → Neutral. Now: Lignite/thermal coal is a structurally declining end market, offset by genuine secular optionality in rare earths (GMDC nominated by GoI for Ambadongar) and the Ambaji copper project, both still 2-3 years from any output. | The long view had said: Core end market is thermal lignite/coal — mature and structurally challenged with mines exhausting — with critical minerals/rare earth as an unproven 3-year-plus optionality rather than current secular growth.
- **Partial monopoly / broken competition / switching costs** (Industry Structure): Neutral → Good. Now: Regional advantage exists via proximity to Gujarat industrial customers and the freight economics that make imported coal the marginal alternative, but there are no genuine switching costs — customers can and do switch on price. | The long view had said: A state-granted regional resource position exists, but there are no switching costs — customers move to imported coal or gas whenever the differential moves, forcing monthly price corrections.
- **Mini-monopolies** (Industry Structure): Neutral → Good. Now: GMDC is effectively the dominant merchant lignite supplier in Gujarat serving local industry, though its pricing is disciplined by imported coal rather than by monopoly power. | The long view had said: GMDC holds Gujarat's lignite resource position but has no monopoly in the customer's mind — buyers freely switch between lignite, imported coal and gas on economics.
- **Customer types (retail vs corporate)** (Customer Benefits): Weak → Neutral. Now: Entirely corporate/industrial customers, correctly served on cost and availability, but without the switching-cost lock-in or TCO advantage the framework requires — the buyer's alternative is a phone call to an importer. | The long view had said: Customers are corporates (ceramics, utilities, industrial users) buying on landed cost with no switching-cost lock-in — the wrong benefit match, since pricing is dictated by import parity and regulated formulas.
- **Capex for growth (not maintenance)** (Capital Allocation): Good → Excellent. Now: Large, clearly growth-oriented capex — INR ~800 cr land acquisition for new lignite projects plus ~650 cr at Bhavnagar, ~INR 5,000 cr lifetime capex on three Odisha coal blocks, and six new lignite mines with 12 MTPA capacity — but incremental ROIC is unproven with volumes repeatedly slipping. | The long view had said: Capex is large, rising and overwhelmingly growth-oriented (new lignite mines, Odisha coal, copper, ATPS revival) with a stated board hurdle of 15% IRR, but returns are unproven and timelines have repeatedly slipped.

**Checks that regressed:**

- **Disciplined stewards** (Management): Good → Neutral. Now: Growth is organic with no M&A and cash of ~INR 2,000 cr on hand, but the simultaneous pursuit of six new lignite mines, three Odisha coal blocks, copper and rare earths is a broad, capital-hungry 4x plan rather than focused stewardship. | The long view had said: Organic-only growth, debt-free base, an explicit leverage ceiling and self-funded capex from internal accruals plus reserves show prudent stewardship.
- **Distribution advantage** (Competitive Advantage): Good → Neutral. Now: A Gujarat-wide mine and customer network gives local logistics advantage over imports, but nothing described is a reach rivals cannot match, and there is no service dimension. | The long view had said: Inland proximity to Gujarat's demand clusters plus capacity expansion designed to cover the whole state gives a logistics reach imported coal cannot match, though it is freight-based rather than service-based.

*Unchanged: Candor; Return on capital (headline); Geographical expansion; Intangible benefits; Cyclical market growth; Out of limelight; Cost of working capital; Oligopoly (vs duopoly); Halo effect (caution); Independent, long-term and tenacious; Assurance benefits; People matter (talent development); Security by obscurity; Convenience benefits; Asset turn; Pricing, Mix and Volume.*

*Carried forward from the long view unchanged (the window could not re-test these — identical by construction): Persistence of growth (Weak); Technology advantage (Weak).*

## How this comparison was built

Both sides ran the same three analysis engines (BusinessAnalysis, MultibaggerPattern, QualityRisks) through their respective orchestrators:

- **Long view (full AnalystSkill):** statements over ~a decade, the whole call timeline — engines ran with_calls.
- **Last one year (RecentAnalystSkill):** latest fiscal year (prior year as baseline only), last 12 months of calls — engines ran 8 items carried from the full-history analysis, with_calls.

Comparison rules: only verdicts assessed on both views are counted; one-year-lens silences are listed but never scored as regressions; every movement line carries both sides' evidence so the WHY of the change is visible, not just the direction. Research tooling; not investment advice.
