# Yatharth Hospital (YATHARTH) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Yatharth Hospital & Trauma Care Services runs a chain of multi-specialty and super-specialty hospitals in North India, treating patients for serious, complex conditions such as heart disease, cancer, kidney and neurological problems, as well as everyday emergency and trauma care. It earns money by charging patients for inpatient stays, surgeries and procedures, outpatient (OPD) consultations, plus the medicines, diagnostics and other services used during treatment; bills are paid in cash by patients, by private insurers/TPAs, and by government schemes such as CGHS. Its hospitals are clustered in the Delhi NCR region (Noida, Greater Noida, Noida Extension, Faridabad, New Delhi) with additional units at Jhansi-Orchha in Madhya Pradesh and Agra, and a new Gurugram hospital under construction. The key operating numbers management tracks are bed capacity (over 3,200 beds, targeting 5,000 in three years), occupancy (67% in Q3 FY26) and ARPOB, or average revenue per occupied bed per day (about INR 33,700), with FY26 revenue of roughly INR 12,072 million, up 36%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Inpatient (IP) services** — Admitted-patient treatment — surgeries, critical care and complex procedures — which is the main revenue driver, measured through occupancy and ARPOB, with inpatient volumes growing 32% year-on-year in Q2 FY25.
- **Outpatient (OPD) services / consultations** — Walk-in doctor consultations and follow-ups, priced off consultation tariffs (revised about 12% across the three Delhi NCR hospitals in FY24) and growing 16% year-on-year in Q2 FY25; it also feeds patients into inpatient care.
- **Pharmacy, medicines and other services** — Medicines and consumables billed to patients accounted for about 33% of revenue and 'others' a further 26% as of the Q1 FY24 call — a large share of the top line alongside the core treatment fees.
- **Super-specialty / high-value specialties** — The high-end clinical work management pushes hardest — cardiology, renal sciences (nephrology and urology), neurosciences, gastroenterology and organ transplants — contributing around 70% of revenue at the Noida Extension hospital and lifting group ARPOB.
- **Oncology (cancer care)** — A full suite of cancer services including radiation and a PET line at Noida Extension; it grew to roughly 11% of group revenue and 20% of Noida Extension revenue in Q2 FY25 (18% of Noida Extension's super-specialty mix by Q3 FY26), described as a four-fold jump year-on-year.
- **Organ transplants** — Kidney transplants started in December 2022 (50 done within the first months, over 95% of them international patients), with liver transplant approvals also received.
- **Robotic and minimally invasive surgery** — Da Vinci robotic surgery installed across Noida Extension, Greater Noida, Greater Faridabad and Agra (Agra's first robot, 50 robotic surgeries completed), used as a differentiator to attract complex, higher-priced cases.
- **Medical value travel / international patients** — Treating foreign patients sourced through overseas OPD camps (Mauritius, Nigeria, Turkmenistan), delegations from Central Asia and outreach in Africa, the Middle East and Iraq; the company is the exclusive hospital partner for the Noida International Airport at Jewar and plans a 200-bed facility aimed at these patients.
- **Acquisitions and new hospital ramp-ups** — A cluster-based growth engine of buying and turning around hospitals — Jhansi-Orchha (2022), Greater Faridabad (2024), Model Town New Delhi (~INR 160 crore), Faridabad Sector 20 (~INR 91 crore plus INR 100 crore of equipment), Agra (250 beds) and an under-construction Gurugram unit (~INR 100 crore plus INR 100 crore) — with New Delhi and Faridabad Sector 20 together contributing about 11% of Q4 FY26 revenue.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Yatharth Hospital comes out of the three reviews as a fast-growing, sub-scale hospital chain that is taking business from weaker rivals, and it scores 67 out of 100 ("Strong"), built from 68 on business quality, 57 on multibagger fit and 76 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound. The growth is measurable rather than promised: beds have gone from 1,405 to over 3,200 against a 5,000 target, revenue from INR 5,203mn in FY23 to INR 12,072mn in FY26, and the March-2026 quarter grew 47% over the prior year while hospitals that were already open still grew 33%. Two quality checks explain how this is paid for. Almost all the spending is on new capacity rather than repairs — new hospitals in Delhi, Faridabad Sector-20, Agra and Gurugram plus extra beds at existing sites — earning about 23% on capital employed, and the business collects cash from patients 26 days before it has to pay its suppliers, so patients effectively fund the expansion. The source of the new capacity is failing competitors: a bank-auctioned bankrupt hospital in Delhi, an unfinished building in Faridabad, a stalled Gurugram project. The company buys the land and the building outright and runs everything itself, with no franchising, which is why the "forward integrator" pattern fits and why it controls its own pricing and patient experience.

The same traits that create the patterns also create the ceilings. "Market share gainer" is the one pattern rated a strong fit, and it lines up directly with the growth and geographic-expansion checks, where cluster-by-cluster moves out of Greater Noida into Jhansi, Faridabad, Delhi, Agra and Gurugram have ramped ahead of internal plan. "Pricing power" and "brand strength" are only rated likely, and for a revealing reason: average revenue per occupied bed has climbed from INR 28,140 to INR 33,282 over the period, with the Noida Extension unit at INR 47.8k, but management itself credits case mix and payer mix rather than the ability to charge more. That is precisely the "good enough goods" risk, flagged as a watch item — government tariffs and pre-agreed insurer rates are contractually frozen and sit below cash rates, capping what can be earned on roughly two-thirds of revenue. The partial defences are what keep this a good business rather than a protected one: real cost discipline (consumables held at 18-19% of sales, housekeeping and catering kept in-house), genuine time-based barriers (banked prime NCR land, 600-plus doctors, JCI accreditation, teaching status, the exclusive Noida airport partnership), and a solid delivery record — Jhansi-Orchha went from 17% occupancy to 85-86%, Greater Faridabad broke even on schedule. But the review scores competitive advantage, industry structure and return on capital at only about 0.33 out of 2 and management at 0.17, and rules out two patterns outright: this is not a small critical input others must buy at any price, and its "innovation" is purchased kit — robots, PET and radiation lines, imported machines — that any well-funded rival can also buy.

The one serious risk is the flip side of the growth story. About 35% of revenue comes from government schemes whose prices the state sets, not the company: management openly credited a government price revision for both revenue and profit in the December-2025 quarter, and back in November 2024 had to deliberately turn away government patients at Noida to protect the business. That is rated high risk, and it is the same dependency that makes the pricing-power pattern only a partial fit. The offsetting move is already visible — the new Delhi and Faridabad Sector-20 hospitals take 100% of their revenue from cash-paying and privately insured patients from day one, so the fastest-growing part of the company is the part least exposed. Every other risk channel came back low or silent: demand is medically necessary care spread across more than 30 specialties, so it does not follow fashion or a customer's spending cycle; occupancy rose steadily from 51% to 71% with margins staying in a narrow 25-30% band; revenue is spread across many patients, three roughly balanced payer channels and nine-plus hospitals; and nothing surfaced on new entrants or changing patient preferences. Two softer marks deserve attention rather than alarm: the acquisitions score only 1 out of 2 because most are distressed or unfinished assets that still need turning around, and profitability scores 1 as well — 24 rupees of operating profit per 100 of sales, steady but not expanding. In short, the engine is share capture funded by patient cash and cheap distressed assets; the brake is that someone else sets the price on a large slice of the revenue.
