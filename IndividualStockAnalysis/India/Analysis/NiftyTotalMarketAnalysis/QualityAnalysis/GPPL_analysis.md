# Gujarat Pipavav Port (GPPL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Gujarat Pipavav Port Limited runs a commercial seaport at Pipavav on India's Gujarat coast, where it loads, unloads, stores and moves other people's cargo for a fee. Its customers are global shipping lines such as Maersk and the OOCL-COSCO group, fertiliser and LPG importers buying on government or private tenders, car exporters, and since October 2025 ONGC, which uses the port as an offshore supply base for its Arabian Sea oil fields. It earns money on a per-unit tariff basis — roughly Rs. 9,000-10,500 per container (TEU) and Rs. 550-650 per metric tonne of bulk or liquid cargo, plus marine services charged across all cargo types — with tariffs raised about 5% in January 2026. The port is not owned outright: GPPL operates it under a concession from the Gujarat Maritime Board on which it pays a waterfront royalty (currently at a concessional rate offset against its investment), the concession runs out around 2028, and renewal talks were still unresolved as of May 2026. Volumes are heavy-fixed-cost and high-margin, with EBITDA margins of 58-65% and a near-100% dividend payout.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Containers** — Handling import/export containers for shipping lines, running around 165,000-180,000 TEUs a quarter at Rs. 8,000-10,500 per TEU; it is the biggest and most-discussed cargo line but has been the weak spot, down roughly 4-5% a year through FY26 after the Red Sea crisis, a withdrawn Maersk service and US tariffs on textiles hit the services calling at Pipavav, with a new Maersk far-East service (FI2) starting July 2026 expected to add about 1,000 moves a week.
- **Dry Bulk** — Handling loose dry cargo, mostly imported fertiliser (urea and others) bought on government tenders, at roughly 500,000+ tonnes a quarter and Rs. 550-650 per tonne; it grew about 35% in FY26 and was the standout volume story, though management calls it slightly margin-dilutive and lumpy because it depends on when the government opens tenders — coal is a smaller piece management was still deciding what to do with in late 2024.
- **Liquid Bulk** — Handling liquid cargo including LPG at Rs. 550-600 per tonne, running at 1.6-1.65 million tonnes a year against a single jetty that can only hold about 1.7 million — so it is effectively capacity-capped, grew about 8-13% in FY26, and management expects strong growth for several years once the new liquid jetty (targeted for completion around Q3 FY27, weather permitting) opens up room.
- **RoRo** — Roll-on/roll-off handling of cars driven on and off vessels, guided at 150,000-175,000 cars a year back in 2024 and hitting a record 62,000+ cars in a single quarter by Q3 FY26; it is the fastest-growing and most trouble-free line, up about 39-40% in FY26, with a dedicated RoRo staging area due to finish around June 2026.
- **Offshore supply base (ONGC contract)** — A five-year contract effective 1 October 2025 to act as ONGC's second offshore supply base after Nhava, providing marine, storage and related services for its Arabian Sea oil fields; management refuses to disclose the revenue as it is confidential customer information, so the calls give no sense of its size.
- **Marine services** — Charges for vessel-side services that apply across every cargo stream regardless of what is in the hold, and were included alongside containers in the roughly 5% January 2026 tariff increase that management expected to lift total revenue by about 3-4%.
- **Concession and future expansion** — Not a revenue line but the frame around the whole business: GPPL operates under a Gujarat Maritime Board concession expiring around 2028, and has signed a non-binding MOU with GMB for roughly Rs. 17,000 crore of port infrastructure spending over 30 years — new liquid, bulk, container and RoRo jetties plus land development — all of which is explicitly contingent on the concession being extended.

### The story in depth

Gujarat Pipavav earns its keep. Out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹61 is operating profit, and that margin has barely moved — about ±2.5 points a year — which the framework reads as costs under control and, alongside flat margins over many years, as a sign that pricing in this market is rational and new competitors are scarce. Capital in the business (shareholders' funds plus borrowings) threw off a 21% cash return this year, working capital ties up only about 14 days of cash, and sales have compounded at roughly 6% a year with unusual year-to-year consistency. Management scores well on candour: it repeatedly and without being asked strips out one-offs (₹43cr insurance recovery, ₹49.6cr SEIS scrips, ₹18.8cr bank-guarantee cost, ₹4.8cr gratuity), gives like-for-like numbers, and publicly raised its own EBIT guidance from 5-7% to 12-15%. Nearly all the profit is paid out as dividends. On the scorecard this shows up as strong Capital Allocation and Return on Capital, and the business is judged structurally SOUND. The overall grade, though, is Mixed — 44 out of 100 — because business quality (58) is dragged down by a weak multibagger fit (13) and only middling risk safety (56).

The multibagger screen found no pattern it could confirm outright. Eight of the eleven come back as partial fits, and the reason is usually the same: the good half of the story is real, but the other half is missing. The physical asset is genuinely hard to copy — a finite deep-water waterfront, double-stack rail connectivity, a pipeline link and a 30-year, ₹17,000 crore master plan — yet Cost to Replicate stops short of a full fit because what GPPL actually owns is a licensed concession with an end date, not the land itself. Port handling at roughly ₹9,000-10,500 per container is a small slice of cargo value but indispensable, and the west-coast market is a narrow field, which is the Toll Roads shape; the screen holds back because there is no evidence customers cannot go elsewhere. A ~5% tariff increase from January 2026 flowed 3-4% into revenue, supporting Pricing Power — but container volumes fell 4% over the year, which is not what unchallenged pricing power looks like. Management claims share gains in liquids, RoRo and fertiliser, with RoRo up ~39-40% and a record 62,000 cars in a quarter, yet the biggest segment, containers, lost share while the Gujarat region grew 6%. Recurring revenue exists — a five-year ONGC offshore-supply-base contract, storage income, annual escalations — but management explicitly denies take-or-pay contracts. Brand Strength registers only as a numbers-based hint with nothing in the calls behind it.

The most important thing to see is that the same handful of facts produce the strengths, the half-patterns and the risks together. The single deep-water concession is what creates the entry barriers and the steady 61% margin, is what makes the asset hard to replicate — and is also the single biggest risk: the whole business sits on a Gujarat Maritime Board concession expiring around 2028, with renewal terms, royalty rates and set-offs entirely GMB's call, and the ₹17,000 crore capex plan explicitly conditional on that extension. That is the review's one HIGH RISK. Being one port in one place also explains the two negative quality checks — an asset-heavy business turning ₹1 of assets into ₹0.38 of sales, and no geographic expansion at all, since every growth option sits inside the Pipavav footprint. Likewise, the fact that shipping lines rather than cargo owners choose which port a service calls at is what makes the Friendly Middleman pattern half-fit, and simultaneously drives two risks: Stakeholder Concentration (container performance repeatedly explained by one anchor line's service decisions, with related-party revenue from that line going nowhere for five to six years) and the Good Enough Goods watch (undifferentiated capacity priced off the market, with GPPL shrinking in containers while the region grew). Cyclicality is elevated for reasons visible in the record — revenue down 10% in one quarter on the Red Sea crisis, six straight quarters of container decline on US tariffs, then up 32% on a fertiliser tender surge — though the review notes the offsetting point that throughput follows trade flows rather than customers' own capital spending, and that the ₹720 crore liquid jetty and RoRo staging area are being built through the downturn. Nothing in six calls points to a technology or format threat, and the screen found no signal at all on new entrants or shifting preferences. In short: a well-run, cash-generative, honestly reported single asset whose defining feature is also its expiry date.
