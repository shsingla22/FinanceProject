# Carborundum Universal Ltd. (CARBORUNIV) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

Carborundum Universal (CUMI) is an India-based industrial manufacturer that makes grinding and cutting products, industrial ceramics, and the raw abrasive minerals that go into them, selling mainly to other manufacturers rather than to consumers. It earns money by selling physical volumes at a price per unit, so its profits swing with input costs, selling-price realisation and currency moves; management discusses results as three reported segments — Abrasives, Electrominerals and Ceramics — at both standalone (India) and consolidated (group) level. Its end customers are in steel, cement, power, glass, carbon black, solid oxide fuel cells, autos, construction and agriculture. Beyond India it operates through subsidiaries in Russia (Volzhsky Abrasives), Germany (Rhodius, Awuko), South Africa (Foskor Zirconia), plus America and Australia; FY26 consolidated sales were about INR 5,149 crores against standalone INR 3,024 crores, so roughly 40% of group revenue comes from outside the Indian parent.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Abrasives** — Grinding wheels, cutting discs and coated abrasives sold into retail, industrial and precision end-uses; the largest segment at about INR 2,271 crores consolidated in FY26 (INR 1,270 crores standalone), but the lowest-margin one — consolidated PBIT margin was around 10% in Q1 FY25 versus about 17.6% standalone, because the overseas abrasive subsidiaries drag it down.
- **Electrominerals** — Makes the raw minerals — silicon carbide, aluminas and zirconia — that feed abrasives and refractories, sold both internally and to outside customers including a growing export book; about INR 1,632 crores consolidated and INR 906 crores standalone in FY26, and historically the segment most exposed to the Russian operation.
- **Ceramics** — Industrial and engineered ceramic products (including wear-resistant and specialty applications, with subsidiaries in Australia and America); smallest of the three by revenue at about INR 1,268 crores consolidated and INR 1,000 crores standalone in FY26, but consistently the highest-margin segment — standalone PBIT margins in the mid-20s percent.
- **Volzhsky Abrasives (VAW), Russia** — Russian subsidiary producing silicon carbide, refractories and abrasives with a large export share (43% of volume in Q1 FY25); a major profit contributor historically, but sanctions imposed in January 2025 cut it out of the consolidated FY26 numbers and cost roughly INR 87 crores of consolidated profit before tax.
- **Rhodius (Germany)** — Acquired European abrasives maker selling cutting/grinding products into Europe and the Americas, around EUR 60 million of annual sales in FY26; loss-making that year (expected full-year loss of about EUR 4.5 million) after a disruptive warehouse shift and a purchase-price-allocation write-off.
- **AWUKO (Germany)** — Small coated-abrasives subsidiary running roughly EUR 3 million of sales a quarter that never turned profitable; CUMI decided in FY26 to wind it up voluntarily, booking an exceptional charge of about INR 119 crores.
- **Sterling Abrasives (India)** — Domestic abrasives subsidiary whose sales are tied to the agricultural market plus exports; small relative to the group and cited mainly as a swing factor when Indian agro demand slows.
- **Foskor Zirconia (South Africa)** — 51%-held zirconia minerals operation that has not made sustainable profits since 2013; high South African electricity costs and global competition made it unviable, and the board decided in FY26 to stop operations, with a INR 16 crore asset write-down.
- **SEDCO (power generation)** — A power-generating subsidiary mentioned in FY23 as a drag on consolidated profits when gas prices spiked; a side operation rather than a core product line.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Carborundum Universal comes out of these three reviews as a genuinely good business wrapped in a mediocre set of capital decisions, which is why the combined rating lands at 54 out of 100 — "Decent", three stars — built from 58 on business quality, 33 on multibagger fit and 72 on risk safety. The strongest part of the record is the Indian core and the people running it. Competitive advantage scores the highest of any quality area (1.0), backed by standalone returns on capital of roughly 45% in abrasives and 41-55% in ceramics, vertical integration into fused grain, and research that has actually reached customers: treated grains that helped double standalone electromineral exports to a third of segment sales, engineering ceramics for solid-oxide fuel cells up 30% on AI data-centre demand, and a new semiconductor wafer-fab components line. That is why the innovation pattern is the only one of the eleven screened that comes closest to a genuine fit, and why technological innovation registers as a low risk — nobody is displacing this company's products; it is adding new ones. Management scores well for the same reason the culture pattern half-passes: they publish segment-level targets and then mark their own homework in public, including the misses (abrasives profit guided at 4-4.5% and delivered 4.3%, ceramics guided at 13-14% and delivered 9.3%, openly flagged as a shortfall).

The single trait that most shapes this company runs straight through the middle of the analysis, and it works both ways. These are industrial consumables — grinding discs, coated abrasives, refractories, wear ceramics — bought over and over by steel, cement, glass, auto and construction customers. That repeat, functional demand is exactly why fashion risk and customer-concentration risk both score low, and why the products sit somewhere near the "toll road" idea of being critical to how a customer operates. But the same commodity character means the company takes the price it is given rather than setting it. The pricing-power pattern is a clear no-fit and the toll-road and cost-to-replicate patterns only partly hold, for one reason: well-funded Chinese supply keeps resetting the price. Realisations in standalone electrominerals fell 4-6% on cheaper imports, Foskor's realisation dropped 13% under Chinese price pressure and the business was written down and exited, and growth is repeatedly volume-led while prices fall — a negative mark on the pricing, mix and volume check. That is precisely the "good enough goods" risk, rated elevated and described as the clearest and most repeated risk in the file, with cheaper competition attacking both ends of the portfolio. The offsets are real but partial: the protected pockets are the specification-driven niches — fuel-cell ceramics, metallised cylinders, semiconductor components — and the relief so far has come from governments rather than from the company, through EU anti-dumping duties on Chinese product and the coming removal of China's abrasive export rebate. Policy cutting the other way is why government dependency sits on watch: US sanctions on the Russian arm cut rouble sales 35% and cost roughly Rs.87 crore of profit before tax plus Rs.104 crore of provisions.

The weakest link is what has been done with the cash, and it explains the gap between excellent standalone returns and an ordinary consolidated result. Capital allocation is the only negative quality area (-0.4) and return on capital scores a flat zero, because the 2022 acquisition programme has destroyed value: Rhodius, bought on an enterprise value of EUR 55 million with a promise of 12-14% operating margin by FY2026, instead posted a EUR 2.6 million loss for FY26, while AWUKO promised a FY2024 breakeven and is being wound up at a cost of Rs.119 crore. Geographic expansion has gone backwards on the same account — Germany shrinking, South Africa closing, Russia sanctioned — which outweighs solid progress in Australia and the Americas. Investment spending itself is honest growth spending rather than mere upkeep (about Rs.350 crore guided, Rs.309 crore delivered in FY26, including the semiconductor facility), but consolidated return on capital still fell from 19.7% to around 16%, and roughly 187 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Put together, the story is a high-return domestic core, real innovation and honest disclosure, diluted by overseas acquisitions that did not work and by a business model with no ability to raise prices — and layered on top of an elevated cyclical swing that showed up in FY26 itself, with a weak first half from dealer destocking and unseasonal rains followed by a 14-15% second-half rebound helped by GST rationalisation. The overall resilience reading is sound, and the market-share gains are genuine, but consolidated growth of 6.5% shows how much of the good work is being absorbed elsewhere.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.1× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Across 2022-2026 management frames demand through commodity prices and customer capex cycles — base-metal corrections in Aug 2022, EMD price realisation falling 4-6% on import pricing, zircon sand price volatility killing Foskor, and ceramics repeatedly missing guidance because customer 'projects are getting delayed' and shipments await customer inspection; consolidated PBIT margin swung from 13% (Q1FY24) to 7-8% (FY26). The abrasives/refractory consumable base tied to customer operations rather than capex cushions this, and capex was maintained at ~INR 350 crores through the downturn.

> "In the base metals, iron ore has corrected by 49% and aluminium by 37% from their respective peaks" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Consumable/flow-product abrasives and refractories linked to customer operations; countercyclical capex maintained (INR 309-350 crores) and new semiconductor/SOFC capacity commissioned during the weak year*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 2 of the last 11 years with swings of ±13 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 11% and 18% (±2.2 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 17% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 6% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Good Enough Goods — ELEVATED
*"Cheaper rivals that are good enough"*

Rhodius, acquired explicitly as the 'globally best quality' premium disc maker, ran a EUR 2.6 million FY26 loss, and AWUKO was wound up entirely; competitiveness in Europe is now partly leaning on anti-dumping duties and FTA tariff relief rather than on the premium itself.

> "the realisation fell by 13% because of the price pressure from Chinese competition" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Niche, specification-driven pockets remain protected — SOFC and engineered ceramics, metallised cylinders for vacuum interrupters, and semiconductor wafer-fab components — where standalone ceramics still earns ~21-24% PBIT margins*

## What to watch

- Consolidated return on capital: whether it recovers from about 16% back toward the earlier 19.7%, which is the test of whether the growth capex check and the zero score on return on capital were a timing issue or a permanent step down.
- Rhodius: whether it turns from its EUR 2.6 million FY26 loss toward the promised 12-14% operating margin now that AWUKO is being wound up — the merger and acquisition check is the report's most negative single item.
- Prices versus volumes: any sign of growth coming from higher realisations rather than only higher volumes would start to soften the "no pricing power" verdict and the elevated "good enough goods" risk; further realisation falls under Chinese competition would confirm them.
- The ceramics margin gap: closing the shortfall between the 13-14% guided and 9.3% delivered, which management itself flagged — this is the live test of the candor and corporate-culture strengths converting into results.
- The working capital cycle: whether the roughly 187 days of cash tied up between suppliers and customers starts to shorten, since it is the standing drag behind the negative capital allocation score.
- Policy direction: how much the EU anti-dumping duties and China's export rebate removal actually help, against the risk of further sanction-type shocks like the one that cost roughly Rs.87 crore of profit before tax in Russia — the government dependency risk cuts both ways.
