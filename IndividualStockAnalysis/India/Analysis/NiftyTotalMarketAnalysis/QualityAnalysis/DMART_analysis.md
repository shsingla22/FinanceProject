# Avenue Supermarts Ltd. (DMART) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: CONSUMER GOODS*

Avenue Supermarts runs DMart, a chain of large value-retail supermarkets in India that sell everyday household goods — food and staples, non-food FMCG items like home and personal care, and general merchandise and apparel — to mass-market, price-conscious shoppers. It makes money by buying in bulk and selling cheap at high volume through its own physical stores: roughly 35.3 crore bills (customer transactions) in FY25 on standalone revenue of about INR 57,790 crores, at an EBITDA margin near 8% and a PAT margin near 5%. Growth comes mainly from opening more stores — 50 in FY22, 41 in FY24, 50 in FY25, taking retail business area past 15 million square feet — using a 'cluster-based' strategy of adding stores densely in existing markets, mostly on owned rather than leased real estate, with no external borrowings. It operates only in India, across many states, with management calling the North of India its main remaining 'white space', and it supplements the stores with an online grocery arm and small in-store food and grocery-packing businesses.

**The parts of the business:**

- **DMart stores (standalone brick-and-mortar retail)** — The core business: physically operated value supermarkets selling Foods, Non-Foods (FMCG) and General Merchandise & Apparel, which is essentially all of the company's revenue (INR 49,533 crores in FY24, INR 57,790 crores in FY25 standalone), with the non-grocery General Merchandise & Apparel piece running about 22%–23% of the mix versus 27%–28% before COVID.
- **Foods** — Groceries and staples such as agri products and edible oils — the largest and growing share of the sales mix, helped in FY24 by unusually high agri food inflation.
- **Non-Foods (FMCG)** — Everyday branded consumer products other than food, such as home and personal care; a steady but slightly shrinking share of the mix in FY24.
- **General Merchandise & Apparel (GMA)** — Higher-margin discretionary goods and clothing aimed at mass-market buyers; it fell from the old 27%–28% of sales to roughly 22%–23%, was under stress in FY22 as lower-middle-class shoppers postponed purchases, and by FY24 apparel had become the fastest-growing category after internal fixes to leadership, teams and sizing.
- **DMart Ready / Avenue E-Commerce Limited** — The online grocery subsidiary, deliberately expanded slowly — 12 cities in mid-2022, 24 cities by FY25, with the model pivoted from store-pickup-plus-delivery to mainly home delivery; sales grew to about INR 2,900 crores in FY24 (+32%) and about 21% again in FY25, but it still loses money (INR 185 crores loss in FY24, INR 247 crores in FY25).
- **Avenue Food Plaza** — Small ready-to-eat / takeaway fresh-food stalls inside DMart stores, described by management as 'the cherry on the cake' for shoppers — tiny relative to the group (INR 177 crores of sales in FY24) and loss-making because it is still in an expansion phase.
- **Align Retail Trades** — The in-house grocery procurement, processing and packing subsidiary that sells entirely to DMart's own stores; about INR 2,800 crores of sales and INR 33 crores of profit in FY24, growing roughly 19% in FY25.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Avenue Supermarts is, at its core, one idea executed with unusual consistency: cap your own markup, hand every remaining rupee of benefit to the shopper, and make money on how fast the goods move rather than on how much you charge. The quality framework rewards this — a short 29-day cash cycle, capital spending that is genuinely for growth (50 new stores and 2 million square feet in FY25, self-funded, targeting 10-20% store-base expansion a year), and top marks for management on candor, independence and staying out of the limelight. The multibagger screen picks up the same trait from a different angle and calls it a STRONG FIT on Low Price Plus, backed by inventory turning 13.6-14.6 times and returns on capital of 17.8-19.1%, with owned real estate taking rent out of the cost line. Culture scores as a second STRONG FIT for the same reason it scores well on candor: the same team calls apparel weakness an internal issue and says plainly of e-commerce that 'whether it will make money or not we don't know.' Market Share Gainers is a LIKELY FIT on 16-18% sales growth and 8.4-9.9% like-for-like growth in older stores, sitting alongside two supportive structural checks — abundant weak competitors in unorganised kirana and roadside trade, and genuine long-run headroom in value retail penetration.

The same design choices that create the patterns also create the ceilings, and the record is explicit about it. Because the model deliberately forgoes pricing power, the company keeps only ₹15 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs — which is why Intangible benefits scores negative, why Pricing Power is a NO FIT (gross margin down 37 basis points in FY24 with further mix-led erosion in FY25, FMCG described as 'very, very competitive on pricing'), and why Brand Strength is only PARTIAL: customers recognise 'brand DMART' but there is no advertising spend and no price premium behind it. Owned property cuts the same way. It is the one concrete, hard-to-replicate asset the Cost to Replicate pattern can point to, and the reason management says 'when you're in the ownership model, you don't get too worried' about sales per square foot — yet it is also why Asset turn scores poorly, with fixed asset turnover drifting from 3.6 to 3.4 and returns on capital easing from 19.1% to 17.8% as the store base grows. Innovation Dominance is a NO FIT by choice: management locates innovation with its suppliers, saying D2C brands 'are doing a significantly better job,' and positions itself as a neutral platform. That neutrality is what makes Stakeholder Concentration a LOW risk — no single vendor holds leverage — but it also means the company is not the source of the product excitement in its own aisles.

The risk review is the most reassuring of the three inputs (safety scored 82, versus 61 on business quality and 63 on pattern fit), with resilience judged SOUND and four of eight channels rated LOW — cyclicality, stakeholder concentration, fashion risk and good-enough goods. Notably, the model's biggest quality weakness is a risk strength: because DMart caps its own markup and welcomes commoditisation, it is itself the good-enough attacker rather than a premium incumbent waiting to be undercut, and its deliberately 'basic, basic, basic' apparel policy removes trend exposure. The pressure sits in one place. Quick commerce is rated ELEVATED under New Entrants and shadows two more channels as WATCH — Technological Innovation and Shifting Consumer Preferences — because a dark-store network is a different fulfilment model that large-box owned property cannot easily copy, management concedes it costs 1-1.5% of same-store sales growth in affected cities, and affluent metro shoppers increasingly want groceries delivered. The offsets named in the record are the company's own DMart Ready channel across 24 cities, the cost advantage of owned stores, and small towns where quick commerce is uneconomic. Netting it out, the combined rating of 67 — 45% weight on quality, 30% on pattern fit, 25% on risk safety — lands at Strong, four stars: a well-run, honestly managed, structurally advantaged compounder whose returns are moderating at the edges and whose one genuine competitive threat is being met, not ignored.

The 1-1.5% same-store sales growth drag from quick commerce (New Entrants, ELEVATED): if that figure widens or spreads beyond affected cities, the Market Share Gainers pattern weakens at its foundation.

Fixed asset turnover and returns on capital (Asset turn check): the drift from 3.6 to 3.4 and 19.1% to 17.8% is tolerable at current growth rates, but continued decline would undercut the turns-based logic of the Low Price Plus fit.

Gross margin mix (Pricing Power, NO FIT): management has already ruled out recovery in the general merchandise and apparel mix — further erosion below the ₹15-per-₹100 level would squeeze a model that has no pricing lever to pull.

DMart Ready's economics (Shifting Consumer Preferences, WATCH): management itself says it does not know whether e-commerce will make money; this is the designated answer to the delivery shift, so evidence either way matters most here.

Candor and Disciplined stewards (both scored 2): zero external debt, no M&A, no land banking and continued willingness to own bad news on calls — any drift toward leverage, acquisitions or guidance-managing would remove the strongest single support in the rating.

### Low Price Plus — STRONG FIT
*"Cheapest — and still the best"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence and the numbers agree (1 of 1 check computable supporting).

**What the calls show:** Every call restates the same value-retail formula: fix a low margin, pass all remaining benefit to price, and earn returns via turns (inventory turnover 13.6–14.6x, fixed-asset turns 3.4–3.6, ROCE 17.8–19.1%) plus owned real estate that removes rent from the cost base; scale and cluster density are explicitly the source of operating leverage.

> "We have a very clear-cut principle about how much margin we should retain. Everything else goes to the customer." — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Keeps ₹15 of every ₹100 of sales after input costs and turns its assets 2.3× a year with 20% returns on capital — thin margins made up on volume, the low-price-winner profile.

### Corporate Culture — STRONG FIT
*"A culture that compounds"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** Across 2022–2025 the same team (Noronha, Baheti, Deb) owns bad news on the calls — apparel weakness called an 'internal issue', DMart Ready 'struggling' with the 24-hour promise, e-commerce 'whether it will make money or not we don't know' — while delivering what was promised (guided 40–45 stores, delivered 41 then 50), refusing forward guidance, staying debt-free, and running a multi-year planned CEO succession to Asawa.

> "we're trying to at least achieve a 24-hour promise, but we are struggling with that to be very honest" — *management, on an earnings call*

### Market Share Gainers — LIKELY FIT
*"Winning customers from rivals"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits and the only computable check supports the pattern.

**What the calls show:** Sustained 16–18% sales growth, 8.4–9.9% LFL on 24-month-plus stores and a stated 10–15% annual store-count expansion imply persistent share gain, and management reports resilience against quick commerce, but no explicit market-share figures versus named competitors are ever disclosed.

> "in spite of the competitive context in the offline and the online space, our business has been resilient" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Sales compounding at 24% while margins held or improved (8% → 8%) — growth that was WON, not bought with price cuts.

### Brand Strength — PARTIAL
*"The name itself sells"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (0 of 2 checks supporting).

**What the calls show:** Management observes customers choosing across channels on the strength of 'brand DMART' and a 20-year format that 'works everywhere' from Punjab to Tamil Nadu, but there is no A&P spend, no premium pricing, and no heritage/consistency narrative — the brand promise is price, not brand equity.

> "We're seeing that people are looking at us as brand DMART, and they're buying depending on the channel" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ Operating margin of 8% — too thin for a protected franchise.

### Forward Integrators — PARTIAL
*"Owns the shop, not just the product"*
