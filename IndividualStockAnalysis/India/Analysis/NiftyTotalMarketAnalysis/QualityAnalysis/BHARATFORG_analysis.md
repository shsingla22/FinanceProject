# Bharat Forge Ltd. (BHARATFORG) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: INDUSTRIAL MANUFACTURING*

Bharat Forge is an India-based engineering manufacturer that forges, casts and machines metal parts — steel and aluminium — and increasingly sells finished systems rather than just components. Its parts go into commercial trucks (especially US Class 8 and Indian CVs), passenger cars, and a range of industrial end-markets such as oil and gas, aerospace, construction and mining equipment, renewable energy and hydraulics, sold mostly to large global OEMs and Tier-1 customers; alongside this it has built a defence business selling artillery guns, armoured vehicles, small arms, naval solutions and unmanned systems, largely on government orders. It makes money by winning long-term component programmes and order books and then supplying at volume, with the high-margin Indian operations (FY26 standalone revenue about INR 8,396 crores at roughly 27.5% EBITDA margin) doing far better than the thin-margin overseas plants. Operations span India (Pune/Mundhwa and other Indian units), Europe (a German steel forging business, CDP Bharat Forge, now being restructured) and the United States (an aluminium forging plant), with FY26 consolidated revenue of about INR 16,812 crores and EBITDA of INR 2,921 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Standalone / India forging and components business (Components Division)** — The core Indian forging and machining operation making automotive and industrial parts for domestic customers and for export; it is the profit engine of the group, roughly INR 8,396 crores of revenue in FY26 at about 27.5% EBITDA margin versus low-single-digit margins overseas.
- **Automotive (commercial vehicle and passenger vehicle components)** — Parts for heavy trucks — notably North American Class 7/8 — and for passenger cars, including a record quarter of nearly INR 200 crores of car-component exports in FY23; it is the most cyclical piece, with North American truck revenue down 51% year-on-year in Q3 FY26 before the market bottomed out.
- **Industrial / non-automotive exports (oil and gas, construction, mining, renewables)** — Non-auto forged and machined products sold to industrial customers worldwide; management repeatedly points to it as the diversifier that offsets auto weakness, and it grew 11% in Q3 FY26 while auto fell 13%.
- **Aerospace** — Components for jet engine structures and landing systems supplied to global aircraft OEMs under long-term programmes; it went from 2% to over 10% of industrial export revenue between FY22 and FY23 and by Q4 FY26 was about 26% of non-auto exports, the second-largest contributor, with strong growth expected as new capacity comes online.
- **Defence** — Artillery (including the ATAGS gun co-developed with DRDO), military vehicles, CQB carbines and small arms, naval solutions, drones and unmanned sea and air platforms, plus MRO and consumables; the order book was close to INR 11,000 crores as of FY26 with INR 2,816 crores of new defence orders won that year and management guiding to 30-40%+ growth in FY27.
- **JSA (JS Auto) — castings business** — An Indian castings company acquired in mid-2022 for an enterprise value of INR 490 crores that supplies construction, mining, renewable-energy and hydraulics customers; it crossed the INR 1,000 crore sales mark, grew revenue 22% and EBITDA 39% in Q3 FY26, and Premji Invest took a 23% stake at a INR 1,300 crore valuation — three-and-a-half to four times the purchase price.
- **K Drive** — A recently acquired Indian business whose top line has been muted but whose EBITDA margin improved from 3% to about 5% in Q3 FY26; it won roughly INR 500 crores of new OEM business in FY26 that starts flowing from the following year.
- **European operations (CDP Bharat Forge — German steel forging)** — The European steel forging plants serving mostly European auto and industrial customers; they run at low utilisation (60-65%) and thin margins (FY26 revenue INR 3,865 crores at 4% EBITDA) and are now being formally restructured, a process expected to finish by end of the next calendar year.
- **US aluminium forging operations** — A newer American plant making specialty aluminium chassis components for EV, hybrid and high-end passenger car, pickup and SUV programmes; it started up in 2022 with losses of over USD 1 million a month, and by FY26 had turned to INR 1,534 crores of revenue and INR 54 crores of EBITDA (3.5% margin), though US aluminium tariffs are hurting demand and profitability.
- **E-mobility (including Tork Motors)** — Electric-vehicle components, subsystems and powertrains plus a stake of over INR 150 crores in electric two-wheeler maker Tork Motors; management impaired the Tork investment in FY25 after funding fell through and wrote off further e-mobility investments in FY26 as global EV adoption took a different path.
- **Fortuna Engineering (30% stake, machining)** — A Nashik-based machining company in which Bharat Forge bought a 30% stake in May 2026; it machines connecting rods and other high-value parts, letting Bharat Forge feed its own forgings into their machining lines.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Bharat Forge earns its keep on the customer side of the business. The quality work gives full marks to what customers get: buyers choose it for proven reliability rather than price, credibility and deliverability are named as the winning factor in passenger-vehicle exports, and defence products are reported to perform without complaint. That reputation is not accidental — it was built slowly, through years of customer qualification, which is why aerospace went from 2% to 26% of industrial exports and why the artillery gun co-developed with DRDO has finally turned into production orders. The same patience shows up in the numbers: out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹17 is operating profit, and that figure has moved only about three points a year over a long stretch, which the framework reads as costs under control, rational pricing and few new competitors bothering to enter. Growth checks score at the top too — the company is taking share in a shrinking domestic commercial-vehicle market, entering export geographies it never supplied, and pointing new capacity at end-markets that are expanding for structural reasons: European defence budgets going from €350bn to €800bn, India's budget up 21%, aerospace localisation and data-centre components.

The multibagger screen looks at those same facts and finds them real but incomplete — and that is the crux of the story. Eleven patterns were tested; not one scored a clear fit. Seven came back partial and four came back as no fit, which is why the multibagger component of the rating contributes zero to the score of 41 out of 100. The reasons are instructive rather than dismissive. Market-share gains are documented repeatedly, but they sit alongside a defence and aerospace business still small enough to be described as a small-base ramp. Innovation output is genuine — nine artillery platforms, small arms, naval and unmanned systems, aluminium light-weighting, jet-engine structures — but research spending is never disclosed, and the electric-mobility bet went the other way. Most tellingly, pricing power lands only partial and the toll-road pattern fails outright, because forgings are a negotiated, visible share of what the customer pays: the company spent years seeking price repair from European customers and absorbed tariff costs of ₹31 crore in one quarter and ₹12 crore in the next rather than passing them on. It defends margins against inflation; it does not set prices. The one place the barriers are unambiguous — multi-year approvals, sole-source export positions, safety-critical aerospace and defence sign-offs — is also the smallest part of the business, while the core forging operation is more replicable.

Those same qualification barriers are what make the risk review call cheap-substitute pressure and fashion risk low, and the overall resilience sound. The trouble is that the two traits driving the best quality scores are the two traits driving the worst risk verdicts. The cyclical-bottom check scores full marks precisely because North American truck revenue fell 51% and orders are only now turning up — and cyclicality is rated high risk for the same reason, with standalone revenue down 5% in FY26 and swings visible across US trucks, oil and gas, European passenger vehicles and wind castings. Likewise, defence and the expanding government budgets that lift the structural-growth score are exactly why government dependency is high risk: the customer is the state, the artillery order slipped on timing that management said is not up to them, and an order book of roughly ₹11,000 crore depends on procurement reform and government-to-government funding. Two further items sit on watch: the powertrain shift, where money went into electric mobility and was written off when adoption did not follow, and a reliance on a few relationships, with the US aluminium plant serving passenger vehicles alone and the US truck business resting on three large customers. Underneath it all, the weakest quality scores are financial rather than commercial — capital allocation and return on capital are the lowest-scoring areas, about 136 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, sales have compounded at only about 7% a year in lumpy fashion, and heavy growth spending on US aluminium, Baramati and up to ₹3,000 crore in Odisha is producing returns held back by loss-making US and European capacity even as overall returns inch to 18.4%. A good business, honestly run, waiting on a cycle and a government — hence the mixed verdict.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across the 16 calls demand is repeatedly described in cycle terms — US Class 7/8 truck orders swinging, oil & gas troughing and recovering, European PV weakness, wind-sector slowdown hitting castings — culminating in FY26 standalone revenue down 5% with North American truck revenue down 51% year-on-year on destocking, then management calling a bottom and guiding ~25% India growth. Steel and energy input costs add a second cyclical layer (European energy up 5x in 2022, energy the 'biggest issue on cost' in 2026).

> "North American truck market revenues are down 51% as compared to quarter three of last year" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Defense MRO/spares and consumables are operations-linked recurring revenue ('constant MRO and support income'); countercyclical investment — capex, JSA/K Drive/Fortuna M&A and a 2,000 crore raise while end markets were weak; strong balance sheet (net debt/equity 0.15–0.18) held deliberately to 'withstand shocks'*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 5 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 13% and 21% (±3.0 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 10% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 14%).

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

## What to watch
