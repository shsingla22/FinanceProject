# Inox India (INOXINDIA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

INOX India (which trades under the brand INOXCVA) designs, manufactures, installs and services cryogenic equipment — the tanks, cylinders, vacuum-jacketed piping and turnkey systems used to store, transport and dispense gases kept at extremely low temperatures, such as oxygen, nitrogen, argon, CO2, hydrogen, helium and LNG. It sells mostly business-to-business: industrial gas companies, LNG distributors and fuel-station operators, truck and bus OEMs, shipyards and marine customers, aerospace and space agencies, scientific research bodies like ITER in France and ISRO, and global breweries. Money comes from making and selling equipment ranging from 1-litre containers to 1 million-litre storage tanks, plus large engineered turnkey projects and installation work, with revenue recognised against an order backlog (Rs.1,043 crore as of 9MFY24). It manufactures at four plants in India (Vadodara-area sites including Savli and Kalol), with part-manufacturing and service in Brazil and service distribution into the Netherlands, and has shipped to over 100 countries — the US, Europe, Saudi Arabia, Brazil, Korea, UAE and Australia being key markets, with exports roughly half the order book. FY26 revenue was Rs.1,632 crore at a 23.8% EBITDA margin; the company describes itself as debt-free and net cash surplus with high return ratios (ROCE 37%, ROE 28% in FY23).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Industrial Gas Solutions (IG)** — The largest segment — about 65% of income in 9MFY24 and still called the biggest revenue contributor in FY26 — making cryogenic storage tanks, transport tanks and semi-trailers, IMO/ISO containers, microbulk units, vaporizers, liquid cylinders (the 'Cryoseal' brand of liquid nitrogen containers), helium dewars, cryo-bio tanks and disposable cylinders, with milestones like 2 million+ disposable cylinders dispatched to the US in FY26, 300+ transport tanks in a year, and a ~Rs.200 crore aerospace order from a US private space company.
- **LNG Solutions** — About 29% of income in 9MFY24, covering LNG storage tanks and regasification systems, LNG semi-trailers (250+ on Indian roads with 85%+ market share), LCNG/LNG fuelling stations, marine fuel gas systems, vehicle-mounted LNG fuel tanks for trucks, buses and locomotives, and mini-LNG terminals — with recent wins including Cochin Shipyard marine fuel tanks, European marine tanks, and 1,500 cubic metre tanks for a Bahamas mini-LNG terminal.
- **Cryo Scientific Division (CSD)** — The smallest but most technically specialised part (~4% of revenue in 9MFY24), doing turnkey cryogenic systems for research and defence — MRI cryostats (including India's first indigenous 4K helium cryostat), satellite and launch-pad facilities and cryogenic propulsion work for ISRO, a LOX tank for submarine applications, and repeat orders for cryo-lines, cryostat panels and shielding at the ITER fusion project in France.
- **Beverage Keg business** — A newer segment that appears in the later calls, supplying stainless-steel beer kegs to global brewers — dispatches up 31% in FY26, with approvals and live orders from Heineken, AB InBev and Molson Coors, which management says together represent over 40% of global beer market volumes.
- **Non-cryogenic equipment and turnkey projects** — Mentioned alongside the main divisions — the company also manufactures specialised non-cryogenic equipment and executes large turnkey projects including design, installation and commissioning, rather than only shipping standalone products.

### The story in depth

Inox India comes through the three reviews as a genuinely high-quality engineering business that earns its 73-out-of-100 "Strong" rating (45% business quality at 79, 30% pattern fit at 71, 25% risk safety at 66). It makes about ₹22 of operating profit on every ₹100 of sales, and that figure has barely moved — roughly plus or minus 0.9 points a year — which the quality framework reads as costs being under control and few new competitors disturbing prices. It carries no debt at all across the whole period, holds net cash of ₹257-261 crore, and pays for its own expansion out of the money the business generates: growth spending of ₹40-50 crore a year against only ₹15-20 crore of upkeep, a ₹100 crore new plant at Savli that produced over ₹200 crore of revenue in its first year, and a new large-tank facility at Kandla taken on a 30-year land lease rather than bought outright. Behind all of that sits patient technical work — hydrogen since 1999, ISRO's second launch pad since 1999, repeat orders on the ITER fusion project, and a decade-long effort to build India's first home-grown 4K helium MRI cryostat, now installed at AIIMS. Its end markets are growing for structural reasons rather than cyclical ones: LNG replacing diesel in heavy vehicles, green hydrogen, semiconductors, space and defence, and now cooling for data centres.

The multibagger screen picks up the same traits and turns three of them into strong matches. Innovation Dominance is confirmed because named new products with real revenue show up in every single year of the record — three cryogenic patents and a hydrogen trailer tank, then the MRI cryostat, Helium Dewar and second-generation LNG fuel tank, then ultra-high-purity ammonia tanks and a data-centre cooling product. Market Share Gainers is confirmed by hard numbers: over 85% of India's LNG semi-trailers, 69% of LNG fuelling stations, 73% of satellite stations, the only Indian maker of LNG fuel tanks, and brewery approvals that widened from AB InBev to Heineken and Molson Coors, together covering more than 40% of global beer volume. Corporate Culture is confirmed because management does what it says — it guided 18-20% revenue growth and 22-24% margins for the year and delivered 21.2% growth at 23.8% margins. Two more patterns are likely rather than certain: the certification maze (IATF 16949, PESO, DOT, PNGRB, a 98% AB InBev audit score, repeat ITER qualification) works like a toll gate that every rival must pay to pass, and the 30-year heritage and awards give it a real name in its field, though in a business of tenders and approvals there is no consumer-style brand to lean on. The remaining six patterns are only partial, and the reason matters: pricing is protected rather than powerful, because large contracts carry formulas that simply pass raw-material inflation through, and one quarter's gross margin actually slipped, with management calling swings of about 3% normal. Its cost edge is India-versus-China arbitrage rather than a deliberate low-cost system, and nowhere in ten management calls is service or maintenance income, renewals or installed-base revenue ever quantified — the conversation is entirely about the order book and getting projects built.

The risk review is where those same qualities show their other face, which is why resilience reads as STRAINED even with a debt-free balance sheet. The very wins that prove the market-share pattern are the ones that create the biggest risk: the first IMO container order in Australia is described by management as going head-to-head with Chinese manufacturers, and holding US disposable-cylinder share came despite tariffs. That standard end of the range — disposable cylinders, plain IMO containers, plain kegs — is what makes "Good Enough Goods" the one elevated risk, and it feeds the separate New Entrants concern for the same reason: where the product is standard, a structurally cheaper rival can take it. The company's answer is the same answer the quality checks praise, namely pushing into complex made-to-order work (aerospace tanks, marine LNG, ITER, submarine oxygen tanks, the very large tanks planned at Kandla), so the defence and the vulnerability are two ends of one product range. Similarly, the patience that scores well under long-term thinking is also what creates government dependency, because those same long-gestation programmes — the 1,000-LNG-station plan, PSU tenders, ISRO and lunar project enquiries, ITER, the government-backed MRI cryostat, Railways and state bus conversions — are gated by policy decisions the company does not control; the offset is that 63% of the order book is exports across more than 100 countries to private buyers. Two further strains are financial rather than strategic: a single roughly ₹200 crore aerospace order visibly swung one quarter, and named individual relationships such as the Adani Total preferred-partner arrangement (7 of 25 stations commissioned) keep driving results, while about 238 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. That long cash cycle is the single worst mark in the whole quality framework and explains why capital allocation (0.5) and return on capital (0.67) score far below competitive advantage, industry structure and management (all 1.5) — the business is excellent at earning profit and slow at converting it into cash.
