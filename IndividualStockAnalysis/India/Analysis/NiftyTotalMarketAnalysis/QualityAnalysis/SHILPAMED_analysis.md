# Shilpa Medicare (SHILPAMED) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Shilpa Medicare is an Indian pharmaceutical company, headquartered in Raichur, Karnataka, that develops and manufactures the active drug ingredients (APIs) and the finished medicines built from them, with a long-standing specialty in cancer (oncology) drugs. It sells to other pharma companies — both large generic players and small innovator biotechs — and increasingly acts as a contract development and manufacturing partner (CDMO), earning milestone payments and long-term supply contracts for new drugs it helps invent, such as the kidney-disease drug OLC it developed end-to-end for US-listed Unicycive Therapeutics. Revenue also comes from its own differentiated finished products (complex injectables, 505(b)(2) formulations, a transdermal patch, and the newly launched NorUDCA liver drug in India) plus an emerging biologics arm making biosimilars and antibody-drug conjugates. Its markets are global — the US, Europe, India and other regions — and in FY26 it reported revenue of INR 1,549 crores, up 18%, with EBITDA of INR 445 crores (29% margin) and gross margins around 70%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **API (active pharmaceutical ingredients)** — The oldest and core business, making the drug substances themselves — around 40 oncology and 18 non-oncology APIs with 212 DMF regulatory filings across the US, Europe, Japan, China and other markets, and management says oncology is the mature, established piece it is now trying to grow beyond.
- **Specialty CDMO** — Contract development and manufacturing for other companies' new drugs, restarted around 2021 and now the most talked-about growth engine, with several US new-chemical-entity programmes running (one commercialized, one in Phase III, plus the Unicycive OLC deal carrying a US$10 million milestone payment, a four-year binding supply agreement and a customer-funded dedicated manufacturing block).
- **Peptides** — A newer, high-entry-barrier API niche with its own dedicated R&D and production block, now including generic and CDMO work on Semaglutide (the GLP-1 blockbuster) in both injectable and oral forms, with a large-scale peptide plant being built for completion in FY27.
- **Polymers** — A small but strategically prized line making specialized polymers used as drug carriers or as high-energy materials — Shilpa says it was the first company qualified by DRDO to supply fully indigenous high-energy polymers to the Government of India — described as small today with room to grow.
- **Intermediates** — In-house manufacture of chemical building blocks that India currently imports almost entirely from China, pursued as a 'China plus one' import-substitution play to cut dependency and improve API margins.
- **Formulations (finished dosage products)** — The finished-medicine business selling complex, differentiated products rather than plain generics — three approved 505(b)(2) products in the US, Nilotinib in Europe, a Rotigotine transdermal patch, complex injectables like long-acting Ondansetron and Abraxane, and its first new chemical entity NorUDCA, launched in India in FY26 and described as exceeding expectations; an earlier US import alert on its SEZ plant forced it to shift some products to third-party approved sites.
- **Biologics** — The newest and most investment-heavy division, developing biosimilars (Aflibercept for eye disease, Nivolumab), novel biological entities including recombinant human albumin, and antibody-drug conjugates — with what management calls India's first integrated ADC facility covering payload, linker and conjugation, plus five active biologics CDMO programmes for partners such as Alveolus Bio and mAbTree.

### The story in depth

Shilpa Medicare is, at its core, a research-led maker of hard-to-copy medicines, and that single fact explains most of what is good about it. The quality work gives its top marks to technology (complex oncology ingredients, peptides including semaglutide, oral films and skin patches, biosimilars, and an integrated facility for antibody-drug conjugates), to end markets that are expanding on their own (oncology drugs losing patent protection out to 2032, GLP-1 peptides, biosimilars, complex injectables), and to a management team that kept pushing programmes announced back in 2022 through a US import alert and years of losses until they became approvals and launches. The pattern screen agrees emphatically: Innovation Dominance is the one clear, strong fit, because named products now arrive every year and are paid for by gross margins of 68-71% — the same margins the quality work credits as evidence that customers willingly pay up for complexity. Growth and management both score well above average as a result.

The trouble is that the very model producing those wins also consumes cash and leans on other people. Building peptide, ADC, oncology and albumin blocks is expensive — ₹361 crore of growth spending in FY26 — and the balance sheet shows it: every ₹1 of assets generates only ₹0.42 of sales, about 301 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and there is ₹613 crore of net debt against ₹1,549 crore of revenue. Capital allocation and return on capital both score zero, which is why the overall rating lands at only 56 out of 100, or Decent, and why resilience is judged strained despite the technology strengths. The same building programme is what the Technological Innovation risk flags: the oldest and biggest part of the business is small-molecule oncology ingredients, while the science the company keeps talking about has moved to biologics and ADCs, so the spending is not optional. The company also reaches patients through partners rather than directly — three Indian pharma companies detail its new NorUDCA launch, Amneal sells in the US, tender partners in Europe. That routing is why Friendly Middleman and Brand Strength register as likely fits and why Toll Roads and Recurring Revenue register partially (the Unicycive arrangement is a four-plus-four-year exclusive supply deal with a customer-funded ₹ block and $10 million of milestones). It is also, in the same breath, why Stakeholder Concentration is the single elevated risk: results depend on a handful of named relationships, and the Unicycive approval already slipped from mid-2025. Forward Integrators is a clear non-fit for exactly this reason — the company deliberately moves away from the end customer, not toward it.

On price, the record is honest and less flattering. Pricing Power and Low Price Plus are both non-fits, and the reason is the ordinary generic ingredients business sitting underneath the exciting one: falling selling prices cut gross margin from 68% to 61% in FY23, non-captive ingredient revenue has been roughly flat at ₹183-186 crore, and Europe is described by management itself as very tender-driven. That single description drives three separate risk flags at once — Good Enough Goods, Government Dependency (tender timing explains the quarter-to-quarter swings in rest-of-world sales), and New Entrants, where azacitidine was already abandoned in the US and generic competition to Nilotinib, the largest European product, is expected in FY27 with the real value hit in FY28. It also caps the Market Share Gainers pattern at partial: the gains are real but borrowed and uneven, with the US partner reaching 35% share in Pemetrexed and European formulation revenue more than doubling past ₹200 crore, set against US sales stuck near ₹28 crore a quarter and rest-of-world shrinking. Operating margin of 28% swinging about 4.6 points a year fits that picture of key costs sitting outside management's control. Corporate Culture likewise scores only partial: the family ownership, candid disclosure and internally funded expansion are genuine, but several 2022 promises arrived years late. The honest read is a company with a rare technical edge and a genuinely growing market, whose returns and cash conversion have not yet caught up — improving, with adjusted return on capital employed up from about 4% in FY23 to 17.4% in FY26, but from a low base.
