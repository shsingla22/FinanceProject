# TBO Tek (TBOTEK) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

TBO Tek runs a business-to-business online travel platform: it does not sell holidays to travellers, but instead gives travel agents, tour operators, online travel agencies, corporate travel firms and independent travel advisors one website where they can search and book travel supply from around the world. On the supply side it aggregates airlines, hotels, car rentals, transfers, sightseeing and cruise operators, and it handles the things that make cross-border bookings hard — finding supply, providing trust between two small firms that have never dealt with each other, settling payments in over 55 currencies, and offering after-sales service across time zones and 16-plus languages. It earns a cut on every transaction that flows through the platform: commission from airlines, and a mark-up on wholesale ('net') hotel rates, which is the far more profitable side — hotels and ancillaries produced about 84% of gross profit in FY25 versus roughly 13% from air. The business is heavily international, spanning more than 150 countries with only about a quarter of gross profit from India (76% came from outside India as of FY24), and management describes itself as one of the top four B2B travel distribution platforms globally, with FY25 gross transaction value crossing Rs. 30,000 crore, revenue of Rs. 1,737 crore and adjusted EBITDA of Rs. 329 crore.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Hotels and ancillaries ("hotels plus" — non-air)** — Wholesale hotel rooms plus add-ons like sightseeing, car rentals and transfers, bought at net rates and marked up; this is the core engine, at 59% of GTV and about 84% of gross profit in FY25, and management deliberately anchors growth here because hotel supply and demand are so fragmented that agents need an aggregator.
- **Air / airline ticketing** — Flight bookings on which TBO earns a commission per ticket; it is a large share of volume but only around 13% of gross profit, was called out as a weak spot in FY25, and recovered to 16% year-on-year organic growth by Q3 FY26.
- **International business** — Bookings by travel agents based outside India selling worldwide destinations — the bulk of the company, growing far faster than the group (19% GTV growth in Q4 FY25, 43% for the full year) and where most of the hotel business sits; the Middle East is the single largest source market and Israel a top-seven country.
- **India business** — The home-market agent network, discussed separately by the co-founder; it is the smaller side of the group, with roughly 24% of gross profit coming from India-based agents as of FY24.
- **Classic Vacations (North America)** — A luxury travel business acquired and consolidated from Q3 FY26 to crack the US market; it books at much higher headline take rates (about 25%, though 12.4% of that is commission passed back to travel advisors), recognises hotel revenue at check-in rather than booking, converts gross profit to EBITDA less efficiently than the core (19.6% vs 25.3%), and is roughly halfway through a platform integration due to finish by end of calendar 2026.
- **Voya (AI-first luxury tool)** — A newly built AI product that lets travel advisors reach ultra-luxury supply, assemble complex multi-stop itineraries and close them with clients — described as an adjacency rather than a pivot, and still early with no revenue disclosed.
- **Buyer and supply network development** — The feet-on-street sales teams that sign up travel agencies and onboard hotel supply — not a revenue line but the main spending lever, with ~60 salespeople added globally in Q4 FY25, 15 new markets opened including Australia, France and Germany, and roughly 49,000 active agencies and 70,000 active bookers at FY25.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

TBO Tek runs a two-sided marketplace whose real product is certainty: it stands between very small travel suppliers and very small travel agents who have never dealt with each other and underwrites the trust, payment and service between them. The quality work scores its network effects, its assurance benefit and its convenience benefit at the top of the scale, and the pattern screen backs this with three strong fits. 'Friendly Middleman' fits because the buyer is always the travel agent or advisor while the traveller is the one who pays, and TBO sells only to the trade through feet-on-street teams in 69 Indian cities and 25 European countries plus a platinum desk for high-touch agents. 'Market Share Gainers' fits because the share gain is actually quantified and repeated — hotel volumes growing about 35% against listed peers HBX and Webbeds, wallet share taken in the UAE, 15 new markets added in FY25, and management pinning recent outperformance directly on share capture — all inside an end market that is itself compounding 5-6% a year toward roughly $2.7 trillion by 2027. 'Corporate Culture' fits because both co-founders serve as Joint Managing Directors, the CFO has been stable since FY24, the Jumbonline integration landed a full year early, and management keeps a public list of its own misses: the airline underperformance, foreign exchange losses, being too slow to expand abroad, and failed attempts in North America. That candour is scored at the top of the quality framework as well, so it is the same trait showing up twice.

The traits that create the strengths also create most of the risks. Selling only to travel agents is what makes the Friendly Middleman pattern work, and it is also exactly what the 'Shifting Consumer Preferences' risk is about: the entire demand base rests on travellers continuing to book through offline agencies, a habit that migrates toward direct and online booking over time. Management's answer is to push up-market into luxury and complex connected itineraries, the kind of trip least easily served by self-service booking, and to build its own AI-first advisor tool, Voya. The same up-market push is the answer to two other watch-level risks. 'Good Enough Goods' is live because agents can buy the identical hotel or airline seat from HBX, WebBeds and others, so price becomes the lever — and the record shows that lever being pulled against TBO, with the airline book earning only about a 2.5% take rate, management conceding that monetising air 'has been a struggle', and incentives being handed to buyers to buy volume growth. That is also why the 'Toll Roads' pattern is scored as no fit at all — there is nothing anchoring the position that would let TBO simply charge for access — and why 'Pricing Power' is only a likely fit rather than a strong one: the take rate did climb from 4.77% to 5.25% to 6.04%, but management attributes that to more hotel business in the mix rather than to raising prices, and says of the Classic Vacations business that it is not trying to optimise margins. Meanwhile the moat that does exist — around 49,000 active agencies, roughly 70,000 bookers, 150 countries, 55 currencies, 16 languages and local sales staff in dozens of countries — is judged real but only partial, because management itself points to at least three comparable players of scale. That is the same reason 'New Entrants' sits on watch: the named competitors are incumbents rather than anything structurally new, but AI-native booking agents were the live question on the recent calls, and the airline segment already shows what happens when a competitive answer is needed.

The weakest part of the story is what happens to the cash. On the headline measure, every ₹100 of capital in the business produced ₹-1 of operating cash this year, a -1% cash return, and about 724 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. This connects straight to the pattern screen: 'Recurring Revenue' is only a partial fit because there is no licence, subscription or maintenance stream at all — the whole revenue line is a transaction take rate — and the customer-funded negative working capital that management highlighted back in FY24 has weakened as receivables built up. 'Low Price Plus' is likewise partial: the model is genuinely low-margin and high-turn, with gross profit at only about 3.5% of gross transaction value against a 45% return on equity and 39% return on capital employed in FY24, and the promised cost discipline was in fact delivered as growth slowed, but there is no itemised cost-obsession culture on display. Growth itself has averaged about 29% a year but arrives in lumps, which is the fingerprint of the elevated cyclicality risk: this is discretionary travel demand, COVID took the Asia-Pacific business to zero, two wars and currency swings marked FY25, and the February 2026 conflict made March 'a complete washout' with volumes down mid-single digits. The offsets are real — revenue moves with travel flow rather than with customers' own capital spending, and the company kept hiring and opening markets through the disruption. Three risks come back low: government appears only as a demand tailwind such as UDAN, airport spending, Schengen visa volumes and Saudi tourism promotion, never as a price setter; the buyer base is deliberately fragmented with no single relationship that moves the numbers, spread across Europe 36%, Middle East and Africa 25%, Americas 18% and Asia-Pacific 8%; and demand is functional and repeat rather than tied to any fashionable destination. Netting it out, the combined rating is 68 out of 100 — 45% weight on business quality at 67, 30% on multibagger fit at 63, 25% on risk safety at 74 — a Strong four-star verdict with resilience judged sound. In plain terms: an excellent competitive position and an honest, founder-led team, attached to economics that presently convert very little of the capital employed into cash.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 2 numeric fingerprints reinforce it.

**What the calls show:** Demand is discretionary travel and swings with exogenous shocks — COVID took APAC 'down to zero', two global wars and forex volatility marked FY25, and the Feb-2026 war made March 'a complete washout' with GTV down mid-single digits — but this is transaction-flow revenue from agents' day-to-day operations, not customers' capex, and no commodity-price linkage appears anywhere in nine calls.

> "Of course, March was a complete washout for us because of the war." — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/operations-linked take-rate revenue rather than customer capex; countercyclical market-development and sales hiring through the FY25-26 disruption; luxury-end mix management says is 'far more resilient to all the geopolitics'*

## What to watch

- Return on capital and the 724-day working capital cycle — the -1% cash return on capital is the single check dragging the verdict down; a return to customer-funded negative working capital, or receivables coming back in, would materially change it.
- Airline monetisation and buyer incentives — the ~2.5% air take rate and the practice of handing incentives to buyers to buy volume are the visible evidence behind the 'Good Enough Goods' risk and the 'Toll Roads' no-fit; more margin given away would confirm the platform cannot charge for access.
- Whether the take rate keeps rising for the right reason — the climb from 4.77% to 6.04% is currently explained by hotel mix, not price; if pricing never becomes the driver, the 'Pricing Power' pattern drops from likely fit to no fit.
- Traveller migration away from agents, including AI-assisted direct booking — this is the 'Shifting Consumer Preferences' and 'New Entrants' risk in one; watch whether the luxury and complex-itinerary pivot and the Voya tool actually hold advisor volumes, since management concedes AI has not yet moved the business either way.
- Hotel share gains versus HBX and WebBeds — the ~35% hotel growth against slower peers is what earns the strong 'Market Share Gainers' fit and the top score on gain in market share; if that gap narrows while growth stays lumpy, both the pattern and the growth persistence check weaken together.
- Cyclical shocks and the FY26 pattern repeating — the February 2026 war turning March into 'a complete washout' shows how fast the elevated cyclicality risk bites; repeated washout quarters would test whether countercyclical hiring and market-opening remain affordable.
