# United Spirits (UNITDSPR) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

United Spirits Limited (also called Diageo India) makes and sells alcoholic drinks in India, mainly whisky — it describes itself as the leader in Scotch and IMFL (Indian-made foreign liquor) in what management calls the world's largest whisky market. It sells a ladder of brands spanning roughly INR120 a bottle up to INR25,000 a bottle, covering both global trademarks (Johnnie Walker, Black & White, Smirnoff, Tanqueray, Don Julio, Singleton, Lagavulin, Talisker) and its own Indian USL trademarks (McDowell's, Royal Challenge, Signature, Antiquity, Black Dog, Director's Special, Godawan). Money comes from selling cases of spirits to consumers through state-regulated liquor retail and on-premise outlets (bars/restaurants) plus channels like the armed forces' Canteen Stores Department, with profit driven by mixing volume with 'premiumization' — shifting sales toward pricier bottles — plus price increases and cost/productivity savings; FY25 net sales value grew about 8.2%, EBITDA margin reached the high teens and return on capital employed 26.4%. It operates almost entirely within India, state by state under heavy regulation of pricing, route-to-market and marketing (Andhra Pradesh restarted after a 5-year gap; Delhi and other states restrict access), with some export/international activity for its Indian single malt Godawan.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Luxury & Premium (including Scotch, malts, tequila, gin, vodka)** — The top end of the portfolio — Johnnie Walker, single malts like Singleton, Lagavulin, Talisker and Indian malt Godawan, Don Julio tequila, Tanqueray gin and Smirnoff vodka — which grew 11% in FY25 and, together with the rest of the top half of the portfolio, contributes 41% of the company's value growth; management calls a recent slowdown from 25-30% growth a temporary blip.
- **BII (Bottled in India) Scotch** — Scotch whisky bottled in India, including Black Dog (1.3x the equity of its nearest BII Scotch competitor) and Black & White — described as 'on fire' with healthy growth over several years, and set to get a 4-5% price cut from the India-UK trade deal.
- **Prestige & Above (P&A)** — The umbrella premium-and-above bucket that management manages to, which grew close to double digits in FY25 (about 9%+ volume growth) and is the stated priority for growth.
- **Upper Prestige** — Brands like Signature and Antiquity — Signature is one of the strongest performers with sustained brand equity scores and over INR500 crores of net sales value.
- **Mid Prestige** — Royal Challenge and Royal Challenge American Pride (RCAP), being expanded nationally and marketed via cricket and music tie-ups; Royal Challenge is one of three trademarks with more than INR1,000 crores of net sales value.
- **Lower Prestige** — Houses the anchor trademark McDowell's — the largest selling whiskey in the world at 30 million cases — and grew 8% in FY25, its fastest in three years, helped substantially by the return to Andhra Pradesh.
- **Innovation pipeline** — New launches such as the McDowell's X-series in rum, vodka and gin, flavoured vodka variants, Double Oaked Barrel and pack-price plays like hipster and pocket packs; currently high-single-digit to low-double-digit of net sales value growth, which management wants to double over 3-5 years.
- **RCB (Royal Challengers Bengaluru)** — A company-owned IPL cricket team used as a marketing asset — management calls it a differentiated asset with exceptional brand equity, ranking among the top five most popular global sports teams by engagement.
- **Supply / manufacturing and productivity program** — Own distilleries and packaging plants plus a multi-year supply agility program started January 2023 that has delivered about two-thirds of its target, roughly INR70 crores into the P&L and INR15-20 crores of working capital benefit, alongside INR388 crores of productivity savings in FY25.

### The story in depth

United Spirits earns its quality marks on the things that are hardest to build. The portfolio rests on heritage trademarks — Johnnie Walker, Black Dog, Lagavulin, and McDowell's at 30 million cases, the world's largest selling whisky — supported by advertising and promotion run at 9-10% of sales with rising absolute spend and measurable results, such as Black Dog's brand equity at 1.3 times its nearest competitor. That combination is why two multibagger patterns come out as strong fits: Brand Strength, and Cost to Replicate, since aged Scotch stock, global trademarks and state-by-state licensing approvals cannot be assembled quickly by anyone starting fresh. The end market is growing on its own — 100 million people reaching legal drinking age over five years in the world's largest whisky market, plus a steady move up the price ladder from INR120 to INR25,000 — and management scores well on candor and on developing people, volunteering bad news about subdued demand, a multi-year share failure in Uttar Pradesh and an uneven X-series rollout rather than over-promising. The economics show it: after direct input costs the company keeps INR64 of every INR100 of sales, and the CFO delivered a margin goal a decade in the making, with high-teens EBITDA margin, ROCE of 26.4% and earnings per share above INR20 for the first time.

The interesting part is that the very traits producing those strengths also produce most of the risks. The brand machine that anchors the Brand Strength pattern runs on celebrity and event visibility — Priyanka Chopra Jonas, Virat Kohli, Ed Sheeran, Lollapalooza, IPL and RCB ownership — which is exactly why the quality framework scores 'security by obscurity' at its worst level and why fashion risk sits on watch: tastes visibly move, with tequila growing fast while gin slows in the same conversation. The state-controlled route to market is a genuine barrier that makes the business hard to copy, but it is also the source of the single highest-rated risk, government dependency, because nearly every growth driver discussed is a policy decision: the India-UK trade deal halving Scotch duty from 150% to 75%, Uttar Pradesh excise reform doubling retail touchpoints from 6,500 to 12,500, and the return to Andhra Pradesh after a five-year policy-driven exit that alone contributed 3.1 percentage points of last year's growth. The same channel structure makes a handful of state administrations effectively the customer base, which is stakeholder concentration, and it already cost the company an INR37 crore receivable write-down. It also caps the Pricing Power pattern to a 'likely' rather than strong fit, since price increases are legislated and lumpy — only 0.2-0.3% a year through FY17-22 against about 2% in the recent good years. And the duty cut that helps the imported portfolio is the same change that lowers the wall against global players who are not yet in India, which is why new entrants sits on watch. Finally, the premiumisation strategy that drives 70% of growth from the top half of the portfolio is the same migration that stranded gin and cooled Luxury and Premium growth from 25-30% a few years ago to 11%.

Put together, this is a strong brand and management story sitting inside a business the company does not fully control, and the combined rating of 56 out of 100 — 'Decent', three stars — reflects that split. Quality scores are highest on management and growth and clearly negative on competitive advantage and industry structure, which is the same tension in another form: the company runs itself well within rules set by others. Several patterns land only partially for that reason — market share gains are real in Andhra but offset by conceded multi-year losses in the large Uttar Pradesh market, innovation has a named calendar and a target to double its contribution over three to five years but no disclosed research spend, and the model has no recurring revenue, no professional prescriber economics and no path to forward integration because retail is state- or licensee-controlled. Three patterns simply do not apply: toll roads, low price plus and forward integrators. The financial picture is solid but not spectacular underneath the headline: sales have compounded at about 3% a year, very consistently; roughly 129 days of cash sits tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers; and while headline return on capital is 26.4%, the business generated INR16 of operating cash per INR100 of capital, a 16% cash return. Overall resilience is judged sound, with technology disruption and cheap substitutes both rated low risk, so the question is not whether the business survives but whether policy, taste and channel access let it compound faster than its recent 3% pace.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Nearly every driver discussed on the FY25 call is a policy variable: the India-UK FTA halving Scotch duty from 150% to 75%, UP's excise reform doubling retail touchpoints from 6,500 to 12,500, re-entry into Andhra after a 5-year policy-driven exit (worth 3.1pp of the 8.2% NSV growth), restrictive route-to-market in Delhi, bureaucratic delays in a key Scotch state, and ENA input costs set by the government's ethanol blending price. Pricing, route-to-market and marketing are all legislated, and management's own framing is that pricing relief 'comes a little slowly' from legislators.

> "There's legislation on pricing, there's legislation on route to market and legislation on marketing." — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Multi-state footprint means no single regulator is decisive, and the current policy cycle (FTA, UP, AP) is running favourably.*

### Cyclicality — WATCH
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Spirits is a repeat-purchase flow product, not customer-capex equipment, but the premium end has visibly cycled: Luxury & Premium growth fell from 25-30% two to three years ago to 11%, attributed to post-COVID revenge consumption and duty-free-to-duty-paid tailwinds unwinding, with headline pricing swinging from 0.2-0.3% (FY17-22) to ~2% (last 3 years) and expected to moderate again.

> "this segment was growing at almost like 25%, 30% 2, 3 years ago. So sequentially moderated... post-COVID revenge consumption kind of moderating" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Consumption-linked, non-capex demand across a INR120-to-INR25,000 price ladder; commodity inputs (ENA, glass) described as broadly stable and offsetting each other.*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 3 of the last 11 years with swings of ±9 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
