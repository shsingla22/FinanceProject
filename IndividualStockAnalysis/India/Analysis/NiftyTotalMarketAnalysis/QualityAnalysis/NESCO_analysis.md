### The story in depth

Nesco comes out as a Decent business — 58 out of 100, three stars — built from a solid quality score of 62, a weak multibagger fit of 28, and a high risk-safety score of 88, with overall resilience judged SOUND. The most striking strength is what the company keeps from what it sells: ₹86 of every ₹100 of sales survives direct input costs, and ₹53 of every ₹100 ends up as operating profit. That is the single fact doing most of the work in this report — it is why Customer Benefits scores the top mark of 2, why the framework reads real pricing power that customers are willing to pay for, and why three of the eleven multibagger patterns (Pricing Power, Brand Strength, and Recurring Revenue with the product paid for upfront) register as PARTIAL rather than absent. Working capital adds a quieter strength: only about a day's worth of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, so the business does not need to fund its own sales.

The same fat margins, though, do not carry through to the return shareholders actually earn, and that is the central tension here. Every ₹1 of assets generates only ₹0.25 of sales — the most negative check in the whole framework at -2 — so this is a fairly asset-heavy operation. Wide margins on a small amount of sales per rupee of assets ends up as ₹12 of operating cash per ₹100 of capital employed, a 12% return that scores a neutral 0 and drags the Return on Capital area to -0.33, the only negative area score. Growth tells a similar story of promise diluted by inconsistency: sales have compounded at roughly 15% a year, which is respectable, but the growth is lumpy year to year, so Growth also scores a flat 0. The multibagger screen reflects this directly — the six NO FIT patterns (Friendly Middleman, Toll Roads, Low Price Plus, Forward Integrators, Market Share Gainers) and the single quant-only Innovation Dominance signal are why the pattern fit lands at just 28, and why high margins alone do not make this a compounding story.

The risks are best understood as the flip side of traits already discussed rather than as separate problems. Operating margins swing by about ±5.1 points a year, and that one number is the shared root of several findings: it holds the Barriers to Entry and Industry Structure scores at 0, it is flagged inside the Profit Margin check as evidence that key costs sit outside management's control, and it is the kind of numeric fingerprint behind the Cyclicality and New Entrants risk flags. Good Enough Goods carries two such fingerprints — a reminder that an 86% gross margin is exactly the kind of prize that invites cheaper substitutes. Crucially, all three risks are marked QUANT FLAG, not confirmed problems: the numbers raise the question and the conference calls say nothing either way. That same silence explains the unusually high risk-safety score of 88 and the two blank quality areas, Competitive Advantage and Management. Every pattern verdict and every risk verdict in this file rests on numbers alone, with no management commentary to confirm or deny them, so the honest reading is a genuinely profitable, cash-light, asset-heavy business whose durability has not yet been tested against what management actually says.
