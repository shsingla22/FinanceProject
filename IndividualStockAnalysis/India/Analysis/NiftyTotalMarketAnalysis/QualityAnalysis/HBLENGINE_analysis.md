# HBL Engineering (HBLENGINE) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

HBL Engineering (formerly HBL Power Systems) is a roughly 40-year-old Indian manufacturer that develops its own technology for small, hard-to-enter markets rather than competing on volume — batteries, railway signalling and safety electronics, and electronic fuzes and other defence hardware. Its customers are mostly government bodies and state-owned buyers: Indian Railways (which is buying its Kavach train-collision-avoidance system), the Ministry of Defence and the armed forces (fuzes for ammunition, grenades, rockets, bombs; lithium-ion cells and torpedo motors for the Navy), plus industrial and export customers for nickel-cadmium batteries. It makes money by selling this equipment on contracts and tenders, deliberately choosing niches with few competitors so it can hold good margins; management stresses that products take 10–15 years of in-house R&D before they earn anything (Kavach R&D started in 2005 and only began paying off in 2025). Operations are almost entirely in India and made in India — the company says it manufactures every component of its fuzes in-house — with exports a small slice, notably nickel-cadmium batteries (Middle East was raised as a market) and negligible exposure to US tariffs. Sales were flat/slightly down in FY25 at roughly ₹2,000 crore; management is 'budgeting' about ₹3,000 crore for FY26 and has said ₹4,500 crore by FY30 still looks reasonable, run through nine largely independent divisions under what the chairman wants to become a holding-company structure.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Kavach (railway signalling / train collision avoidance)** — A complex train-protection system supplied to Indian Railways in two forms — loco (on-train) sets and trackside sections — and it is 'the big story of the year': orders went from a drought to a flood, it drives the jump to ~₹3,000 crore of sales, and the company can't even finish its current order book this year, with track-section orders booked out roughly 24 months.
- **Electronic fuzes for ammunition and other explosive devices** — In-house-developed electronic detonating devices for artillery shells, grenades, air-defence/anti-drone rounds (23mm, 30mm, 40mm), naval rockets, air-dropped bombs and avalanche-control shells, sold to the Indian armed forces; management calls this the next growth engine after Kavach — expected to ramp from FY28 and become the second-biggest business in three to five years — and notes the high-volume artillery ammunition fuzes are actually the least profitable part of it.
- **Fuze batteries** — The specialist batteries that go inside fuzes — the adjacent product HBL entered first and the reason it could build the whole fuze; the company says it is the only Indian maker and one of only a handful of factories worldwide, and it declines to sell these to rivals like BEL.
- **Nickel-cadmium batteries** — A mature, slow-growing industrial battery line where technology was developed fully in-house and suppliers worldwide are few; described as one of the company's big export earners.
- **Lithium-ion cells for the Navy** — A deliberately narrow defence niche — instead of chasing mass-market lithium cells (which management says nobody outside China makes money on), HBL is putting in about ₹200 crore or less of capex and expects to be profitable from year one.
- **TMS and CTC (railway traffic management / centralised traffic control)** — Additional railway control systems where the only competitors are foreign firms; HBL has now cleared the qualification hurdle, but management sizes the opportunity modestly at maybe ₹100–300 crore a year and warns it will not be another Kavach.
- **Electric trucks** — An electric heavy-vehicle effort now focused on 55-tonne and 35-tonne trucks (smaller prototypes were dropped), built without importing kits on the bet that government indigenisation pressure will favour a domestic maker — still pre-commercial, with no orders reported.
- **Torpedo motors and homing heads** — Naval defence components under development — motor prototypes in FY27 with sales possible from FY28, while the homing head needs at least three more years to qualify; neither is counted in the ₹4,500 crore FY30 target.
- **Surplus cash / Mittelstand Technology Partners** — Not an operating business but a newly set-up trust through which HBL plans to invest its growing surplus cash into smaller high-tech Indian manufacturers that also widen its own market access — the alternative to mutual funds, big acquisitions or buybacks, alongside a second dividend planned for March.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

HBL Engineering earns its 65-out-of-100 Strong rating on the strength of what it makes and how it is run, not on how fast it is growing. It is the only Indian maker of fuse batteries, with only a handful of peers worldwide, fully vertically integrated, and the customer comes looking for it when imports stop. That position came from patience: roughly two decades of development work on Kavach and on fuses, with Kavach R&D starting in 2005 and first revenue only arriving in 2025. The money side supports the story — for every ₹100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings in the business, it produced ₹32 of operating cash this year. Management scores well on candor and stewardship: it pre-announced a flat FY25 on the annual report cover and delivered close to it, refuses to present budgets as guidance, says plainly when it does not know an outcome, and deliberately stayed out of the lithium-ion cell and PLI rush that hurt Exide, Amara Raja and Northvolt. Competitive advantage is the standout area at 2.0; capital allocation (0.2) and growth (0.33) are the weak ones.

The multibagger screen reads the same facts and confirms them from a different angle. Innovation Dominance, Cost to Replicate and Corporate Culture are all strong fits, and they are really one trait described three ways — the 10 to 15 years it takes to carry a product from concept to commercial sale, 20 years on fuzes, every component made in-house, and an owner-manager who has run the business across the whole five-year record and speaks carefully about what he does and does not know. Toll Roads and Pricing Power are likely fits: HBL sells small-value but mission-critical parts — fuzes that detonate munitions, nickel-cadmium batteries, Kavach signalling — into niches with only two or three qualified suppliers, where the Ministry of Defence is described as willing to pay a premium for quality made in small volume. The six patterns rated only partial all fail for the same structural reason: the company sells business-to-government through tenders. There is no dealer channel, no brand story, no own stores, no service contracts or renewals, and no explicit market-share percentages — only directional evidence such as exports and forex earnings tripling from about Rs 100 crore to Rs 300 crore over five years.

The risks are largely the flip side of the strengths, not separate problems. The same tender-based sales to Indian Railways and the armed forces that create the toll-road position and the pricing premium also make Government Dependency a high risk and Stakeholder Concentration elevated — two buyers drive almost all incremental revenue, and the entire growth story runs through the railway ministry's qualification process and Ministry of Defence tenders. Cyclicality is high for the same reason: FY25 was flat-to-down purely because Kavach orders slipped, and FY26 is budgeted to jump about 50% to Rs 3,000 crore on an order flood. Lumpy government orders also sit badly with the one clearly negative quality check — about 171 days of cash stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, which ties up capital that the 32% cash return would otherwise be compounding. The deliberate policy of staying in unglamorous niches too small to attract capital, and of not growing a niche past the point where rivals notice, is scored as a genuine strength, but it is also why the growth score is low by design. Three risks sit on watch: new entrants (five vendors already hold Kavach orders with more approvals expected, Adani Defence and Reliance Infra have built large ammunition capacity, and a foreign multinational's Indian arm is described as willing to lose money in nickel-cadmium), commoditisation (HBL has already withdrawn from lead-acid exports, refuses imported-cell lithium at zero margin, and concedes fuses are its least profitable line), and technology shifts (telecom towers already moved from nickel-cadmium to lithium, a market HBL chose to abandon rather than defend). Resilience is judged sound, but the risk-safety score of 34 is what holds the overall rating back from the 78 the business quality alone would support.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Revenue is driven by lumpy Indian Railways and defence procurement programmes rather than steady operating consumption: FY25 was flat-to-down purely because Kavach orders slipped, FY26 is budgeted to jump ~50% to Rs 3,000 cr on an order 'flood', and the Chairman himself flags a possible FY28 decline once the Kavach installation wave passes. Over five years the base moved 1,000-1,500 cr, then ~2,000 cr, then a step change — swings set by the customer's capex cycle, not by HBL's operations.

> "the drought of Kavach orders has now become a flood. And this is typical of these kinds of customers we have" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Nine largely independent divisions and a replacement/export nickel-cadmium business; ammunition fuses are a consumed item rather than customer capex*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 5 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
