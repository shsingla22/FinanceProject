# Azad Engineering (AZAD) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Azad Engineering makes extremely complex, life-critical rotating metal parts — mainly airfoils, blades and nozzle vane segments — that go inside gas, steam and nuclear turbines and inside aircraft and fighter-jet engines, plus precision assemblies for oil and gas; these are 3D-shaped parts made from exotic alloys that run at extreme heat and pressure, not ordinary machined components. It does not sell to end users but supplies global original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) such as GE Vernova, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, Siemens, Baker Hughes, Rolls-Royce (defence and civil), Safran and Pratt & Whitney, competing head-on with suppliers from the US, Europe, China and Japan. Money comes from manufacturing and shipping these components under long-term, multi-year contracts and purchase agreements that it can only win after years — sometimes a decade — of qualification, audits and certification, which makes the business sticky and high-margin; the order book was over INR 6,000 crores in FY25 and over INR 6,500 crores by Q3 FY26. It manufactures out of Hyderabad, Telangana, India, including several new 'lean' plants dedicated to individual customers, and sells into global markets; FY26 revenue was INR 590 crores with EBITDA margin near 36.9% and PAT margin 22.4%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Energy and Oil & Gas** — Critical rotating components and airfoils for gas, steam and nuclear turbines plus complex precision assemblies for oil and gas — the original business and still the dominant one, at INR 97 crores or 77.7% of Q4 FY25 revenue, and management says it continued to contribute the majority of revenue through FY26.
- **Aerospace and Defence (A&D)** — Mission- and life-critical rotating parts for aircraft and military jet engines, entered more recently on the back of the energy experience — INR 25 crores or 19.8% of Q4 FY25 revenue, described as the 'next big leap' with a steadily rising share, anchored by Rolls-Royce and newer engagements with Safran and Pratt & Whitney.
- **Dedicated customer facilities (mega-factory vision)** — Separate lean manufacturing plants built for one marquee customer each — Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (7,200 sq m), GE Vernova (7,600 sq m), Siemens and, from April 2026, Baker Hughes — described as the deepest form of customer integration; four such plants exist since listing, with the infrastructure build-out about 70%–80% complete and full utilisation expected by FY28.
- **Subsidiaries: Azad Prime and Azad VTC** — In-house 'special processes' operations that bring work previously sent to outside vendors inside the group, cutting dependence on external suppliers and saving cost; management expected them to begin contributing from FY26.

### The story in depth

Azad Engineering earns a Strong rating (65 out of 100, four stars) because it has done something genuinely hard: spent fifteen-plus years getting a narrow set of life-critical rotating parts qualified inside the supply chains of the world's biggest turbine and engine makers. The 34-check work gives it top marks for mini-monopolies (single-source status from Mitsubishi Heavy Industries on hot-section nozzle vanes under an eight-year contract), for switching costs (qualification runs years to a decade, with 1,400-plus components approved over fifteen years and 30 to 48 months to onboard a single customer), and for what customers are willing to pay — after direct input costs the company keeps about ₹90 of every ₹100 of sales. Those same facts are what make Cost to Replicate and Market Share Gainers strong-fit patterns and Toll Roads and Pricing Power likely fits: wallet share is still under 1% of a $28 billion market, the order book has grown to ₹6,500 crores, and marquee names (Rolls-Royce, then Safran, Pratt & Whitney, Baker Hughes) keep being added. End markets are expanding on their own — aerospace and defence around 9% a year, oil and gas around 7% — so the runway is not a one-off.

The catch is that the very thing creating the moat also creates the main risks, and the records are unusually direct about this. Winning single-source work means building dedicated plants for each OEM, which is why ₹392 crores was capitalized in FY26 with another ₹191 crores still under construction. That capex scores well as growth spending because it is contract-backed and arrives alongside 30%-plus revenue growth, but it is also why every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.27 of sales, why the same handful of customers drive essentially all results (energy and oil and gas alone were 81.5% of FY26 revenue), and why Stakeholder Concentration is the one HIGH RISK finding. Deep integration into a customer's supply chain is the moat and the dependency at once. Cyclicality sits alongside it on WATCH for the same reason: the plants are being built into what management and analysts call an unusually hot cycle, with OEMs booked out, so the assets are being committed before the demand behind them has been tested through a downturn.

That combination shows up plainly in the cash. About 1,720 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, receivables were ₹309 crores in FY26, and for every ₹100 of capital in the business the company generated negative ₹6 of operating cash this year — a -6% cash return. This is why Return on Capital scores -1.0 and Capital Allocation only 0.2 against Customer Benefits at 1.75, why the business model fails the Recurring Revenue pattern (it consumes working capital rather than supplying it), and why overall resilience is judged STRAINED even with a Strong grade. The offsetting comfort is that the threats which usually kill a niche manufacturer are all rated LOW: new entrants would need fifteen to twenty years to replicate the approvals, no competitor platform or format shift appears anywhere in nine calls, and cheaper substitutes are not a live threat because a sophisticated OEM engineering team gates the order on capability before price is even discussed — margins rose from 33.8% to 36.9% rather than eroding. So the question this rating leaves open is not whether the moat is real. It is whether the cash cycle and the concentrated, asset-heavy bet turn into cash before the cycle turns.
