# HCL Technologies Ltd. (HCLTECH) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: IT*

HCLTech is an India-headquartered technology services company that does IT and engineering work for large corporations around the world — running and modernising their computer systems, moving them to the cloud, writing and maintaining their software applications, handling back-office processes, and designing products and chips for technology manufacturers. It also sells its own software products under the HCL Software label. It earns money mostly from multi-year service contracts and staffed project work billed to clients (it books $9.3 billion of new contract value a year and reported $14.7 billion of revenue in FY26 at about a 17% operating margin), plus software licence and subscription fees. Clients are large global enterprises spread across the Americas, Europe and the rest of the world, served by roughly 224,000 employees working from India and local delivery centres, across verticals such as financial services, manufacturing, life sciences and healthcare, telecom and media, retail and consumer, and technology.

**The parts of the business:**

- **IT and Business Services (ITBS)** — Runs and modernises clients' IT — cloud migration and hybrid cloud operations, digital application development, digital workplace, data platforms and outsourced business/process operations — and is the company's biggest business, growing 3.7% in FY26.
- **Engineering and R&D Services (ERS)** — Helps product companies design and build their own products — chip design and verification, embedded/IoT software, product sustenance engineering and autonomous-robot navigation software — and was the fastest-growing service line in FY26 at 9.8%.
- **HCL Software (products and platforms)** — Sells HCLTech's own software licences and subscriptions, notably a data-management product family (DataConnect, Informix, Ingres, Zen, VectorAI DB, Zeenea, Jaspersoft), worth about $1.4 billion of revenue with $1.05 billion of annual recurring revenue — under a tenth of the group, lumpy quarter to quarter, and roughly flat to slightly declining recently.
- **Advanced AI / AI offerings** — A newer, cross-cutting revenue line built on in-house platforms (AI Force for service delivery, Enterprise AI Foundry, AI Labs, VisionX edge AI, AI Factory datacentre build-outs) that reached about $620 million annualised in FY26 and is the company's main growth engine, with management saying nearly all deals now include an AI component.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

HCL Technologies earns unusually good money on unusually little capital, and that single fact drives most of what the three reviews found. Every ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.02 of sales, ₹22 of every ₹100 of sales is operating profit, and ₹100 of capital in the business threw off ₹25 of operating cash this year. The quality framework's two strongest areas are exactly these: return on capital scored highest of the seven areas, and customer benefits scored full marks because clients demonstrably pay for certainty of execution — an explicit premium on very large deals, high renewal rates among the biggest clients, and being the only firm named a Gartner Customer's Choice across all six IT-services assessments. Management reinforces this rather than undercutting it: disclosure is granular and self-critical, misses are named with their causes, the balance sheet is net cash at $3.17bn with about 87% of profit paid out, and talent is built systematically through fresher training, mass reskilling and an internal CFO promotion from a three-decade company veteran. The risk review calls overall resilience sound, and the two structural dangers that sink many companies — dependence on governments and dependence on fashion — are both rated low, because revenue comes from contracted, repeat multi-year work for private global enterprises across seven industries and four regions.

The patterns that fit best grow directly out of those strengths, and so do the biggest risks — often the same trait doing both jobs. Brand strength is a likely fit on the back of the same institutional trust that shows up in the customer-benefits checks, and market-share gains are a likely fit because that trust converts into organic client growth: one new $100m client and eight new $50m clients in FY26, none bought. But the same client pyramid that produces those wins is what makes individual customers move the numbers — two large US telecom clients cutting discretionary spending caused the Q4 FY26 services miss, and two more client situations are flagged as roughly a half-point drag on FY27 growth, which is why stakeholder concentration sits on watch. The sharper example is artificial intelligence. Innovation dominance is a likely fit thanks to a dated, disclosed product cadence — AI Force releases, 23 industry AI solutions, 38 patents in FY26, and Advanced AI revenue at $620m growing 62% year-on-year — yet the same technology shift is the largest risk in the file, because management itself estimates that 40% of the industry can shrink and that this flows through as 2-3% price deflation on its own portfolio. The offshore cost machine behaves the same way: the low-price pattern is what produces the very high asset turns and returns, and it is precisely that cheaper-equivalent delivery logic that now reprices the core services book downward, which is why the good-enough-goods risk is elevated too. AI is simultaneously the growth story and the deflation story; cost leverage is simultaneously the return engine and the exposure.

What the company does not have is protection, and that is where the score is capped. Two of the seven quality areas — competitive advantage and industry structure — scored zero, and the pattern screen agrees: pricing power and toll-road economics are both flat no-fits. Large contracts carry annual productivity give-backs that mechanically reduce revenue each first quarter, the deal mix leans toward cost cutting and vendor consolidation, an earlier attempt at pricing increases never turned into lasting margin, and FY26 operating margin came in at 17.2%, about a point lower than the year before. Several other patterns land only as partial for related reasons: the recurring-revenue stream is real but not compounding, with software ARR at $1.05bn while that segment fell 14.1% year-on-year in Q4 FY26 and 5.3% in Q1 FY27 as buyers move away from perpetual licences — the same shift the risk review flags as changing customer preferences. Growth is the third soft spot: sales have compounded around 12% a year but lumpily, and FY27 guidance has been cut to 1-4% against a backdrop of tariff volatility, which is why cyclicality is rated elevated. The response is a deliberate change of character — a growth capital-spending step-up into AI datacentres of ₹3,500 crore and up to 50MW with anchor demand secured, alongside a telecom carve-out that is much larger than the small bolt-ons of the past four years — but the return on that money is unproven, and it moves the business away from the asset-light shape that produced the returns in the first place. Netting it out, the combined rating is 59 out of 100, a Decent three stars: a very well-run, cash-rich, highly profitable business whose defences against price erosion are the weakest part of the case.
