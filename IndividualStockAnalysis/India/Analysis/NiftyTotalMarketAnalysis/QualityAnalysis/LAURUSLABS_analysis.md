# Laurus Labs Ltd. (LAURUSLABS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: PHARMA*

Laurus Labs is an Indian pharmaceutical manufacturer, based in Hyderabad and Visakhapatnam (Vizag), that makes drug ingredients (APIs and advanced intermediates) and finished tablets/capsules, and also manufactures on contract for other drug companies. It sells to global health agencies and donor-funded tenders in low- and middle-income countries (mainly HIV/anti-retroviral medicines, where management says it serves about a third of the world's HIV patients), to generic drug customers in the US, Europe and Canada, and to big pharma and biotech innovators who outsource development and manufacturing of their new molecules. Money comes from volume supply contracts on generic products plus higher-margin development and commercial supply contracts for innovator products; FY26 revenue was about INR 6,813 crores (up 23%) with EBITDA margin of 26.8%, rising to INR 2,026 crores in the June 2026 quarter alone. Manufacturing and R&D are in India, with a US front-end for generic launches, a new South Africa office, and heavy ongoing capex on new plants (Unit 7, peptides, fermentation, an animal-health site and a formulations plant under a KRKA joint venture).

**The parts of the business:**

- **CDMO (contract development and manufacturing; earlier called the Synthesis division)** — Develops and manufactures ingredients and intermediates for other companies' patented new drugs, plus animal health and crop protection ingredients, using specialised chemistry (biocatalysis, flow chemistry, hydrogenation, high-potency, and now peptides and ADCs); it is the main growth engine, at INR 2,080 crores in FY26 and over 30% of company sales versus 13% six years earlier, with small-molecule CDMO growing 69% in Q1 FY27 to INR 835 crores.
- **Affordable Medicines division (formerly Generics)** — The legacy volume business selling generic active ingredients and finished-dose medicines — HIV/ARV, oncology, and cardiovascular, diabetes and asthma products — to global health agencies, emerging markets and developed markets; it is still the largest single division at INR 4,733 crores in FY26 (up 18%), with formulation capacity of 12 billion units, 92 DMFs and 96 developed-market dossiers filed.
- **Generic API business (within Affordable Medicines)** — Sells bulk drug ingredients — ARV APIs (INR 400 crores in one quarter of FY25, the market leader in first-line HIV treatment), oncology APIs (described as the largest high-potency capability in India), and other APIs in diabetes, cardiovascular and asthma — to other drug makers, and was the company's historical core.
- **Generic formulations / FDF** — Makes the finished tablets and capsules, sold both into low- and middle-income country ARV tenders and under Laurus's own label in the US, Europe and Canada, and as contract manufacturing for partners; roughly INR 274 crores in Q1 FY25 and now expanding through a joint venture with KRKA including a new Hyderabad plant.
- **Laurus Bio** — The fermentation and enzyme arm, producing animal-origin-free ingredients and enzymes/biocatalysts and doing bio-CDMO work for partners; the smallest division at roughly INR 35-65 crores a quarter, with a large commercial-scale fermentation site in Vizag (400+ kilolitres in Phase 1) due to start by end of 2026.
- **Cell and gene therapy / new modalities** — An early-stage R&D-led effort covering cell therapy (enabling the NexCAR19 product), GMP viral vectors and gene therapy, and recently ADCs via an agreement with Aarvik Therapeutics for two clinical-stage molecules for India; it generates little revenue today and shows up mainly as higher R&D spend (4-6% of sales).

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Laurus Labs earns a Decent rating of 57 out of 100 — 69 on business quality, 56 on multibagger fit, and only 38 on risk safety — and the three readings all point at the same underlying company: a low-price, high-volume generics maker that is deliberately converting itself into a contract manufacturer for innovator drug companies. The strongest quality checks describe what that conversion has bought. Customers demonstrably pay for certainty: 132 regulatory and customer audits a year cleared without a critical finding, and uninterrupted supply through geopolitical disruption. The technology platform — biocatalysis with in-house enzymes, flow chemistry, hydrogenation, continuous drug-product manufacturing, high-potent work, peptides, ADCs and gene therapy — scores top marks, and end markets are in genuine secular expansion as drug companies look for trusted partners outside China. The Competitive Advantage and Management areas score full marks, and the moat check that matters most is regulatory switching costs: once a molecule is validated at a particular site and batch size, innovators will not move it. That single fact is the hinge of the whole story. It is why building capacity before the orders arrived won the programmes, it is what makes the CDMO side a partial fit for the Toll Roads pattern, and it is the core of the Cost to Replicate pattern — 8,200 cubic metres of reactor volume plus years of customer qualification is a real barrier, even if not an un-crossable one.

The uncomfortable part is that the very traits producing the strengths also produce the top risks. Building capacity ahead of demand is scored as growth capex, not maintenance — ₹2,000 crore across FY23-24, ₹1,070 crore in FY26, guidance lifted from ₹1,000 crore to ₹2,000 crore for FY27 and above ₹3,000 crore for FY27-28 — and it was sustained straight through the FY24-25 downturn because the founders refused to gear up the balance sheet, holding net debt to EBITDA at 2.3, then 1.25, then 1.28. That discipline is exactly what the Corporate Culture pattern rewards as a strong fit: the same CEO and CFO across all 18 calls, growth entirely organic, and management publicly owning the bad stretch rather than explaining it away. But paying for plant years before it earns pushes cash out the door, and the framework's harshest mark is on working capital — roughly 263 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers — with Capital Allocation and Return on Capital among the weakest scoring areas at 0.40 and 0.67. The same installed base creates the Elevated technological risk: 8,200 cubic metres is small-molecule capacity at the moment demand is migrating toward peptides, ADCs, cell and gene therapy, which analysts have probed directly. Rising R&D, from 3% of sales in Q1 FY23 to 5.8% in Q1 FY27, and filings stepping up from 74 to 92 DMFs, are the hedge against that — the basis of the Likely Fit on Innovation Dominance — but they are an option on the next wave, not proof of it.

The second knot is that Laurus wins volume without winning price, and that shows up on both sides of the ledger. It supplies roughly a third of the world's HIV patients, ARV sales rose to ₹2,800 crore in FY26 on volume rather than price, and the mix has shifted CDMO from 13% to over 30% of sales in six years — enough for a Likely Fit on Market Share Gainers and a partial fit on Low Price Plus, with backward integration, raw-material renegotiation and process improvements lifting gross margin from about 55% to 62.7%. But the same record shows four years of price deflation, so Pricing Power is a clear No Fit, Brand Strength is a No Fit because the 2024 rebrand is business-to-business positioning with no premium pricing behind it, and Recurring Revenue is a No Fit because the money arrives through tenders, RFPs and project deliveries rather than renewals or licence fees. That order-driven base is precisely what the risk review flags twice over: Government Dependency is High because donor- and government-funded buyers set price and volume in winner-takes-all bidding, and Stakeholder Concentration is High because the FY23 peak and the collapse that followed both traced to one large CDMO purchase order. Those same swings explain the merely average scores on margin and return on capital — operating margin around 26% but moving about 5.5 points a year, EBITDA falling from roughly 30% to 14% with return on capital at 6% in FY25 before recovering to 26.8% in FY26 and 31.8% in Q1 FY27. Resilience is still judged Sound, because demand is medical and non-discretionary, Fashion Risk and Shifting Consumer Preferences are Low, and the deliberate dilution of ARV from 67% of revenue to about 33% is the management answer to the concentration problem. The honest read: a well-run company with a real and defensible position in contract manufacturing, still carrying a large generics book where it is a price-taker, and asking shareholders to fund heavy plant today against returns management itself frames as an FY30 outcome.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration, Technological Innovation, New Entrants. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.5× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 80% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital moderated from about 15% to 11% — lower, but still well clear of the deep slide this check looks for.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** A large minority of revenue (ARV was 67% six years ago, 41% in FY26, ~33% in Q1FY27) is sold into donor- and government-funded LMIC procurement — Global Fund, PEPFAR, South Africa tenders — where price and volume are set by winner-takes-all bidding and agency buying patterns; ARV formulation sales fell by a third sequentially in Q1FY25 purely on 'global agencies buying pattern'. The AP government land parcel and duty/geopolitical exposure add to it.

> "most of the tenders, except South Africa, winner takes all tenders... that is the case with global fund and PEPFAR" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Deliberate multi-year dilution of policy-driven revenue: ARV share down from 67% to ~33% while absolute sales were sustained, with CDMO up from 13% to over 30%*

### Stakeholder Concentration — HIGH RISK
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 1 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** The FY23 peak and subsequent collapse were driven by a single large CDMO purchase order — 'the CDMO segment itself saw a big collapse because of disappearing of that purchase order' — and analysts repeatedly press on product concentration and innovator destocking; formulation growth also hinges on one named partner (KRKA JV plus an extended CMO contract adding 3 billion tablets of capacity for that partner), and Laurus Bio's R2 is built around a single large-scale CDMO customer.

> "more we are concentrated on a few products with more risk of destocking" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Broadening base: over 70 active CDMO projects and ~10 commercial products, 3 new commercial APIs with multi-year patent life, new big-pharma client onboarded in Q1FY27, and a stated aim to 'reduce any concentration risk from a single product or a single therapy'*

- ⚠️ Customers now take about 122 days to pay, up from 93 — lengthening collection terms are how weak bargaining power against concentrated customers shows up in the numbers.

### Technological Innovation — ELEVATED
*"Out-invented by the industry"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** The installed base is 8,200 cubic metres of small-molecule reactor volume just as demand migrates toward peptides, ADCs, cell and gene therapy — analysts probe this directly ('is biotech getting the attention it truly deserves... that's the sort of coming wave... along with the AI'), and management concedes gene therapy/ADC spend 'is not going to give returns' for years. The cadence of incremental platform additions is a genuine hedge rather than a bet-the-company pivot.

> "up to 10% of our investments or our profits into a disruptive technology, either in-house or external" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Continuous small-scale, value-adding platform innovation (biocatalysis, flow chemistry, hydrogenation, continuous manufacturing) plus early optional investment in peptides, ADCs and gene therapy alongside the core*

- ⚠️ Growth has stalled: sales compounded at 24% a year in the first half of the record but only 7% recently — demand may be structurally fading.
