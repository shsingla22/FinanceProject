# Marksans Pharma (MARKSANS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Marksans Pharma is an India-based drug maker that manufactures generic and over-the-counter (OTC) medicines — mostly everyday, repeat-purchase products in pain, cough and cold, allergy and gastrointestinal categories — and sells them into regulated Western markets. It largely supplies other companies rather than consumers directly: retailers and store-brand customers (management names Kroger and Sam's Club) plus distributors, so revenue comes from high-volume manufacturing contracts at negotiated prices that are renegotiated periodically, with a smaller prescription (Rx) generics business that faces regular price erosion. Production is centred on low-cost plants in Goa, India (capacity expanded repeatedly, plus a newly acquired unit), with the company backward-integrating into its own APIs (raw drug ingredients) to protect margins. In FY26 it earned INR 2,951 crores of revenue and INR 420 crores of profit, is debt-free with ~INR 990 crores of cash, and operates through overseas arms including Marksans Pharma Inc (US), Relonchem (UK), Nova Pharma (Australia) and newly formed entities in Ireland and Canada.

**The parts of the business:**

- **US and North America** — Generic and OTC formulations sold to US retail and store-brand customers — the largest and fastest-growing market at INR 1,533 crores in FY26 (52% of revenue, up 24%), with 112 SKUs launched in the year, 51 more in the pipeline and an order book management pegs at over $220 million.
- **UK and EU formulations** — Medicines sold in Britain and Europe, largely through UK subsidiary Relonchem which keeps winning MHRA approvals; the second-biggest business at INR 1,015 crores in FY26 (about 34% of revenue), it hit a pricing-pressure patch early in FY26 before recovering to a record INR 308 crore quarter, and the company plans to file over 200 products in the UK over four years.
- **Australia and New Zealand** — A long-standing OTC business, roughly INR 303 crores in FY26 (about 10% of revenue) and growing fast (61% in Q4), now extended for the first time into branded prescription generics with 11 launches through Nova Pharma.
- **Rest of the World** — A small collection of other export markets, INR 99 crores in FY26 (about 3.4% of revenue), where management says it is staying cautious because of macroeconomic conditions.
- **API / backward integration** — An in-house effort to make its own active drug ingredients for core molecules (starting with pain products), filing DMFs and tying up CMO partners — not a separate revenue line, but management's main lever to secure raw material supply and defend gross margins against Chinese suppliers.
- **R&D and product pipeline** — New product development and regulatory filings that feed launches across all regions; spending rose from about 2% of sales (INR 8.9 crores a quarter in FY23) to INR 89 crores, or 3% of revenue, in FY26, against a stated ambition of 4-5%.

### The story in depth

Marksans Pharma comes out of these three reviews as a well-run company inside a hard industry, and the record scores it 54 out of 100 — 'Decent' — as 45% of 58 on business quality, 30% of 33 on multibagger fit and 25% of 72 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound. Almost all of the strength sits with the people and the balance sheet: Management is the top-scoring area at 1.4, with full marks for candor (guiding down UK expectations, flagging raw-material inflation before it hit, admitting a guidance miss it later beat), for stewardship (debt-free across all sixteen quarters with INR990 cr of cash and capex gated on order-book evidence rather than ambition), and for tenacity (a INR2,000 cr milestone flagged in FY23, a INR3,000 cr target set in FY25 and crossed in FY26). The one growth engine that scores as well is share capture: North America went from INR635 cr to INR1,533 cr in four years on deeper basket penetration with existing customers, which is exactly why 'Market Share Gainers' is the single strong fit in the pattern screen. Modest growth capex — roughly INR131 cr in FY26 on the acquired Teva Goa plant — has paid back visibly, with that plant now running INR560-600 cr of revenue.

The uncomfortable part is that the same business model producing those wins is what produces the biggest risks and the long list of pattern misses. Marksans makes unbranded over-the-counter medicine that a handful of very large retailers — Walmart, Kroger, Sam's Club are named repeatedly — sell under their own labels. That is precisely what lets the company win share: it can go to an existing buyer and sell them more of the basket. It is also why two of the eight risks come back elevated. Stakeholder concentration is elevated because those few buyers reset prices on a fixed calendar under two-year contracts; 'good enough goods' is elevated because the end consumer has no idea who made the product and management says plainly that buyers will switch to a cheaper supplier. The same fact runs through the framework scores and the pattern verdicts: pricing/mix/volume scores -2 because growth is volume- and share-led against constant high single-digit price erosion in the US and flat UK revenue on pricing pressure, industry structure scores -2 on oligopoly because the US generic/OTC market is fragmented with excess capacity, and barriers to entry score -1 with margins swinging by ±5.6 points. On the pattern side that combination knocks out 'Pricing Power', 'Brand Strength', 'Toll Roads' and 'Friendly Middleman' outright. Asked directly about the moat, management offered nothing beyond experience — which is why 'Cost to Replicate' is a no-fit, why competitive advantage and return on capital both score a flat zero, and why new entrants sits on watch.

The rest of the picture is a company deliberately working on these weaknesses without having solved them yet, plus one cost that gets less attention than it deserves. Management is building its own front-ends — Relonchem in the UK, subsidiaries in the US and Australia, and new entities in Germany, Ireland and Canada in FY26 — and is explicitly targeting new and relatively bigger clients; that is the stated answer to buyer concentration and it is why 'Forward Integrators' rates a partial fit rather than a miss, but there are no own stores or franchising, so it is only half the pattern. The same goes for cost leadership ('Low Price Plus', partial): low-cost manufacturing, backward integration into API and scale from the Goa plant do show up in a 20.4% FY26 EBITDA margin, being debt-free and INR328 cr of free cash flow, but the record contains no itemised cost comparison to prove the edge. The filing cadence — R&D lifted from about 2% to 3% of sales at INR89 cr in FY26, 112 SKUs launched in North America, 51 in the pipeline, an intent to file 200-plus products in the UK — earns a likely fit on 'Innovation Dominance', though it is generic and OTC filing volume rather than genuinely new science. The quiet drag is working capital: about 261 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, a -2 score, and the same inventory-heavy, one-off-supply model is why 'Recurring Revenue' is a clear no-fit — there is no subscription, licence or renewal stream anywhere in sixteen calls. Against all this, the four remaining risks — cyclicality, technological change, shifting consumer preferences and fashion — are all low, because the demand base is repeat-purchase functional medicine and the one visible consumer shift, shoppers moving from branded to store-brand products, runs in Marksans' favour.

## What to watch

- Whether the 'Market Share Gainers' engine keeps outrunning price erosion — the pattern rests on North America going from INR635 cr to INR1,533 cr and a $220mn order book; if basket penetration slows while high single-digit erosion continues, the one strong pattern stops offsetting the -2 on pricing/mix/volume.

- The 261-day cash conversion cycle (the -2 on cost of working capital, against 151 days working capital in 9M FY26 and 138 days in FY26) — sustained improvement would lift a weak capital-allocation area of 0.2; further stretching would strain a business that has no recurring-revenue cushion.
