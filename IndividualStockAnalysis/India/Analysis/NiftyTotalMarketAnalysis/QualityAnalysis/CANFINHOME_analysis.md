# Can Fin Homes Ltd. (CANFINHOME) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

Can Fin Homes is an Indian housing finance company, sponsored by Canara Bank and headquartered in Bengaluru, that lends money to individuals to buy or build homes, plus some non-housing loans against property-type lending. It borrows wholesale (bank term loans, commercial paper, non-convertible debentures and refinance from the National Housing Bank) and lends that money out at a higher rate — in the latest quarter earning about 9.81% on its loan book against a 6.98% cost of borrowing, for a spread of roughly 2.8%; almost all its profit comes from this interest margin. Customers are ordinary retail borrowers split between salaried people and self-employed/non-professionals (SENP), and the loans are secured on the property. It runs a branch network across India (about 200 branches in 2022, organised into 6 zones), with Karnataka, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh repeatedly called out; the loan book grew from about INR 22,000 crores in 2022 to over INR 40,000 crores by FY26, with FY26 disbursements of INR 10,531 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Housing loans** — The core product — loans to individuals to buy or construct a home, which management treats as the main engine of the book; housing disbursements grew 28% year-on-year in Q1 FY27.
- **Non-housing loans (NHL)** — The smaller secondary lending line management reports alongside housing, growing slightly faster (32% in Q1 FY27), used to lift overall yield.
- **Salaried segment** — Borrowers with regular jobs — the larger and traditionally safer half of new lending, growing 21% year-on-year in Q1 FY27.
- **Self-employed / non-professional (SENP) segment** — Borrowers with business or irregular income, the faster-growing half of disbursements (up 44% in Q1 FY27) and the part management is deliberately expanding.
- **Borrowing / treasury (liability side)** — Raising funds through bank term loans linked to T-bill or repo, commercial paper, NCDs and NHB refinance under the Affordable Housing Fund — management describes managing this cost as the main lever on the ~2.75–2.85% spread it guides to.
- **Branch and zonal network** — Around 200 branches (in 2022) grouped into 6 zones that source loans, with branches opened after March 2023 and a dedicated sales team named as contributors to recent growth.
- **Recovery / collections** — Chasing overdue loans, including selling seized property under SARFAESI; the calls describe this as small in scale — gross NPAs of 0.85% and credit cost of about 10–15 basis points.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Can Fin Homes is a mortgage lender whose scorecard splits cleanly down the middle, and the rating arithmetic says so: business quality scores 44 out of 100 while risk safety scores 64, blending to 51 and a "Decent" three-star grade. The strong half is entirely about the people running it. Capital Allocation scores a full +1 and Management +0.67, supported by candour that rates a rare 2 — the company voluntarily disclosed and quantified branch-level frauds in FY23 (INR3.93 crore and INR2.43 crore, followed by a 200-branch audit), admitted to negative disbursement growth, and in July 2026 publicly said its own thesis on balance-transfer outflows had been wrong. Growth is organic, leverage sits at 7.84x against a 12x regulatory cap, and the spending is genuinely for expansion rather than upkeep: 54 new branches with 28 more planned, an in-house sales team taken from roughly 35 people to 150, and a roughly INR40 crore technology programme, with new branches already contributing INR863 crore. The weak half is about the business itself. Return on Capital scores -1, Industry Structure -0.83 and Competitive Advantage -0.67, because a prime home loan is a commodity, roughly 80% of business is sourced through third-party agents who can place a file anywhere, and there is no brand or taste premium to defend a thin 2.83% spread.

The pattern screen makes the same point from a different angle: not one of the eleven patterns is a clean fit — four are partial and seven are outright misses — and the partials all fail for the same two reasons. The Friendly Middleman pattern half-applies because origination really is intermediated (80% through agents, plus 331 approved builder projects), but the benefit that normally comes with a middleman — a customer who does not shop on price — is missing, since borrowers transfer their loan away over 50 basis points. Low Price Plus half-applies because the cost base is genuinely lean (cost-to-income of 15.84% in Q1FY23, guided to 18-19%) on high-quality salaried lending with credit costs around 10 basis points, but the savings get handed straight to customers rather than kept. That hand-back is precisely why Pricing Power, Toll Roads and Cost to Replicate all register as no-fit: the book was moved from annual to quarterly rate resets, a full 50 basis points plus a further 15 was passed through to stop customers leaving, portfolio yield fell to 9.81%, and larger tickets above INR20-30 lakh are written "on the finer side" of pricing. The branch and sales-team build-out is best read as management trying to fix exactly this — a partial Forward Integrators move to own more of its own distribution — but with 80% of volume still arriving through outside agents, the fix is not yet visible in the economics. Meanwhile Market Share Gainers fails on the trajectory: loan book growth decelerated from 24% and an 18% guidance in July 2022 to 10.44% in FY26, with negative disbursement growth through FY24-25.

The risk review then reads as the same facts wearing different clothes. New Entrants, Good Enough Goods and Stakeholder Concentration are all rated Watch, and all three trace back to the identical trait that broke the pattern screen — an undifferentiated product sold through agents to price-shopping borrowers, with a persistent INR370-410 crore of balance transfers out every quarter and geographic concentration in Karnataka and Bengaluru at roughly 22% of the book. Cyclicality is a Watch because earnings move with rates rather than with a commodity cycle: cost of funds went 5.8% in FY23 to 7.58% in FY25 to 6.98% in FY27, compressing spread, and management flags seasonal first-quarter bad-loan build-ups. Technology is the one clearly Low risk, since the technology story is the company's own catch-up modernisation rather than an outside platform threatening the model — though that same catch-up nature is why Innovation Dominance is a no-fit, with the new loan system still piloting in five branches in July 2026 and carrying INR40 crore of cost. The single High Risk is Government Dependency, and here one fact cuts both ways: refusing a sanctioned INR1,500 crore refinance line from the National Housing Bank when its subsidised component disappeared is scored as evidence of disciplined stewardship, yet it is also proof that a meaningful funding advantage depends on an annual budget allocation the company does not control, alongside subsidy schemes that shape the smaller-ticket segment. The silver linings are real — spread of 2.83% and margin of 3.81% held above guidance despite the rate give-back, transfers out rose only INR8 crore year on year, borrowers are granular and salaried, and funding was diversified enough to skip the housing bank entirely in FY24-25 without paying up — but they describe a defence holding, not an advantage compounding. Overall resilience is recorded as unknown, which fits a company where honest management and a lean cost base are running a business with no moat in a market it does not price.
