# Nava (NAVA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Nava Limited (formerly Nava Bharat Ventures) is a Hyderabad-based Indian group that runs a diversified set of heavy-asset businesses across India and Africa. Its two main money-makers are electricity generation — coal-fired power plants in India (Odisha and the 150 MW Nava Bharat Energy India unit) sold on the exchange and via bilateral contracts, plus a large 300 MW plant in Zambia held through Maamba Energy Limited that sells power to the state utility ZESCO under a long-term dollar-denominated PPA and into the Southern African power pool — and manganese ferro alloys (silico manganese and ferro silicon) sold mainly to steel mills, including annual contracts with two large Japanese mills and quarterly contracts with big Indian steel producers. It also mines and sells coal in Zambia, and is building out newer ventures in agriculture (an avocado plantation and the Kawambwa sugar project) plus a 100 MW solar plant and a 300 MW thermal expansion in Zambia. Earnings at the parent company come largely as dividends and buyback proceeds pushed up from the overseas subsidiaries, and the group also owns non-core land parcels in India (65 acres at Nacharam in Hyderabad, plus Samalkot and Dharmavaram) that it may monetize later.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Energy (Zambia — Maamba Energy Limited)** — A 300 MW coal-fired plant in Zambia selling power to state utility ZESCO on a 20-year-type PPA at roughly 10.3 US cents/unit (Phase 1, valid to 2036) with surplus sold into the regional power pool; management repeatedly calls this the single biggest contributor to consolidated revenue and profit, running at 97–100% plant availability, and a further 300 MW expansion (~$400 million capex, 65:35 with partner ZCCM-IH) is due to be commissioned in early 2027.
- **Energy (India)** — Domestic coal-fired independent power plants in Odisha and the 150 MW Nava Bharat Energy India Limited (NBEIL) unit, selling on the IEX exchange and under bilateral contracts — NBEIL alone did about Rs.201 crores of revenue and Rs.68 crores pre-tax profit in one quarter at 91%+ PLF, with FY26 realizations estimated near Rs.5.50 per unit and coal drawn entirely from Singareni Collieries in Telangana.
- **Metals / Ferro Alloys** — Manufactures and sells silico manganese and ferro silicon to the steel industry — a volatile, price-driven business (it swung from a small loss to Rs.23 crores pre-tax in one comparison quarter) that management says is insulated because roughly 40% of output goes to two Japanese mills on yearly contracts and another 40–50% to Indian steel producers on quarterly fixed contracts, leaving only 10–15% exposed to spot prices.
- **Mining** — Coal mining operations in Zambia (alongside the group's earlier manganese-ore exploration concession in the Ivory Coast), selling roughly 35,000–42,000 tonnes a month; described as a steady, meaningful contributor with revenue up about 17% quarter-on-quarter in Q3 FY26 on higher volumes and margins.
- **Agri business** — Newer, still-small ventures: Nava Avocado Limited's plantation in Zambia (four divisions of 275 hectares each; first pilot crop ~140 tonnes, first commercial harvest ~150 tonnes, expected ~1,000 tonnes next year and doubling annually to 2034) and the Kawambwa sugar complex, whose processing unit should be ready around April 2028 — management says the segment needs another 4–5 years to become significant.
- **Renewables / new projects** — A 100 MW solar plant in Zambia (~$90 million capex, commissioning from July 2026) plus further renewable opportunities being explored, aimed at diversifying earnings as Zambia moves away from hydropower dependence.
- **Land and non-core assets** — Idle land parcels held for eventual monetization — 65 acres at Nacharam in East Hyderabad plus the former sugar plant land at Samalkot and land at Dharmavaram — which management says are appreciating and will be sold opportunistically rather than urgently.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Nava's clearest strength is the way its management behaves rather than the economics of what it owns. The quality framework's highest mark goes to being independent, long-term and tenacious: over four years the company renegotiated the tariff it charges Zambia's state utility, pursued arbitration, and ground an overdue receivable down from $563m in August 2022 to $30.5m by February 2026, all while restructuring debt and beginning Phase 2 of the Zambian plant. The multibagger screen reaches the same conclusion from a different direction — Corporate Culture is the single Strong Fit among eleven patterns, because promises made on early calls visibly came true later: the group went debt-free at the standalone level, and a May-2024 promise of a 'corporate action' became an interim dividend three months on. The Zambian expansion itself is credited as credibly executed — debt repaid, roughly 100% plant availability, the Phase 2 power purchase agreement signed and construction under way — and the business currently turns ₹100 of capital into ₹21 of operating cash. So the record of doing what it said it would do is genuine, and it is largely the record of fixing one difficult situation.

The trouble is that almost everything else the screen looks for is absent, and the same handful of traits keep producing both the modest positives and the serious negatives. Six of the eleven patterns are outright No Fit and four are only partial. There is no repeat or subscription revenue — sales are spot and merchant power, ferro alloy tonnage and coal tonnage. There is no pricing power; the record runs the other way, with the Zambian tariff cut from 11.57 to 9.06 cents, Phase 2 signed at 9.7 cents against Phase 1's 10.3, and domestic exchange power down 12% year on year by February 2026. There is no brand and no innovation story: across sixteen calls no technology differentiation is discussed, no research spending or product pipeline is disclosed, and the one attempt at product diversification, ferro silicon, was started in FY25 and abandoned by FY26 — which is why the framework scores technology advantage at its lowest level. The one pattern with real substance, Cost to Replicate, rests entirely on the Maamba asset: a captive coal deposit feeding a 300MW baseload plant in a grid that was roughly 95% hydro, under a 20-year regulator-approved contract with a state partner. Note that this is also precisely what makes the business asset-heavy — every ₹1 of assets generates ₹0.30 of sales, and the growth spending is on the same scale, with $400m of capital for $180-200m of annual revenue on Phase 2 and $90m for $15-16m on solar, alongside 178 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers.

The risk review then names the flip side of each strength. The single asset and single state buyer that make Maamba hard to replicate are also why stakeholder concentration is a high risk — one plant drives consolidated profit, one utility buys the power, and the Telangana business takes 100% of its coal from a single supplier. The state relationship that provides a 20-year inflation-linked contract is the same relationship that cut the tariff by fiat and let arrears build to $563m, which is why government dependency is also high risk. The undifferentiated commodity output that rules out brand and pricing power is what makes earnings swing with the steel and power cycles — high risk again — and puts 'good enough goods' and new entrants on watch, with new coal mines around Maamba triggering a price war and European safeguard duties pushing roughly a million tonnes of export alloy back into India; the framework sees the same thing in profit margins that move by nearly 12 points and in sales growth that averages about 8% but arrives lumpily. Coal-fired baseload in a market management itself describes as shifting toward solar is the remaining watch item. There are real mitigations — contracted volumes covering 85-90% of metals output, receivables now arriving on time, and deliberate moves into avocado, sugar and the company's own 100MW solar plant — and the balance sheet is judged sound. But the combined rating of 34 out of 100 is a Weak, one-star verdict: creditable management working an asset-heavy, price-taking business with no self-reinforcing advantage underneath it.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration, Fashion Risk. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.3× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 3 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across all four years the earnings swing with commodity cycles: ferro alloy revenue rides steel prices (Aug 2022 robust on 'sustained high demand from the steel industry', then Aug 2024 'prices which you're seeing in quarter 1 have tapered down in quarter 2', then Feb 2026 near-breakeven EBIT), Indian merchant power realizations move with exchange prices (down 12% year-on-year in FY26), and coal sales collapsed ~50% in Q1 FY23 on a cement-industry slump; management itself attributes fluctuating dividends to the cycle.

> "our profits keep fluctuating, the commodity cycles keep fluctuating. Nothing is constant and stagnant" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Partial de-risking over time: 20-year USD PPA at Maamba indexed to US inflation, a 5-year bilateral contract with Tamil Nadu at INR5.2/kWh, and by May 2026 yearly contracts with two Japanese mills (40% of output) plus quarterly fixed-price contracts with Indian mills (another 40-50%), leaving only 10-15% spot exposure; power/coal are operations-linked flow products rather than customer capex.*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 4 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from 14% to 46% (swinging ±11.6 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 90% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 5% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Fortunes are set by state counterparties throughout: the Maamba tariff was cut by regulator/state utility fiat from 11.57 to 9.06 cents with ZESCO arrears peaking at $563m and a multi-year arbitration; Indian PLF was throttled because 'when tariffs go high, they divert almost all the coal to government power plants and give us nothing'; and profitability depends on Zambian tax holidays, EU safeguard duties, MOIL ore pricing, mining concessions and (May 2026) Zambian elections.

## What to watch

- Whether the Zambian receivable stays near $30.5m or starts rebuilding — the collection record is the main evidence behind both the tenacity check and the Corporate Culture pattern, and the reason government dependency is only partly mitigated.
