# Siemens Ener.Ind (ENRIN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Siemens Energy India Limited (ENRIN) is a "pure play" energy equipment and services company — it makes the hardware and provides the engineering and after-sales services that generate electricity and move it across the grid. Its customers are power utilities (generation, transmission and distribution), industrial companies such as cement, steel, sugar, paper, oil and gas and semiconductors, plus newer users like data centres and infrastructure such as railways and ferries. It earns money by winning orders for equipment (gas and steam turbines, transformers, switchgear, substations, grid-stabilisation systems) and for projects and long-term services like modernisation, upgrades and efficiency improvements on machines already installed, then executing that order backlog into revenue. It is based in India — eight factories, four service centres and four engineering/R&D centres, about 4,500 people, listed in June 2025 — selling mainly into the domestic market but with a growing export share, including transformers to the US data-centre market and switchgear to the Middle East. In the half year to March 2026 it reported revenue of about ₹43 billion (up ~27%), an order backlog of about ₹184 billion (up 22%), and profit from operations of 20.7% of revenue.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Power Transmission** — Sells the equipment and services that carry electricity from power plants to users — high-voltage AC and DC substations, HVDC links, power transformers, switchgear, grid-stabilisation systems (STATCOMs) and related services; it is the slightly larger of the two segments by revenue and the faster-growing one, with backlog up from about ₹98 billion to ₹125 billion and revenue up about 30% to ₹24 billion in H1 FY26, and management says it holds roughly 30% of India's HVDC capacity.
- **Power Generation** — Supplies equipment and services for making electricity — large gas turbines for the grid, industrial gas and steam turbines for captive power at oil and gas, sugar, paper, cement and steel customers, plus waste-heat-recovery systems that turn hot exhaust gases into extra steam and power; management describes it as a steady grower resting on a large installed base, with its Vadodara steam-turbine factory having shipped around 2,000 turbines and the company holding about 25% of India's gas turbines.
- **Service business (within both segments)** — Maintains, modernises, upgrades and improves the efficiency of machines already installed — including refurbishing turbines at coal-fired plants (a 210 MW turbine in Orissa) even though the company does not sell coal-plant equipment; management calls this fleet the largest in the country and names service expansion as a key growth priority.
- **Exports / global network role** — Manufactures finished products and components and does engineering and project execution out of India for Siemens Energy companies and customers abroad; exports rose about 500 basis points as a share of revenue year on year and helped lift margins through better price realisation.
- **Green hydrogen (PEM electrolysers)** — The company says it is the market leader in PEM electrolysers, but treats this as a longer-term bet — it does not yet manufacture electrolysers in India and expects demand to pick up only around five years out.

### The story in depth

Siemens Ener.Ind sells into a market that is genuinely expanding: India's generation base is described as heading from roughly 500GW toward 1,000-1,200GW, with 800 GVA of new transformation capacity needed and per-capita electricity consumption at a third of the global average. The company converts that backdrop into strong numbers today — ₹22 of operating profit on every ₹100 of sales, held steady year to year, and ₹73 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital employed. Orders rose 49% in FY25, the backlog grew 22% to ₹184bn, and management is funding three expansions off it: a ₹7.4bn brownfield programme doubling Kalwa transformer capacity from 15,000 to 30,000 MVA plus Sambhajinagar switchgear, and a ₹20.6bn greenfield transformer plant. This is growth spending, not upkeep. What the customer is really buying is reliability — STATCOMs that prevent the kind of blackout seen in Spain and Portugal, record-time restoration of a failed plant, a no-lost-time-injury record — the sort of purchase where the cost of failure dwarfs the price of the equipment.

The multibagger screen is where the story gets more qualified, and the qualifications trace back to the same traits that produce the strengths. Margins have climbed steadily (12.7% FY23 → 15.7% FY24 → 19.3% FY25 → 20.7% in H1FY26, about 18.9% adjusted), but management attributes the gains to operating leverage, export price arbitrage and product mix rather than to raising prices — so pricing power reads as a likely fit, not a confirmed one. Every pattern that could have been an outright fit is held back by the parent relationship or by missing disclosure: the technology, the trademark and export market access all belong to the German parent, which caps both 'brand strength' and 'cost to replicate' at partial credit. The installed fleet does generate a service annuity — 25.6% of FY25 revenue and 27% (about ₹12bn) of H1FY26 revenue, supported by new workshops at Raipur and four service centres built near customers — but attach rates and renewal terms are never discussed, so recurring revenue only scores partial. Four patterns fail outright, and for consistent reasons: sales are direct B2B through competitive tenders and TBCB auctions with no intermediary, the products are large lumpy capital items rather than small consumables, the culture prizes selectivity over cost-cutting, and the company cannot legally reach customers outside South Asia.

The risks are largely the strengths viewed from the other side. The secular demand story that makes the end market attractive is set by policy — the CEA capacity plan, NITI Aayog's net-zero scenarios, the 500→1,000GW target, the 100GW nuclear ambition contingent on pending legislation, PLI incentives and state-run TBCB auctions — which is why government dependency rates high risk. The same order book that funds the expansions is lumpy by management's own admission: a very strong FY25 for STATCOMs was followed by a muted half, and revenue ultimately depends on customers' capital spending decisions, making cyclicality a high risk too. The export mix shift driving roughly 170bps of margin improvement sits entirely at the parent's discretion, so the margin engine and the concentration risk are the same fact. Two further risks sit on watch: transformers, switchgear and substations are won in price-led tenders against lower-cost domestic manufacturers, and the generation mix is being reshaped around the company — no coal new-build, gas turbines not localisable, large steam turbines and synchronous condensers not made in India, SF6 switchgear migrating to clean air. Offsetting all this, the service annuity and countercyclical capacity investment give real ballast, and demand shifts and fashion risk are both low. The combined rating is 48 out of 100 — 45% weight on business quality at 72, 30% on multibagger fit at 24, 25% on risk safety at 32 — which lands at Mixed, two stars, with resilience judged sound. The honest reading: a high-quality, high-return business in an expanding market, whose ownership structure and dependence on tendered, policy-driven capital spending keep it out of the compounding category.
