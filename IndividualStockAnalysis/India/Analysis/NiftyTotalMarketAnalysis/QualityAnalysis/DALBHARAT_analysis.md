# Dalmia BharatLtd (DALBHARAT) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Dalmia Bharat Limited makes and sells cement in India — it digs limestone from its own mines, burns it into clinker at its plants, grinds it into cement, bags it and sells it to builders, contractors, infrastructure projects and individual home-builders through a dealer network. The calls describe it as India's fourth-largest cement player, with capacity of roughly 46-50 million tonnes selling into over 23 states, historically strongest in the East, North-East and South and now pushing into the West and Central regions to become a 'pan-India' player. It earns money on the gap between the price per tonne it realises and its cost per tonne, so management talks almost entirely about volumes (7.4 million tonnes in a quarter), realisation per tonne, and EBITDA per tonne (around INR 901 to INR 1,055), with quarterly revenue of roughly INR 3,300-3,900 crores. Because it is essentially a one-product commodity business, its profits swing with cement prices, with fuel costs like petcoke and coal, and with freight — and management's stated levers are being the lowest-cost producer, selling more premium-branded cement, and adding capacity organically and by buying distressed assets.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Cement (the core and effectively the whole business)** — Manufacturing and selling grey cement — mostly blended cement, which was 82% of the mix in FY23 — from clinker units and grinding units across the East, North-East, South and now West/Central India; this is the only revenue-generating line the calls discuss, at close to INR 3,900 crores of quarterly revenue.
- **Premium products / premiumisation** — A higher-priced branded range aimed at retail home-builders and channel partners, including a new 'Weather365' premium-plus product launched in FY27; management says premium products had reached 25% of the portfolio and are a main way to lift realisation per tonne without relying on market price rises.
- **Trade vs non-trade sales mix** — Sales split between the dealer/retail 'trade' channel — about 64% of volumes — and bulk institutional/project sales; trade is the better-priced channel, so the mix is managed deliberately.
- **Captive renewable and waste-heat power** — Solar, wind, floating-solar and waste-heat-recovery capacity built or contracted (including 127 MW under group captive arrangements) not to sell power but to cut the power-and-fuel bill, which is one of the largest cost lines at around INR 1,000 per tonne; renewable share went from 35% toward a targeted 50%, and the company also targets carbon neutrality by 2040.
- **State government incentives** — Subsidies earned for operating plants in incentive states, accrued at roughly INR 45-74 crores a quarter with a large uncollected balance outstanding (INR 683 crores in 2022 rising to INR 822 crores by mid-2026) — a real but slow-paying add-on to cement earnings, not a separate business.
- **Capacity expansion and acquisitions** — An ongoing programme of new plants (Belgaum, Pune, Kadapa, a Chennai bulk terminal) plus buying the Jaypee cement assets, which added 5.2 million tonnes of cement and 3.3 million tonnes of clinker capacity in Central India; management guides capacity from about 50 million tonnes today to roughly 67 million tonnes by Q3 FY28 and an aspiration of 110-130 million tonnes by 2031, funded by capex of about INR 3,200-3,400 crores a year.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Dalmia Bharat comes out of these three reviews as a well-run, growing company in a hard industry, and the combined rating of 48 out of 100 ("Mixed", 2 stars) is the arithmetic of that split: business quality scored 61, multibagger fit only 26, and risk safety 50. The quality framework is strongest exactly where the company controls its own conduct — Growth and Customer Benefits both score a perfect 1.0, Management 0.83 and Capital Allocation 0.75. Sales have grown around 8% a year with unusual consistency, operating profit is ₹21 of every ₹100 of sales with margins moving only about ±3.4 points a year, ₹84 of every ₹100 of sales is left after direct input costs, and only about five days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Two checks score the maximum of 2: Indian cement demand is in a long expansion tied to infrastructure, housing and public capex (management guides 7-8% a year), and there is an unusual supply of weak competitors to take share from — Jaiprakash into insolvency with its assets since bought, Holcim's exit, and small players under sustained margin pressure. But the same framework scores Competitive Advantage at minus 0.33 and Return on Capital at zero, and that is the whole tension in the file: the company operates well, but the industry does not let good operations turn into high returns.

The pattern screen tells the same story from the other side. Only two of eleven patterns are a likely fit, and both are about how the company sells rather than what it owns. "Low Price Plus" is the cleanest — cost leadership runs through all four years of calls, with the lowest total cost per ton claimed in August 2022 at ₹4,360, a ₹150-200 per ton three-year reduction target set in FY25 and then beaten (₹183 reported, ₹211 adjusted by Q4 FY26). "Brand Strength" fits on heritage back to 1939 across 23 states, a refreshed logo and premium sub-brands, and the quality framework separately credits marketing spend with offsetting sector price falls. Everything else lands at PARTIAL, and one — "Toll Roads" — is a flat no, because cement is a bulk commodity and a large share of construction cost, with calls describing sector-wide price declines, discount schemes and an inability to pass on fuel inflation. Crucially, the traits that earn the patterns are the same traits that create the risks. Being the low-cost producer is why "Good Enough Goods" is an ELEVATED risk: in a near-commodity where the dealer largely picks the brand, cost leadership is what lets you survive good-enough pricing, but it also means price is the argument. Premiumisation (premium share 24% to 25%, the Weather365 launch, discount rationalisation, realisation up 6% quarter-on-quarter in Q1 FY27) is simultaneously the reason "Pricing Power" is only partial — for most of the period management flagged multi-quarter price weakness — and the main defence offered against the good-enough-goods risk. And the abundant weak competitors that score a maximum 2 as "share donators" exist because the industry is consolidating, which is precisely what makes "New Entrants" an ELEVATED risk: Adani's arrival via the Holcim exit pushed top-four share from 48% to 55% in fifteen months.

The risk review rates overall resilience SOUND, with four of eight channels LOW or no-signal — technology, stakeholder concentration and fashion risk are all genuine non-issues here, since demand is functional and repeat-purchase, no single customer drives results, and the innovation discussed (83% blended ratio, renewable share from 35% to 48%, waste-heat recovery, captive coal) lowers cost rather than threatening the installed base. The pressure sits in the remaining four. Government Dependency is the only HIGH risk: state capital-subsidy incentives are a recurring profit-and-loss and receivable item, with ₹839cr outstanding at FY26-end and only ₹14cr collected in a quarter because of delays in payouts by a few state governments — the same public capex that earns the maximum score on end-market growth also sits on the balance sheet as money the company is waiting to be paid. Cyclicality is ELEVATED because results are explained across eighteen calls almost entirely through petcoke and coal prices ($218/t in Q1 FY23, $106 in Q1 FY25, back to $160 after the West Asia conflict) and swinging regional prices. These risks land on an asset-heavy base, which is where the zero score on Return on Capital comes from: every ₹1 of assets produces ₹0.45 of sales, roughly ₹2,500cr of capital work-in-progress sits idle, utilisation is in the 60s-70s, and core cement return on capital is 12.1% even after a 200 basis point improvement. Growth capex of ₹3,000-4,000cr a year is taking capacity from 49 to 61.5 to 67MT via Belgaum, Kadapa and Pune, and the geographic move from a South and East base into Central (Jaypee) and West is credible and executing — but the pan-India roadmap has slipped repeatedly (75MT pushed from FY27 to FY28), analysts have flagged continued share loss, and management has pivoted toward profitability language, which is why "Market Share Gainers" stays partial. Management's own conduct is the offset the file keeps returning to: a prompt ₹113cr provision against Jaypee, net debt to EBITDA held at 0.17x-1.47x against a stated 2x limit, delivery of multi-year cost guidance, and a smooth 16-month CFO transition.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Cyclicality, New Entrants, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.4× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.

- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 5% to 7% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Fortunes are repeatedly tied to the state: state capital-subsidy incentives are a recurring P&L and receivable item (Rs839cr outstanding at FY26-end, with collections of only Rs14cr in a quarter 'due to delays in pay-outs by a few state governments'), demand is framed around central/state capex and Viksit Bharat, election quarters visibly depress volumes twice (Q1FY25, Q1FY27), and the business rests on government-granted limestone mining leases/auctions with policy costs like the Tamil Nadu mineral levy of Rs160/t plus an ED land-attachment case.

> "collections for the quarter were subdued at just INR14 crores. This was due to delays in pay-outs by a few state governments on account of elections" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Prices are market-determined, not regulator-set, and the footprint spans 23 states, so no single state's policy is decisive*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but none of the 3 computed fingerprints confirms it in the numbers yet — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Cement is a classic construction/commodity cycle: across the 18 calls management explains results almost entirely through petcoke/coal prices ($218/t in Q1FY23, $106 in Q1FY25, back to $160 after the West Asia conflict) and swinging regional cement prices, with EBITDA/t moving from ~Rs901 to Rs1,055 and pricing weak for 7-8 straight months in FY25; simultaneously the company is in a heavy multi-year capex cycle (49mt to 75mt to 110-130mt, Rs3,200-3,400cr capex a year plus the Rs2,850cr Jaypee buy) funded into an uncertain price environment.

> "Petcoke prices have soared to about $160 per ton, and Rupee depreciation is an added impact... we are looking at a risk of 125 to 150 on the horizon" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Cement is a flow/consumption product tied to ongoing construction rather than a single customer's capex; countercyclical investing (buying Jaypee assets out of insolvency, expanding while prices were weak) plus a low-cost position and sub-2x net-debt/EBITDA discipline*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 1 of the last 9 years with swings of ±7 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
