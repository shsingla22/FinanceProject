# Ceat Ltd. (CEATLTD) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: AUTOMOBILE*

CEAT Limited is an Indian tyre manufacturer that makes and sells tyres for two- and three-wheelers, passenger cars and SUVs, trucks and buses (both bias and radial), farm/agricultural vehicles, and off-highway equipment. It sells through three routes: the replacement market (aftermarket buyers replacing worn tyres via its distribution network, including rural dealers and a small direct-to-consumer online channel), OEMs (vehicle makers such as Maruti Suzuki, Mahindra, Renault-Nissan and Daimler who fit CEAT tyres on new vehicles), and international markets (Europe, the U.S., Middle East, Brazil, Vietnam, Peru and others). It earns money on the spread between tyre selling prices and raw material costs — mainly natural rubber and crude-linked inputs — so profit swings with commodity prices and how fast it can push through price increases, with roughly 20% of standalone revenue from international sales and about 23% including CAMSO. Manufacturing is centred in India (Halol, Chennai, Ambernath, Nagpur) plus off-highway operations in Sri Lanka, with quarterly standalone revenue around INR4,163 crore and EBITDA margins that have run between roughly 9% and 15%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Replacement (aftermarket)** — Selling tyres to existing vehicle owners through dealers and distributors — described as a core growth engine that grew in the mid-teens in recent quarters, with a distribution advantage in rural India where growth ran 40%-50% faster than urban.
- **OEM** — Supplying tyres fitted onto new vehicles at the factory, spanning two-wheelers, passenger cars, trucks/buses and farm vehicles; management says CEAT holds close to 50% share of business in two-wheeler OEMs and around 25%-29% share on electric-vehicle launches.
- **International Business (exports)** — Exporting tyres to Europe, the U.S., Middle East, Latin America and other markets, which management calls a core focus area and margin-accretive; it has risen to about 20% of standalone revenue (23% with CAMSO) and grew around 30% in the latest quarter, supported by new branches in Germany, the U.K., France and Poland.
- **Two-wheeler and three-wheeler tyres** — Motorcycle and scooter tyres, one of the strongest-growing categories (replacement volumes up in the mid-20s in one quarter), with a premium sub-segment of radials and tyres for 250cc-plus bikes.
- **Passenger car and SUV (PC/UV) tyres** — Car and SUV tyres where management pushes 'premiumisation' via 17-inch-plus rim sizes; premium 17-inch-plus replacement volumes reportedly doubled in one quarter and the company targets market leadership in the category.
- **Truck and bus tyres (TBR and bias)** — Commercial vehicle tyres, split between newer truck-bus radials (TBR) and older bias tyres; a large commercial category that grew in low teens in replacement and has recently begun launching top-of-the-line premium products offering lower total cost of ownership.
- **Farm / agricultural tyres** — Tyres for tractors and farm equipment, tied to rural incomes and harvests, with capacity expansion at the Ambernath agri-radial plant and OEM approvals from names like AGCO Massey Ferguson and John Deere Brazil.
- **Off-highway tyres (OHT) / specialty** — Tyres for construction, mining and other off-road machinery, sold heavily into European and U.S. aftermarkets; the company launched 130-plus new OHT SKUs in one year and treats it as a high-realisation specialty business.
- **CAMSO** — An acquired premium off-highway tyre and rubber-track brand (operations in Sri Lanka) closed around May 2025, described as running roughly USD10 million of quarterly topline at customer prices; it is still in a multi-quarter transition where CEAT sells through Michelin and buys semi-finished goods from Michelin, with full value-chain control expected by FY28.
- **CEAT Sri Lanka OHT** — An overseas manufacturing entity making off-highway tyres and tracks in Sri Lanka; it carries about USD80 million of debt, and Sri Lankan rupee depreciation cost roughly INR48 crore in one quarter, which is why consolidated profit lagged standalone profit.
- **Digital / D2C and AI** — Online and direct-to-consumer selling plus internal digitisation — D2C was about 6% of passenger replacement sales in FY23 (peaking 8%-9%), and the company runs WEF 'Lighthouse'-recognised smart factories at Chennai and Halol alongside an enterprise data lake and agentic-AI programmes.

### The story in depth

CEAT comes out of these three reviews as a solid, well-run tyre maker rather than an exceptional one — a combined score of 62 out of 100, or "Decent," with resilience judged "Sound." The strongest things about it are operational and cultural. Sales have compounded at about 10% a year with unusually steady year-to-year growth, and the company keeps converting that into measured share gains in both replacement and original-equipment channels. Customers effectively fund the business: cash comes back 21 days before suppliers have to be paid. Every ₹100 of capital in the business generated ₹21 of operating cash this year. Management scores well on candour — it gives detailed forward guidance on raw materials and pricing, admits when its own forecasts were wrong, and volunteers the awkward items like VRS charges and working-capital deterioration. Growth spending has scaled from roughly ₹700 crore in FY23 to ₹1,076 crore in FY26, guided to ₹1,300–1,400 crore for FY27, plus a board-approved ₹1,205 crore two-wheeler expansion, funded largely from money the business generates itself.

Those same strengths explain why the multibagger screen lands where it does — only 48 out of 100. Just one of eleven patterns is a strong fit (Corporate Culture: promoter-family ownership, an MD who has been inside the company since 2005, and capex promises set and honoured year after year), and one more is a likely fit (Market Share Gainers, backed by quantified numbers — around half the two-wheeler OEM business, 29% share in passenger EVs, replacement share up 0.5–1%, international at 19% of sales). Eight patterns sit at partial, and two are outright no-fits. The interesting connection is that the trait driving the share gains is the same one blocking the higher-quality patterns: CEAT wins by being a "quality value player," giving customers more for less. That underpins the Low Price Plus and Market Share Gainers patterns, but it is precisely why Pricing Power is a no-fit — pricing is cost pass-through with a lag, not pricing ahead of cost inflation. The heavy, consistent advertising spend (IPL and WPL partnerships, a top-10 Brand Finance ranking, doubled premium lead generation) earns a good mark as a check and shows up in share and premium mix, but Brand Strength stays only partial because the brand buys attention, not insulation from price competition. Toll Roads is a clear no-fit for the same underlying reason: tyres are a big, negotiated line item for fleets and carmakers, not an invisible cost nobody argues about. That is also why the Industry Structure quality area scores zero and Return on Capital only a third.

The risk review is the mirror image of the pattern review, and it flags the same fault line. Cyclicality is the one elevated risk: across fourteen calls, margins swing with the rubber and crude cycle rather than with volumes — EBITDA went 13.1%, then 10.5%, then 14.6%, then 9.1% — and in the most recent quarter a 16–18% raw-material surge was met with only 7–11% of price taken. That is the Pricing Power no-fit showing up as a P&L outcome. Good Enough Goods is on watch for the same reason: realisation depends on rivals following price hikes in a category where dealers sway the buyer's choice. Three other exposures are watch-level rather than alarming. Policy moves the business in both directions — the September 2025 GST cut from 28% to 18% was called the decisive turning point for FY26 volumes, while US tariffs sit at 25% plus 4% on on-road tyres and off-highway duties swung from 50% to 10%. The CAMSO acquisition currently routes an entire acquired book of business through Michelin as a middleman, which takes cost plus margin out of what CEAT realises until the transition finishes. Against that, the risks that would be genuinely dangerous are low: demand is functional and repeat-purchase, tied to vehicle numbers and fleet age rather than fashion, and the big technology shift — electrification — is one CEAT is capturing (roughly a quarter to 29% OEM EV share) rather than being disrupted by. The capital intensity that limits returns is also the thing that keeps new entrants out — ₹1,200 crore for a single two-wheeler capacity block — so the low return on capital and the barrier to entry are two readings of one fact.

## What to watch

- Cyclicality plus Pricing Power (no-fit): whether the gap in the latest quarter — a 16–18% raw-material surge against only 7–11% of price taken, with EBITDA down to 9.1% — closes as price hikes stick, or becomes the normal pattern.

- Capex for growth check: whether the ₹1,300–1,400 crore guided for FY27 and the ₹1,205 crore two-wheeler expansion stay funded from internal accruals without eroding the 21% cash return on capital or the 21-day negative working capital advantage.

- Government Dependency risk: durability of the GST cut's volume boost and the direction of US tariffs (25% plus 4% on on-road, off-highway having swung from 50% to 10%), given international sales are now 19% of the business.
