# Birlasoft Ltd (BSOFT) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Birlasoft is an India-based IT services company that gets paid by large companies to build, modernise and run their business software and technology systems — things like SAP/ERP implementations, digital and data projects, cloud migration, IT infrastructure support and, more recently, AI and 'agentic AI' solutions built on its own Cogito platform. It earns money project-by-project and through multi-year managed-service contracts, which it reports as new deal wins or total contract value (TCV) — roughly $160-210 million of TCV signed per quarter recently — that then convert into billed revenue as teams of engineers work on them. Revenue is about $150 million a quarter (FY26 full-year revenue of Rs. 53,100 million, roughly flat to slightly down), earned mostly in US dollars from clients in the Americas, with smaller businesses in Europe and the Rest of the World, while most of the delivery work is done offshore in India by a workforce of over 12,000 people. Profitability depends on utilisation of staff, offshore versus onshore mix, and fixed-price versus time-based contracts — EBITDA margin was 16.3% for FY26 versus 13% the year before.

**The parts of the business:**

- **ERP** — Implements and upgrades enterprise resource planning software, especially SAP (including cloud-based 'RISE with SAP'), and management calls it a large, historically important service line whose project slowdowns have repeatedly dragged the whole company's revenue down; a 25-year SAP veteran was hired in FY26 specifically to return it to growth.
- **Digital & Data** — Covers digital transformation, data modernisation and AI/GenAI work — the faster-growing, higher-value part of the business that management describes as making Birlasoft an 'enterprise digital company' and that now anchors most of its marquee deal wins.
- **Infra** — IT infrastructure and support services — described as a newer, smaller service line that management is deliberately building, and which delivered strong sequential growth from fresh engagements.
- **Manufacturing (vertical)** — Serving manufacturing clients is one of the company's biggest end-markets and the source of most of its ERP revenue, so its ups and downs swing overall results; management notes exposure is 'higher to manufacturing-led customers'.
- **BFSI (banking, financial services and insurance) (vertical)** — Work for banks, payments/card companies and insurers, including AI and GenAI deployments such as scaling GenAI services in the North American property & casualty insurance market; described as one of the steadier verticals and a repeated growth driver.
- **Life Sciences & Services (vertical)** — Serves pharmaceutical, medtech and related clients — including a multi-year AI and data modernisation programme for a Fortune 500 MedTech customer — and is grouped by management with Manufacturing as a 'manufacturing-led' exposure.
- **Energy & Utilities (vertical)** — A smaller vertical serving energy and utility clients, which management repeatedly cites alongside BFSI as one of the steadier, growing parts of the portfolio.
- **Geographies** — The Americas is by far the main market and where the largest accounts and sales investment sit, with Europe and a smaller 'Rest of the World' business that grew strongly off a low base.
- **Large-account mining** — Not a segment but a stated revenue engine: the top 5, 10 and 20 clients contributed about 36%, 53% and 65% of revenue respectively, from an active client base of roughly 300 accounts, so growth depends heavily on expanding within existing large customers.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Birlasoft is a roughly $600 million Indian IT services company that is, by the record here, well run at the level of housekeeping and honest about its own shortfalls. The balance sheet carries no debt and INR 2,637 crore of cash, the strategy is purely organic with no acquisitions, and the company kept investing through the downturn while paying a consistent dividend (INR 6.50 for FY26) out of its own cash generation. Out of every INR 100 of sales, INR 16 is operating profit, and that figure has moved only about two points either way in a year, which points to costs genuinely being under control. Management talks plainly: the current CEO owns bad results out loud, including admitting the company has 'consistently not performed' in its ERP business, a clear change from the FY23 management that denied execution problems while missing targets. The calls are numbers-led with no personality-driven claims, and detail is handed to the CFO and functional heads. That combination is why the overall resilience read is SOUND and why management and return on capital are the only two areas of the quality framework scoring positive.

The trouble is that none of this housekeeping has turned into a business that compounds. Across the eleven multibagger patterns, not one is a full fit: five are outright no-fits and the rest are only partial. The single headline achievement of FY26 — 333 basis points of margin expansion to 16.3% — is what earns the partial marks, and it is exactly where the strengths and the doubts collide. It is a real cost programme (moving work offshore, cutting small unprofitable accounts), but management itself attributes much of it to currency, one-off items and revenue it chose to exit, which is why the framework flags a halo effect: with the top line shrinking 6% in dollar terms, skill cannot be cleanly separated from accounting tailwinds. The same offshore and fixed-price shift that lifted margins also lowers the price per unit of work, so it simultaneously explains the positive margin story and the negative marks on pricing and market share. Growth here is not price-led at all — clients push work offshore on price, and AI deals require handing productivity savings to the customer up front, which is why Pricing Power, Brand Strength and Market Share Gainers are all rejected outright and why revenue fell while peers grew.

The risk review lands on the same fault lines rather than on new ones. Four of eight channels are elevated. Demand is cyclical and, on the company's own telling across four years, results turn on the customer spending cycle — delayed project starts, deferred ERP ramps, furloughs — which is also why the Recurring Revenue and Toll Roads patterns fail: the work is project-based by management's stated preference, and customers treat it as a line they can squeeze. Much of the base is commoditised staff augmentation and standard ERP and infrastructure work priced against cheaper alternatives, the direct counterpart of the pricing findings above. Revenue is concentrated, with the top 5, 10 and 20 clients at 36.2%, 52.6% and 65.1% of sales, and single relationships have visibly swung whole quarters. Technology is the sharpest edge: analysts ask directly whether AI and Palantir's compression of ERP migration work are deflating the ERP business, and management concedes FY26 revenue was dented by productivity given away on AI deals — so the same AI push that earns a partial innovation mark is also the elevated technology risk. Nothing in four years of calls describes a barrier a funded competitor could not rebuild, and the leadership rebuild through external Tier-1 hires (COO, ERP, Data/AI, Life Sciences, Americas) both signals a thin internal bench and underlines that the differentiators are hireable. The combined score of 36 out of 100 — Mixed, two stars — reflects that split: a safe balance sheet and candid stewardship sitting on top of a franchise question that is still open.
