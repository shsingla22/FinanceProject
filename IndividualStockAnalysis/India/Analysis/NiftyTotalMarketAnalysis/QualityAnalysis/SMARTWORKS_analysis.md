# Smartworks Coworking (SMARTWORKS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Smartworks leases entire large bare-shell office buildings from developers and landlords in prime locations, fits them out into fully managed, branded campuses with amenities like cafeterias, gyms, crèches, convenience stores and recreation zones, and then rents desks and offices to companies under a single contract, one SLA and one invoice. Its customers are mid-to-large enterprises — Fortune 500 and Forbes 2000 companies, MNCs, GCCs (global capability centres), Indian conglomerates and well-funded unicorns — who typically take 300+ seats and sign four-to-five-year agreements, giving revenue an annuity-like, highly predictable character; enterprises make up about 90% of rental revenue. Money comes almost entirely from recurring rental income (about 94% of revenue in FY26), with a small and growing slice from ancillary/value-added services including Fitout-as-a-Service. It operates across India (56 centres in 15 cities at listing, growing to roughly 17 million sq ft of total area and 10.4 million sq ft operational by Q1 FY27, with over 760 clients and 150,000 professionals on-site), plus a small international presence in Singapore.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Managed office / core rental business** — Leasing whole buildings, converting them into Smartworks campuses and renting fully-serviced seats to enterprises on long tenures — this is the core, making up about 94% of revenue and the bulk of the ~INR 546 crore quarterly top line in Q1 FY27.
- **Ancillary / value-added services (VAS) including Fitout-as-a-Service (FaaS)** — Extra services sold on top of the office space, notably designing and building out client fit-outs; management calls it still 'a very small part of total revenue' at about INR 27.5 crore in Q3 FY26 (out of INR 66 crore of other operating revenue), but expects it to increasingly support growth and margins.
- **SmartVantage platform (GCC solutions)** — A platform launched around FY26 that sells global companies a broader solution for setting up India capability centres rather than just space; management credits it with over four 1,000+ seat mega GCC deals in nine months, and GCC clients grew to 21% of rental revenue by Q1 FY27 from 15% in FY26.
- **International operations (Singapore)** — A small overseas arm running over 1,500 seats in Singapore after acquiring Workstudio, at high occupancy, self-funded from international cash flows with no risk taken on the Indian balance sheet.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Smartworks sells certainty to companies, and almost everything good about the business flows from that one idea. It takes bare-shell buildings, fits them out to a standard design library, and hands an enterprise client a ready campus in under two months against an industry norm of six to nine, on one contract, one service agreement and one invoice. Because the customer base is roughly 90% corporate rather than individual, that promise of reliability and low total cost is the right thing to sell, and it shows up in the numbers: about 94% of revenue is annuity rent on four-to-five-year contracts, billed in advance, with debtor days of only five to six and negative working capital, and 87% of FY27 revenue already contracted. Retention runs above 80-85% and no campus has ever been surrendered. This is why the screen calls Recurring Revenue a strong fit, and it is the same trait that earns top marks on the assurance and convenience checks. The growth story sits on top of it — the company is growing 44% while flex overall grows 23% and the wider office market grows 6%, taking share from a shrinking tail of small operators who cannot secure large buildings, inside an end market that is itself expanding. Founder-led since 2016, self-funded, virtually debt-free at INR 5.6cr net debt, with return on capital employed moving from 13% at listing to 21.5% in a year and promises made in one year delivered by the next — hence strong marks on both the culture pattern and the stewardship check.

The catch is that the very things that make the model work are also what limit it. Cost leadership and a standardised, modular product give the company its lowest-cost position and its Low Price Plus fit — but a standardised fit-out of a leased building is, by the framework's own reading, replicable, which is why the 'good enough goods' risk is flagged and why the cost-to-replicate barrier only partly holds: the buildings are leased, not owned, and the moat is really finite prime supply, developer relationships and a balance sheet, secured through 10-15 year rights and buildings locked for FY27-29. The same cost-leadership stance explains why pricing power scores no fit at all: management explicitly says margin expansion comes from occupancy maturity and operating leverage 'without any material pricing action', and positions the company as the value-priced provider with only a contractual annual escalation. Brokers still originate a meaningful share of deals at about 2.5% of rental revenue, which supports the friendly-middleman pattern while simultaneously being the reason a third party sits between the company and its client. And the annuity structure cuts both ways: long, fixed rent owed to landlords is matched against enterprise office demand that is discretionary and cycle-sensitive, so cyclicality is on watch — sharpened by a fresh, heavy capital spending cycle of INR 550-600cr guided for FY27, up 66% year-on-year, and by occupancy slipping to 81%. The resilience read is STRAINED for exactly this reason, and it is the same fixed-rent-against-employee-seats exposure that puts shifting workplace preferences on watch, since the company itself concedes clients moved out during work-from-home.

Where the risk review is reassuring, it is reassuring in a way that fits the same story. Concentration is low and getting lower — top ten clients are only about 21% of rental revenue and falling even as deal sizes grow, no client is above 30% of a campus, there are 760-plus clients, IT exposure has been deliberately cut, and there are multiple landlords. New entrants are not the threat because flex is itself the new format resetting office economics and the industry is consolidating toward the largest operators, with the top ten operators' share of flex leasing rising from 67% to 72% to 74%. Technology shows up as a tool the company owns rather than a threat, and demand is functional and contracted rather than taste-driven, so fashion risk is low. The combined rating of 82 — Outstanding — is carried mostly by the pattern fit at 95, with business quality at 74 and risk safety at 80; and within business quality, the thin spots are precisely the ones the risk review points at, with growth, industry structure and management scoring lowest of the seven areas. In plain terms: an unusually clean, contracted, cash-efficient way to ride a genuinely growing market, run by owners who have done what they said, but one whose earnings rest on keeping buildings full and costs lowest rather than on charging more, while it spends heavily into a cycle it does not control.
