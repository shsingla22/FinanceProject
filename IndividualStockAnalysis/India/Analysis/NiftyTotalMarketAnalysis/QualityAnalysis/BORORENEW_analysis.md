# Borosil Renewables (BORORENEW) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Borosil Renewables makes solar tempered glass — the thick protective glass sheet that goes on the front of a solar panel — and sells it in bulk to companies that assemble solar modules. It runs glass furnaces at its plant in Jhagadia, Bharuch district, Gujarat, and earns money on the volume of glass melted and sold, priced per millimetre of thickness per square metre (about INR 160.3/mm in the June 2026 quarter, up from about INR 105–141 in earlier years), so profits swing with selling prices, fuel and soda ash costs, and how full the furnaces run. Most sales are to Indian module makers, with a small export slice (7.4% of FY26 sales, down to 2.8% in Q1 FY27 as export markets stayed weak). Its fortunes are tied closely to Indian government policy — import duties, anti-dumping and countervailing duties on Chinese, Vietnamese and Malaysian glass, and the ALMM local-content rules — because roughly 70% of India's solar glass is still imported; standalone FY26 sales were INR 1,534.83 crores with EBITDA of INR 491.68 crores (32% of sales).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Solar tempered glass (India / standalone operations)** — The core and now essentially the only revenue-generating business — glass furnaces at Jhagadia, Gujarat producing tempered glass for solar modules; it accounts for effectively all of the company's INR 1,534.83 crores FY26 sales, and a 600 tonnes-per-day expansion at the same site is due to commission in Q4 FY27, which management says will lift sales by about 60%.
- **Overseas subsidiaries (Interfloat / GMB, Germany)** — A European solar glass manufacturing arm acquired around 2022 that was hit by cheap Chinese module imports killing off German module makers; it swung between roughly INR 55–130 crores of quarterly revenue with negative EBITDA in FY25, then GMB filed for insolvency and the company wrote off its INR 325.91 crores exposure in FY26 — by Q1 FY27 the overseas subsidiaries generated no revenue at all.
- **Captive renewable power** — Solar-plus-wind hybrid power plants set up to supply the glass plant's own electricity — a 10 MW project via an SPV (BRL holding 31.2%) targeted for December 2022 and a further 16.5 MW hybrid plant commissioned in March 2026 — which by Q1 FY27 covered 93% of the company's power needs and cuts energy cost rather than earning outside revenue.
- **Rooftop solutions division** — A new, small trading-style business announced in the May 2026 call to sell rooftop solar products sourced from outside suppliers under the Borosil brand and distribution network, aimed at the PM Surya Ghar Yojana and commercial/industrial rooftop market, with no capex planned initially and no revenue reported yet.

### The story in depth

Borosil Renewables makes tempered solar glass, and on the money it earns today it looks good: every ₹100 of capital in the business (shareholders' funds plus borrowings) threw off ₹25 of operating cash this year, and ₹28 of every ₹100 of sales is operating profit. It sells into a market that is genuinely growing — annual solar installations rose from 23.8 GW to 44.6 GW, module capacity went from 11 GW five years ago to 203 GW, with ALMM mandates plus electric vehicles, data centres and green hydrogen adding to demand. The company is expanding into that growth, taking capacity from 450 tonnes a day to 1,000 and on to 1,600 by March 2027, and management earned its stripes with four years of self-directed lobbying that delivered exactly what it promised: it formed the Solar Ancillary Manufacturers Association and then won a 10% basic customs duty in October 2024 and anti-dumping duties on China and Vietnam in December 2024. That is why the multibagger screen marks Market Share Gainers as a likely fit. But the combined rating comes out at 36 out of 100 — Mixed, two stars — because the returns and the growth sit on foundations the company does not own.

The single most important thing to understand is that the same fact is simultaneously the best news and the biggest risk. The duty wall is what turned realisations around — prices fell from ₹128 per mm in Q1 FY24 to ₹99.6 by Q4 FY24, then recovered to ₹150-160 in FY26 and ₹160.3 by Q1 FY27 — and management itself attributes that turnaround to the anti-dumping and customs duties, not to anything the product does better. So the strength that supports the volume story is also what makes Government Dependency a high risk, and it is precisely why the screen rules out Cost to Replicate: duties can lapse or be renegotiated, and they are not a wall the company built. The same doubling-back shows up in pricing. The company prices at Chinese landed cost plus 3-5%, which means there is no pricing power, no toll-road position (glass is a big line item for module makers, not a trivial one they accept without argument), and the cheaper import remains a genuinely good-enough substitute — an elevated risk on its own. There are also no network effects: this is one industrial commodity sold to a concentrated set of buyers, with the top 10 customers at 65-68% of volume. That is how the competitive advantage area ends up at the bottom of the scale. The one place the company is trying to build something of its own — leaning on the Borosil name to launch a branded rooftop solutions division — is what earns partial marks on both Brand Strength and Forward Integrators, and it is also management's answer to policy dependence. But at a target of roughly ₹36 crore it is small, and it rides the PM Surya Ghar government scheme, so the escape route runs through the same door as the risk.

The record on deploying capital outside the core is poor, and it is poor in a way that connects directly to the risks. The flagship move abroad — the cross-border Interfloat/GMB acquisition in Europe — was bought with an asset that had suffered a furnace leak before closing, bled losses of ₹33.95 crore of negative EBITDA in one quarter, shut German production from July 2025 and was written off at ₹325.91 crore; meanwhile exports fell from 23.3% of turnover in Q1 FY23 to 2.8% by Q4 FY26. That single episode is what drags down both the geographic expansion and the acquisition checks. It is also, oddly, the strongest evidence for the partial fit on Corporate Culture: leadership has been stable from August 2022 through July 2026 and explained the failure openly and in detail rather than burying it. Elsewhere the pressures compound. Earnings are highly cyclical — operating margins have run 50.1%, then 30.2%, 5.8%, 12.3%, 16% in FY25, 32% in FY26 and 35%, meaning loss-making quarters sat only two years before record ones — and about 96 days of cash stays tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Competition is arriving in force: imports still hold about 70% of Indian consumption, four rival domestic plants added 1,300 tonnes a day in a single year, and domestic capacity is set to nearly triple from 2,600 to 7,700 tonnes a day by March 2027 with Reliance among the entrants — the opposite of operating in a quiet corner nobody has noticed. The resilience read is still sound, and the cost programme is real (dual-fuel furnaces, captive solar and wind taking renewable power to 93% of requirement by July 2026), but taken together this is a well-run, well-timed, high-return operator in a commodity whose current profitability depends on government protection and a favourable point in the cycle, not on an advantage competitors cannot copy.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK

**What the calls show:** Realisations and margins have swung violently across the 17 calls — ASP Rs.140.8/mm (Q1FY23) → Rs.99.6 (Q4FY24) → Rs.160.3 (Q1FY27), with EBITDA margin going 50.1% → 30.2% → 5.8% → 12.3% → 16% (FY25) → 32% (FY26) → 35%, i.e. loss-making quarters two years before record profits. ### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Across every call the fortunes of the business turn on policy: ADD on China/Vietnam, CVD on Malaysia/Vietnam, the 10% basic customs duty from Oct 2024, ALMM-I/II/III, PLI and the PM Surya Ghar scheme.
