# Bharti Hexacom (BHARTIHEXA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Bharti Hexacom is an Indian telecom operator listed on the NSE and BSE (symbol BHARTIHEXA, IPO completed shortly before the May 2024 call) that runs under the Airtel brand and reports alongside Bharti Airtel on the same joint quarterly earnings calls, with offices in Gurugram and New Delhi. Per those calls it sells connectivity services to Indian consumers and businesses: prepaid and postpaid mobile plans (including 5G), home broadband over fiber and fixed-wireless access, television service via DTH and now IPTV, and enterprise/B2B connectivity sold as 'Airtel Business'. It makes money mainly from recurring monthly subscriptions and usage — management tracks it as ARPU (average revenue per user, quoted as Rs.209 in Q4 FY2024 rising to Rs.259 by Q3 FY2026) multiplied by a customer base that grows through net adds, plus enterprise contracts — and profitability is discussed as EBITDA/EBITDAaL margin against heavy network capex on towers, 5G sites and fiber. Note that the calls are shared Airtel-plus-Hexacom events and nearly all figures quoted are Airtel's India or consolidated numbers; the transcripts never break out Hexacom's own revenue, geography or customer count separately.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Mobility / mobile business** — Prepaid and postpaid mobile voice and data, including a 5G network that management says reached 181 million 5G customers and about 74% population coverage by Q3 FY2026; described on the calls as the largest piece, with India mobile at 56% of Airtel's consolidated revenue in FY2025 and 52% in FY2026.
- **Homes / broadband** — Fixed home internet delivered both by fiber to the home (FTTH) and fixed wireless access (FWA), with over 13 million connected homes and over 3 million FWA customers by Q3 FY2026 — a fast-growing segment management repeatedly calls a strategic priority where it 'needs to raise its game'.
- **Digital TV (DTH and IPTV)** — Pay-television service, historically satellite DTH — an industry facing 'macro headwinds' where the company has stopped subsidising set-top boxes — now increasingly IPTV delivered over its own broadband, adding roughly 73,000–76,000 customers a quarter and used to bundle TV with internet ('convergence').
- **Airtel Business (B2B / enterprise)** — Connectivity and network services sold to companies, including global capacity from subsea cables landed in FY2025; revenue of about Rs.5,316 crore in Q4 FY2025 and Rs.5,350 crore in Q3 FY2026, and deliberately shrunk for a period as management exited 'commoditized low margin' work.
- **Digital businesses / new growth bets** — A smaller portfolio of newer services — Airtel Cloud (a 'telco-grade sovereign cloud' with 24 deals signed in FY2026), cybersecurity, IoT and CPaaS — described by management as early-stage but receiving additional investment.
- **Financial services (Airtel Payments Bank, Airtel Money)** — Digital payments and lending: the Payments Bank had about 120 million monthly transacting users, roughly Rs.3,400 crore annualised revenue and over Rs.4,600 crore of deposits by Q4 FY2026, and subsidiary Airtel Money received RBI approval to operate as a non-deposit-taking NBFC, with loan disbursements running over Rs.550 crore a month.
- **Data centres (Nxtra)** — A data-centre business targeting 1 gigawatt of capacity over the next few years, which raised $1 billion from outside investors alongside Airtel in FY2026; still small relative to connectivity but named one of three priority 'adjacencies'.
- **Passive infrastructure (Indus Towers) and Airtel Africa** — Group-level items that appear in the consolidated numbers discussed on these calls — the tower business Indus at about 6-7% of revenue and African mobile operations at 24-29% — though the calls give no indication these belong to Hexacom itself rather than to Bharti Airtel.

## The verdict: Strong — 77 out of 100 ★★★★☆

In one breath: the quality framework finds an excellent business today (80/100); it strongly fits 4 of the 11 patterns long-term winners share; the risk review found 1 high risk (Government Dependency). Weighing those together gives 77 out of 100 — strong.

**The exact arithmetic:** Overall = 45% × 80 (business quality) + 30% × 86 (multibagger fit) + 25% × 60 (risk safety) = 77 out of 100 → Strong (4 stars).

- **Business quality (80/100):** The 34-check quality framework scored the business +1.20 on its −2 (poor) to +2 (excellent) scale, with 94% of checks backed by evidence; mapped onto 0–100 that is 80 points.
- **Multibagger fit (86/100):** the foundation test partly passed (+10); 4 patterns fit strongly (Low Price Plus, Market Share Gainers, Corporate Culture, Cost to Replicate) (+60); 2 likely (+16) → 86 of 100.
- **Risk safety (60/100):** started from a clean 100; 1 high risk (Government Dependency) (−20); 5 worth watching (−20) → 60 of 100.

### The story in depth

The core story here is a low-cost operator taking business away from weaker rivals in a market that is still growing on its own. The quality framework scores full marks for market share gain, share donators, persistence of growth (sales up about 15% a year, very steadily) and structural end-market growth (connected homes moving from 46 million toward 100 million, 5G and smartphone upgrades, and data-centre demand behind a 1GW build plan). The multibagger screen reads the same facts and finds four strong fits that reinforce each other: Low Price Plus, resting on the repeated 'war on waste' with itemised savings of Rs.2,500 Crores of network cost in FY2024 and Rs.2,200 Crores in FY2025 and site running costs down over 6% in four years despite adding 5G and 45,000 rural sites; Market Share Gainers, with every circle gaining share in FY2024 and again in FY2025 and FY2026 closing at a lifetime-high mobility revenue market share; Cost to Replicate, built on licensed spectrum, 1,43,000 km of fiber laid over three years, 45 million home passes, subsea cable landings and a 1 GW data-centre build funded by a $1 billion raise; and Corporate Culture, evidenced by promises kept — capex guided lower and delivered lower, Rs.42,000 Crores of high-cost DoT debt prepaid, borrowings down from 2.86 times to roughly 1 times earnings, a rating upgrade and a raised dividend. The management checks sit underneath all of this: candour about the company's own shortfalls, disciplined stewardship, an internal chief executive succession telegraphed a year ahead, and one unchanged five-pillar strategy across all nine calls.

The most important thing to notice is that several of these strengths and several of the risks are the same fact seen from two sides. The rock-bottom tariffs that make Low Price Plus work — 'one of the lowest globally' in management's own words — are also why Pricing Power only rates a likely fit rather than a strong one: the rise in average revenue per user from Rs.209 to around Rs.257 comes mostly from customers upgrading rather than from the company raising prices, and management openly says it cannot price at will. Those same low tariffs are the direct residue of a structurally different new entrant resetting the industry's economics, which is why New Entrants stays on the watch list and why the return on capital was only 9.5% in FY2024. The same commoditisation drives the Good Enough Goods watch risk, visible in the decision to walk away from low-margin business at the cost of a reported revenue decline. And the ability to fix any of this rests with the regulator, not the company — which is why Government Dependency is the single high risk: spectrum, licence fees and payment obligations all come from the government, and the whole financial-recovery narrative hinges on permitted tariff repair. That is also what holds the Toll Roads pattern to partial despite an essential service at a tiny share of the customer's wallet in a three-player market. Likewise, the fiber, sites and spectrum that create the replication barrier are exactly what must be rebuilt every technology generation — 5G capex absorbed with 'no monetization in sight', a bet on one 5G approach later re-platformed, and satellite broadband arriving as a parallel route — which is the Technological Innovation watch risk.

Two things temper the picture. First, the returns arithmetic is split: the headline check is excellent — Rs.34 of operating cash for every Rs.100 of capital in the business — yet Return on Capital scores lowest of the seven quality areas at 0.33, with Capital Allocation at 0.8 and Industry Structure at 0.67, and management itself names weak return on capital as a shortfall. The cash is real; the accounting return has not yet caught up. Second, Bharti Hexacom is a two-circle operator in Rajasthan and the North East whose brand, network technology, systems, management and even its earnings call belong to Airtel — every one of the nine calls is an Airtel call with a Hexacom section attached. That parentage is precisely what supplies the Brand Strength and Cost to Replicate patterns, and it is precisely what makes Stakeholder Concentration a watch risk; investors cannot have one without the other. Elsewhere the risk picture is calmer: cyclicality and fashion risk are both low because demand is essential, subscription-based and repeat-purchase, with convergence bundles cutting churn by about half, and the erosion of linear television is a real but bounded headwind that the company is answering with its own internet television and bundled streaming. Netting it out, the combined rating is 77 out of 100 — 80 for business quality, 86 for multibagger fit and 60 for risk safety — a Strong, four-star verdict with resilience judged sound: a genuinely advantaged operator whose main uncertainty is not execution but who sets its prices.

### Toll Roads — PARTIAL
*"Small cost, can't skip it"*
