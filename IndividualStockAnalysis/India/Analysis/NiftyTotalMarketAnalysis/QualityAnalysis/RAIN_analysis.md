# Rain Industries (RAIN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Rain Industries is a Hyderabad-based company that makes industrial raw materials, above all the two carbon products — calcined petroleum coke (CPC) and coal tar pitch (CTP) — that go into the carbon anodes used to smelt aluminium, which management says have no viable substitute. It buys cheap feedstocks (green petroleum coke, a refinery byproduct, and coal tar, a byproduct of blast-furnace steel mills), processes them, and sells the finished product to aluminium smelters and other industrial customers; management describes its role as a 'converter' that passes raw-material costs through and earns a margin for processing and service, so profits swing with the gap between feedstock costs and finished-product prices. It runs plants across three continents — India, Europe, and North America (including a distillation plant and solid pitch re-melter in Canada) — and also sells specialty chemicals and, in India, cement. In the March 2026 quarter it reported revenue of ₹45.21 billion and adjusted EBITDA of ₹7.15 billion.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Carbon segment — Calcination** — Bakes green petroleum coke into calcined petroleum coke for aluminium smelters (plus graphite-electrode and some battery-material customers), and it is the company's main volume and profit driver — Indian CPC plants ran at optimum capacity in Q1 2026 after import restrictions in place since 2018 were relaxed; a new anhydrous carbon pellet (ACP) product in the US is still being scaled up and tested with customers.
- **Carbon segment — Distillation** — Distills coal tar into coal tar pitch (the other essential anode ingredient) plus creosote, crude naphthalene, and carbon black oil; it is the other half of the Carbon segment and management says its North American network could process all available regional coal tar and supply up to 100% of the region's carbon and creosote needs.
- **Advanced Materials** — Sells higher-value chemical products — petrochemical intermediates, engineered products, HHCR products, and resins — made largely from the same distillation streams; it is smaller than Carbon but described as a steady, more resilient profit contributor, with resins in particular holding up earnings in weak quarters.
- **Cement** — Makes and sells cement in South India, the smallest of the three reported segments, with earnings driven by fuel costs and by heavy price competition as the Indian cement industry consolidates.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Rain Industries scores 34 out of 100 and lands at Weak (1 star), built from 49 on business quality, 10 on multibagger fit and 36 on risk safety. The quality framework shows a company that is respectable at the operating level and poor at the capital level: customer benefits (+0.5), management (+0.4), growth (+0.33) and industry structure (+0.33) all score positively, while return on capital (-0.67) and capital allocation (-0.75) drag hardest. The numbers behind those two are the core of the story. Every Rs 1 of assets produces only Rs 0.82 of sales, about 134 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and every Rs 100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings generated just Rs 5 of operating cash this year. The business is heavily asset- and debt-intensive across calciners, distillation plants and cement, carrying net debt of 4.3 times EBITDA. Spending over the period was overwhelmingly maintenance and turnaround rather than growth, the one brownfield expansion was deferred, and the earlier growth projects (shaft calciner, ACP, HHCR) delivered poor incremental returns. So the modest strengths sit on top of a capital base that is not earning its keep.

The multibagger screen tells the same story in a different vocabulary, and its 10-out-of-100 result comes from a clear pattern: seven of eleven patterns are only partial fits and four do not fit at all. The most revealing is Toll Roads. Calcined pet coke and coal tar pitch are genuinely indispensable, substitute-free inputs to carbon anodes, stressed by management on calls from May 2023 through May 2026, and that is classic toll-road language. But the toll is not actually collected: anodes are a large and closely negotiated share of a smelter's costs, so the indispensability does not convert into money. That single trait explains why Pricing Power and Brand Strength are outright non-fits, and it is also what makes Good Enough Goods an elevated risk. Management describes itself as a converter earning a processing spread rather than a premium franchise, blended realisation fell about 21% in the third quarter of 2024, and customers pushed back hard on pitch prices while tar costs rose. The same essential-but-unpriced position shows up again in the checks, where pricing, mix and volume scores negative because growth comes from volume and market quotations under explicit pass-through economics. Network effects score the worst mark available (-2), because nothing in the model gets stronger with scale. What is left as a moat is Cost to Replicate, which is a partial fit: a permitted North American distillation and Canadian re-melter network, global blending and multi-modal logistics, and decades of smelter qualification are real and hard to rebuild. The company's honest answer to being unable to raise prices is the Low Price Plus pattern, also partial: the November 2024 pledge to scrutinise every expenditure was followed by delivered 2025 actions on plant rationalisation, labour restructuring and insourcing that showed up in first-quarter 2026 profits. That is cost defence, not compounding. Innovation Dominance is partial for a similar reason: there is a real and persistent programme (ACP pellets worked on from 2023 to 2026, CARBORES and PETRORES, a Canadian battery and energy-storage materials demonstration plant commissioned mid-2025, and about 30% of the resin portfolio developed in five years), but research spending is under 0.5% of sales.

The risk review, which scores 36 for safety, mostly names the flip side of things the quality framework counted as strengths, and the overlap is what keeps the verdict low. Cyclicality is high risk, with management framing results in cycle and commodity language across all eighteen calls, a $21 million inventory write-down in the first quarter of 2023, and quarterly profits swinging from Rs 6.85 billion to Rs 2.92 billion; yet cyclical market growth scores positive on the checks because the 2023-2025 trough is visibly turning, with margins normalising, smelter restarts, elevated aluminium prices and a return to the targeted quarterly earnings range in 2026. Same trait, two signs, and the framework notes much of that upturn is already visible rather than ahead. Government dependency is the second high risk and works the same way: Indian calcination capacity was throttled for six years by pet coke import restrictions, and earnings turned on regulatory relief in February 2024, with Supreme Court emission rules, annual import allocations and an unresolved special-zone exemption still open. That very relief is what let Indian plants run at optimum capacity and is one of the three reasons the gain-in-market-share check scores positive, alongside a new European aluminium customer and a possible competitor exit in North American distillation. Chinese exporters are the elevated new-entrant risk and are also the reason pricing power fails and cost discipline became the strategy. Stakeholder concentration is a watch item, with a whole quarter's carbon shortfall traced to maintenance outages at two major pitch customers. Three risks are low - technology, consumer preferences and fashion - because there is no credible substitute for these products and demand comes from smelter run rates, steel output and construction. Set against that, the Market Share Gainers pattern does not fit at all: cement volumes were lost to pan-India acquirers in South India in early 2026, and the 2026 carbon recovery is utilisation-led at existing customers rather than new wins. Management scores well and Corporate Culture is a partial fit on stable, long-tenured leadership present across all eighteen calls and prompt disclosure of bad news, including injuries and the write-down. Resilience is judged SOUND. The picture is a durable, honestly run, genuinely needed business that cannot charge for being needed, and therefore struggles to earn a decent return on the very large amount of capital it must carry.

## What to watch

- Return on capital (headline): whether the 5% cash return on capital and the 4.3x net debt to EBITDA improve as the cycle turns, or whether the recovery passes through without lifting either.
- Capex for growth (not maintenance): whether the deferred brownfield expansion is revived and earns better incremental returns than the shaft calciner, ACP and HHCR projects did.
- Toll Roads pattern and Good Enough Goods risk: any sign the company starts capturing value from being a substitute-free input - realisations set by something other than market quotations and Chinese export pricing - would move this from a partial fit toward a real toll.
- Government Dependency (high risk): resolution of the special-zone exemption, the annual import allocations and the emission rules, since the same regulatory lever that delivered the 2024 relief and the market-share gain can reverse.
- Market Share Gainers (no fit) and Stakeholder Concentration (watch): whether cement volume loss to pan-India acquirers in South India stops, whether the possible competitor exit in North American distillation actually happens, and whether new customers broaden a base that a two-customer maintenance outage can still swing.
