# Force Motors (FORCEMOT) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Force Motors is a 66-year-old Indian vehicle maker based in Akurdi, Pune, that today sells mainly vans and buses — its Traveller and Urbania passenger vans, 26-seater Travellers, 33- and 41-seater Monobuses, Trax delivery vans, and the Gurkha off-roader for the armed forces. It earns money by selling these vehicles to Indian customers (shared-mobility and fleet operators, delivery businesses, and the defence forces) and increasingly to export markets in the Middle East, Latin America and Africa, plus a steady stream of spare-parts sales through about 287 service touch-points in India and 23 abroad. Alongside vehicles, it runs a components/aggregates business that manufactures engines and parts for global carmakers Mercedes-Benz and BMW, which management calls a good 'ballast' to the vehicle business. It has exited tractors and stopped making small commercial vehicles to focus on these lines; FY2024-25 revenue rose about 16%, EBITDA about 21%, ROCE reached roughly 43%, profit was around Rs 1,200 crore versus Rs 100 crore three years earlier, and the company is now essentially debt-free after repaying about Rs 500 crore.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Vehicle business — T1 (Traveller and Urbania vans)** — Its core and biggest product line: passenger vans used for shared mobility, where management says it holds roughly 77% market share; the premium Urbania currently runs at 500-600 units a month with a target of about 1,000, and the Traveller platform is undergoing a major upgrade due to launch around Q1 of next year.
- **Vehicle business — T2 (26-seater Traveller)** — The larger 26-seat version of the Traveller, where market share has grown to close to 19%.
- **Monobus platform (33- and 41-seater buses)** — Full-size buses that have been gaining share, reaching about 7% in the 33-seater segment.
- **Delivery vans (Traveller and Trax platforms)** — Goods-carrying vans holding roughly 16% market share; new niche, higher-margin Trax variants are being developed to grow volumes.
- **Gurkha / defence** — A rugged vehicle sold to the armed forces — already supplied as a light strike vehicle, and last year the company won a major contract for 3,000 Gurkhas as troop carriers, with Indian and international defence seen as a growth avenue.
- **Automotive component / aggregates business (engines for Mercedes-Benz and BMW)** — Manufactures engines and components for Mercedes-Benz and BMW in India; it grew about 16% last year, has no seasonality, and specific engine volumes are confidential — India's luxury car market it feeds is around 60,000 vehicles a year and growing.
- **Spare parts business** — Aftermarket parts sold through the service network; grew about 20% last year and is targeted to grow over 20% again this year.
- **International / export business** — Exports of Urbania and Traveller vans started in the last six months into the Middle East, Latin America and now South Africa and wider Africa; management expects at least 100% growth this year off a small base.
- **FMTU joint venture (referred to by a shareholder as the Rolls-Royce JV)** — A joint venture company that reached break-even last year after three years of ramp-up and localisation — described as a big milestone but no size figures were given.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Force Motors comes out of these three reviews as a genuinely good business with a narrow base. It owns roughly 77% of the Traveller/Urbania van segment, supplies engines to Mercedes-Benz and BMW, and won a 3,000-vehicle Armed Forces order — sales made on proven reliability rather than on price. The financial housekeeping is unusually clean: about ₹500 crore of debt repaid to reach a debt-free position, a ₹2,000 crore capex plan paid for out of earnings rather than borrowings, only about 20 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, roughly ₹31 of operating cash generated for every ₹100 of capital employed, and a management team that exited tractors and small commercial vehicles to concentrate on what works. On the quality framework this shows up as full marks for capital allocation, customer benefits, industry structure and management, with competitive advantage and growth scoring only half. The combined rating lands at 54 out of 100 — 'Decent', three stars — built from a strong business-quality score of 70, a weak multibagger-fit score of 25, and a middling risk-safety score of 58, with overall resilience judged sound.

The gap between the strong quality score and the weak pattern score comes down largely to one trait that cuts both ways: this management does not explain itself. The framework rewards that as a strength — being out of the limelight, speaking to shareholders once a year at the AGM, choosing substance over publicity — and it scores full marks. But the same silence is why ten of the eleven multibagger patterns could not be confirmed. Only Market Share Gainers is a strong fit, and it is well evidenced: share quantified across every segment (about 77% in T1, 19% in T2, 7% in 33-seater buses, 16% in delivery vans), rising consistently for two to three years, won alongside 16% revenue growth, 21% EBITDA growth and roughly 43% return on capital. Everything else stalls for lack of disclosure rather than lack of substance. Innovation Dominance is only partial because a shareholder asked for the R&D figure and never got it, so the ₹2,000 crore programme cannot be sized against peers. Corporate Culture is partial for the same reason — strong signals on return on capital, debt repayment and family stewardship, offset by the refusal to explain. Pricing Power is partial because although Urbania is positioned as premium, a shareholder observed that margins rose without price increases, and GST cuts are being handed to buyers as ₹1–4 lakh price reductions rather than kept. Recurring Revenue and Brand Strength are partial in the same way: a real spare-parts business growing over 20% with 287 domestic and 23 international touch-points, and a 66-year three-generation heritage, but no discussion of renewals, attach rates or brand spend to confirm them.

The risks are mostly the strengths viewed from the other side. The dominant 77% share sits in a single vehicle category, and that category's economics were set by a tax slab rather than by the market — management admits the company 'was suffering for years' under a 43% duty classification on its core 10-13 seater, and attributes much of the expected near-term upside to one policy change, which is why government dependency is the single high risk. Cyclicality is elevated for a related reason: vans, buses and Gurkhas are capital purchases by fleet operators and the armed forces, and the engine business rides Mercedes and BMW luxury volumes in a market management describes as only about 60,000 vehicles a year. That engine business is prized as an assurance-driven, hard-to-replicate relationship in the quality checks, and it is simultaneously the stakeholder-concentration risk, since the whole components vertical rests on those two named customers with volumes management cannot disclose. The undisclosed source of margin gains appears a third time as the Good Enough Goods risk — two shareholders pressed on whether record margins can hold when competitors exist in every product line, and management called the answer a trade secret. Technology is on watch because the franchise is built on diesel platforms and engine assembly, with the EV stance described as reactive and most of the ₹2,000 crore going into upgrading existing products. Against all of this sit real cushions: a debt-free balance sheet funding capacity at 60-65% utilisation, a spares annuity growing over 20%, a completed pivot away from low-cost utilitarian products, a fleet demand base that is functional rather than fashion-driven, and an export push into the Middle East, Latin America and Africa that would dilute dependence on Indian policy.
