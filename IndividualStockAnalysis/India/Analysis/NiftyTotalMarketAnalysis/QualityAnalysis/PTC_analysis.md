# PTC India (PTC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

PTC India Limited (formerly Power Trading Corporation of India) is an Indian power trading company, set up in 1999, that buys electricity from generators and sells it on to buyers such as state distribution utilities and large commercial and industrial customers — it does not itself own the power plants it trades from. It makes money mainly on a thin trading margin per unit of electricity it moves (around 3.2 to 3.4 paisa per unit on volumes of roughly 71 to 93 billion units a year), earning income from long- and medium-term contracts (PPAs) as well as short-term deals done on the power exchanges, which in recent years have been more than half of volumes. It also earns meaningful income from late-payment surcharge and rebate on customer dues, from a consulting/advisory business, and from selling renewable energy certificates. It operates across India and trades power across the border with Bhutan, Nepal and Bangladesh, and holds stakes in a lending subsidiary (PTC India Financial Services) and a power exchange (Hindustan Power Exchange).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Power trading (long-term and medium-term)** — Bilateral contracts to buy power from large generators and sell it to distribution companies over multi-year tenures; management says the ideal portfolio is about 50:50 long/medium-term versus short-term, though by FY26 short-term had risen to 56% because buyers are reluctant to sign long contracts, and the portfolio is currently weighted to thermal and hydro with wind and solar starting to come in.
- **Short-term trading / power exchanges** — Day-to-day buying and selling of power to bridge supply-demand and price mismatches, mostly through the power exchanges; this is the biggest and fastest-growing chunk of volume (53% in FY23, 52% in FY25, 60% in the first nine months of FY26), though margins here are thin — in FY23 PTC deliberately walked away from exchange volume that was earning a negative margin.
- **Cross-border trading** — Import and export of electricity with the three grid-connected neighbours — Bhutan, Nepal and Bangladesh — including a contract to export up to 2,000 MW to Bhutan in winter and steady, regularly-paid supply to Bangladesh; it is a smaller but steady contributor that helped lift volumes in FY26.
- **Surcharge / rebate income** — Interest-type income PTC earns on overdue payments from customers (and rebates on early payment); the calls treat this as a swing factor in profits rather than a business line — it was hit by the government's interest-free LPS repayment scheme in FY23 (roughly Rs 170 crore impact) and fell again in FY26 as state utilities' liquidity improved, dragging operating income down.
- **Consultancy / advisory (techno-commercial consulting)** — Fee-based advisory work for utilities, ports, industrial bodies and governments — revenue of about Rs 57.5 crore in FY23 (up 45%) — covering things like power-market development for commercial and industrial consumers, smart-meter and rooftop-solar work under the PM Kusum programme, transmission lines and substations for Talcher Fertilizer and MPIDC, and an ADB-funded project to build power markets linking Laos, Thailand and Cambodia.
- **Renewable energy certificates (RECs) and renewables sourcing** — Trading of green certificates that buyers use to meet renewable obligations — about 68 lakh certificates in FY26 — plus a tender (expression of interest) for 500 MW of renewable capacity to tie up new purchase and sale arrangements as India's energy mix shifts toward solar and storage.
- **PTC India Financial Services (PFS) — listed subsidiary** — A lending arm that finances power projects; it swings consolidated profits noticeably (a reversal of past impairment provisions boosted nine-month FY26 consolidated profit), it has struggled to raise fresh funds or grow its loan book, and management has hired SBI Caps to advise on selling down or diluting PTC's stake.
- **PTC Energy Limited (PEL) — divested wind subsidiary** — A wind generation subsidiary that PTC sold to ONGC Green Limited on 4 March 2025 for Rs 1,175 crore, booking a one-off profit of about Rs 521 crore that inflated FY25 earnings; it is no longer part of the business.
- **Investments and joint ventures** — Minority stakes carried on the books, including equity in hydro project Teesta Urja (whose fair-value changes move comprehensive income up and down), an associate holding in Hindustan Power Exchange, and a joint venture with NLC India Renewables to develop hydro projects.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

PTC India earns a genuinely high return on the money invested in it: every ₹100 of capital produced ₹36 of operating cash this year, and every ₹1 of assets turns over into ₹1.33 of sales, all on a debt-free balance sheet. That is the whole of the good news and it comes from one trait — the company owns almost nothing. It buys and resells electricity, so it needs very little capital to grow, and it is growing: volumes rose 11% in FY25 against national generation growth of 5.21%, 9% in the first nine months of FY26 against demand growth of under 1%, and 12% in FY26, in an end market expected to expand 6-8% a year as renewables climb from 16% to 26% of energy. It has also built a real cross-border franchise across all three grid-connected neighbours, a 2,000 MW Bhutan winter export contract and an ADB-funded mandate in Laos, Thailand and Cambodia. On the quality framework this shows up as a perfect score on return on capital and positive marks on growth and industry structure.

The problem is that the same asset-light trait that produces the high return also means there is nothing to stop anyone else doing it. The multibagger screen found no clear fit on any of its eleven patterns — seven partial, four outright no fit — and the reason is consistent across them. 'Cost to Replicate' fails because 70 licensed traders already compete for exchange volume and the product is undifferentiated electricity intermediation at about 3.4 paisa a unit. 'Pricing Power' and 'Brand Strength' are only partial because twenty-five years as the pioneer buys first call from long-duration and cross-border counterparties but no premium price; after direct input costs the company keeps just ₹6 of every ₹100 of sales. The quality framework says the same thing from the other side, scoring intangible benefits at its worst level. Worse, the growth and the weakness are the same event: the volumes being won are short-term and exchange trades earning 0.87 paisa a unit, displacing long-term contracts earning 7.91 paisa, so blended margin sits roughly flat at 3.3 paisa. Exchange trades have gone from about 52% to 60% of volumes. Market-share gain and margin erosion are one story, not two, and that is exactly what the risk review flags as elevated technology and 'good enough goods' risk.

The risk review's single high-risk finding is government dependency, and it connects straight back to what looks like strength elsewhere. The cross-border franchise that gives PTC its most defensible niche — where management says only four to six credible rivals compete — rests on three government counterparties in Bhutan, Nepal and Bangladesh, which is also why counterparty concentration is rated elevated. Domestic customers are state distribution companies, with ₹1,600 crore once outstanding in Jammu & Kashmir, since cut to near nothing with a ₹1,000 crore recovery, plus Bihar. Policy sets earnings directly: one Ministry of Power scheme removed roughly ₹170 crore of surcharge income in FY23. About 95 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Management has delivered on what it promised — selling the generating arm for ₹1,175 crore, clearing auditor qualifications, staying debt-free — but has then left the roughly ₹1,100 crore of proceeds and surplus cash idle for over a year behind exploratory agreements, and paid ₹11.70 a share against the roughly ₹14.42 its own 50%-of-profit policy implied, with no buyback. That is why capital allocation scores worst of the seven quality areas. The combined rating is 32 out of 100, or Weak — 54 on business quality, zero on multibagger fit, 32 on risk safety — with the balance sheet judged sound. A cash-generative, debt-free business with no durable advantage and no visible plan for its cash.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Fortunes are set by policy at nearly every level: the Ministry of Power's LPS scheme wiped ~Rs.170cr of surcharge income in FY23 in a single stroke, CERC regulations determine market coupling and storage classification, customers are state DISCOMs (J&K, Bihar arrears), promoters are PSUs with the Ministry of Power the largest shareholder, and PFS divestment requires RBI approval. > "there will always be some of the things which are not under the control of the company, and that is because of the changes in the government rules or the regulations" — *management, on an earnings call*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Volumes are flow-linked to national power consumption rather than customer capex, but earnings swing on weather, exchange prices and DISCOM liquidity — FY26 saw national demand grow <1%, and benign weather cut power procurement costs, collapsing rebate/surcharge income (operational income flat at ~Rs.450cr despite 12% volume growth). Management itself calls the working-capital need cyclical.

> "weather has been generally very benign and the power purchase cost on the exchanges have been very down and subdued... they temporarily have better liquidity" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/operations-linked demand (electricity consumption, not customer capex); trading margin per unit has been stable-to-rising, 2.82 to ~3.38 paisa, and the company is debt-free with a large cash war chest*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 5 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 5% and 13% (±2.1 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.

## What to watch
