# Karur Vysya Bank Ltd. (KARURVYSYA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

Karur Vysya Bank is an Indian commercial bank, headquartered in Karur, Tamil Nadu and listed on the NSE and BSE, that takes deposits from the public and lends the money out at a higher rate. Its customers are mainly retail households, farmers, small and medium businesses (MSMEs) and, to a smaller extent, larger corporates, served through a branch network (including newer "lite" branches and an open-market sales channel) plus digital channels. It makes money chiefly from net interest income — the spread between what it pays on deposits and what it earns on loans and investments, running a net interest margin of roughly 3.7%-4.3% — plus fee and other non-interest income; management steers the bank on three stated metrics: growth, profitability and asset quality. As of June 2026 total business was about INR 2,27,267 crore, split roughly 54% deposits (INR 1,22,587 crore) and the rest advances (INR 1,04,680 crore).

**The parts of the business:**

- **RAM verticals (Retail, Agriculture, MSME/Commercial)** — The bank's core lending engine and stated focus area — granular, mostly secured loans that make up about 86% of total advances and grew 18% year-on-year in FY26.
- **Retail advances** — Loans to individuals — jewel (gold) loans, mortgages, housing loans and personal loans — about 27% of the loan book, growing 25% in FY26 and 23% year-on-year by Q1 FY27, led by mortgage and retail jewel loans; mortgage-focused branches were raised from 144 to 175.
- **Agriculture** — Farm lending at about 25% of the loan book, of which roughly 91%-92% is agri jewel (gold-backed) loans at a loan-to-value around 55%-64%, growing about 19% in FY26 with LTV monitored through automated branch alerts.
- **Commercial / MSME (Small Business Group and Business Banking Group)** — Working-capital and business loans to small and medium enterprises — the largest single slice at about 35% of advances, grew 11.6% in FY26, now sold through a relationship-manager model across 77 branches with products like a GST surrogate loan and an open term loan.
- **Corporate and institutional banking** — Lending to larger companies — about 14% of advances; shrank for a period as the bank walked away from low-yielding business, then returned to 12% growth in FY26 (20% including credit substitutes) via commercial real estate, capital-market participants and EPC/infrastructure clients.
- **Credit substitutes** — Bond-like exposures to existing, externally A-rated corporate customers used instead of plain loans because the pricing is better — INR 969 crore added in FY26 and INR 171 crore in Q1 FY27.
- **Liability business (deposits, CASA and NRI)** — Raising current, savings and term deposits — 54% of total business, with retail households supplying 72% of deposits; run through a dedicated acquisition channel, 24 CASA product variants and separate desks for corporate salary, institutional, NRE, government, trade/forex and business correspondents, with FCNR (B) deposits used to court NRI customers.
- **Transaction Banking Group** — Digital plumbing for corporate and MSME clients — API-based real-time banking, bulk payments for payroll and vendor/B2B settlement, and automated trade finance and supply-chain workflows; described as supporting portfolio diversification and big client relationships rather than as a large earner on its own.
- **BNPL (buy-now-pay-later) partnership book** — Small consumer credit sourced through an outside partner, INR 798 crore outstanding at March 2026; it shrank in FY26 after the partner changed management and household debt levels rose, and grew 2% in Q1 FY27.
- **Co-lending with NBFCs** — Loans made jointly with non-bank lenders, deliberately shrunk to about INR 249 crore because it was not adding to return on assets, and treated as secondary to the bank's own organic lending.
- **Microfinance (MFI)** — A deliberately tiny book of INR 173 crore, just 0.18% of the portfolio, now with government (CGFMU) guarantee cover and guardrails after stress in the segment.
- **Treasury and investments** — Manages the bank's securities portfolio and liquidity — contributes yield on investments and occasional mark-to-market write-backs to non-interest income, and was reorganised under the new RBI investment-classification rules from April 2024 (P&L neutral, +INR 81 crore to reserves); the bank has no overseas or GIFT City presence.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

## The verdict: Mixed — 47 out of 100 ★★☆☆☆

In one breath: the quality framework finds an average business today (59/100); it strongly fits 1 of the 11 patterns long-term winners share; the risk review found 1 high risk (Government Dependency). Weighing those together gives 47 out of 100 — mixed.

**The exact arithmetic:** Overall = 45% × 59 (business quality) + 30% × 15 (multibagger fit) + 25% × 64 (risk safety) = 47 out of 100 → Mixed (2 stars).

- **Business quality (59/100):** The 34-check quality framework scored the business +0.37 on its −2 (poor) to +2 (excellent) scale, with 82% of checks backed by evidence; mapped onto 0–100 that is 59 points.
- **Multibagger fit (15/100):** the foundation test did not pass (+0); 1 pattern fit strongly (Corporate Culture) (+15) → 15 of 100.

### The story in depth

Karur Vysya Bank is, on this evidence, a management story rather than a franchise story. Across all 17 calls the same managing director (Ramesh Babu, in post since 2020) has set three goals — growth, profitability and asset quality — and hit them: return on assets from 0.19% in December 2020 to 2.11%, gross bad loans from 3.97% to 0.74%, and the corporate loan book cut from 45% of advances to 14%, with guidance repeatedly met or beaten. That consistency is what earns the one and only strong pattern verdict in the screen, Corporate Culture, and it is the same behaviour the quality checks reward with top marks for disciplined stewardship (no acquisitions, deliberate shrinking of low-yield business, counter-cyclical provisioning, an 18.61% capital ratio and no share issuance) and for candour (line-by-line explanations of cost and fee declines, open admission of past corporate-lending mistakes, and explicit warnings not to extrapolate a strong quarter). Capital allocation follows the same logic — a 130% dividend and a 1:5 bonus paid out of retained profits with no need to raise dilutive capital, though there is no buyback. On the framework's own area scores, management and capital allocation are the clear positives; growth and customer benefits are mildly positive; competitive advantage and return on capital sit flat at zero and industry structure is negative.

The screen returns no second strong fit, and the reason is that the very traits producing the good numbers also cap them. Cost discipline and clean underwriting — cost-to-income down from 56% to 46% and 42% for FY26, operating costs up only 2.45%, net bad loans at 0.19% — make the Low Price Plus pattern half true, but low cost is not the same as a low-cost moat, and the bank still cannot charge more. That shows up directly as Pricing Power NO FIT: management concedes it must 'compromise on margins' in MSME lending and to keep existing loan-against-property and commercial customers, and has guided FY27 margins below the current exit level. The same fact appears again on the quality side as the two negative checks — no barriers to entry worth having, because pricing behaviour in the core segments is irrational, and no brand-driven pricing power, leaving the bank a price taker with margin guided down to 3.7–3.8%. Market share gains are real (advances up 16–17%, retail up 25%, total business from INR1.7 lakh crore to INR2.27 lakh crore, an explicit target of 1–2% above system credit growth) but only a partial fit, because growth in a business where accounts change hands on rate is not the same as growth that sticks. Brand Strength and Cost to Replicate are likewise partial: a Tamil Nadu bank since 1916 with 72% of deposits from retail households would take a newcomer years to copy, but there is no advertising spend and no premium pricing to prove the brand does economic work. Toll Roads and Recurring Revenue do not fit at all. Hence the multibagger score of just 15 out of 100 against a business-quality score of 59.

The risk review lines up point for point with those weaknesses rather than adding new ones. Government Dependency is the single HIGH RISK, and it is structural rather than contractual: the bank takes no subsidies or government contracts, but the regulator sets its economics — 55% of the loan book reprices with the policy rate, so 125bps of cuts flow straight through to margin, alongside changes to investment classification and expected-loss provisioning rules. That is the same margin pressure the Pricing Power verdict describes, arriving from a second direction, and it feeds the Cyclicality WATCH, where repo cuts pushed margin guidance from over 4% down to 3.7–3.8% and Q4 FY26 required a one-time INR163 crore prudential provision. New Entrants and Good Enough Goods are WATCH for the same underlying reason — accounts lost on price, housing loans growing only 2% because yields are low and competition strong — while Shifting Consumer Preferences captures the deposit side, where savers have been moving money to other investment options throughout the period. The two LOW risks, technology and stakeholder concentration, are where the turnaround has genuinely bought protection: steady small-scale digital work rather than a legacy format under threat, and a deliberately de-concentrated book (retail, agriculture and MSME at 86% of advances, retail households at 72% of deposits, co-lending cut to INR249 crore because it was not adding to returns). Resilience is recorded as UNKNOWN. The combined rating — 45% × 59 business quality, 30% × 15 pattern fit, 25% × 64 risk safety — gives 47 out of 100, a Mixed two-star verdict: a well-run bank executing a genuine repair job in an industry that does not let good execution turn into durable pricing power.

*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

## What to watch
