# PI Industries Ltd. (PIIND) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FERTILISERS & PESTICIDES*

PI Industries is an Indian agrochemical company that makes crop-protection chemicals — insecticides, herbicides and fungicides — and sells them two ways: as a contract manufacturer to large global agrochemical innovators, and as its own branded products to Indian farmers. The bigger half by far is the export business, called CSM (custom synthesis and manufacturing), where global patent-holders hand PI their early-stage patented molecules to develop the process for and manufacture at scale; in Q1 FY23 exports were INR 11,421 million of INR 15,432 million total revenue, and management says PI ranks among the top three CSM companies in the world. The domestic side sells branded formulations to Indian farmers (over 3 million farmers engaged, per the calls) through launches like CLARET, EKETSU and PI Mitra Kisan, a direct-to-farmer app. Manufacturing and R&D are based in India — including a single-site integrated research centre at Udaipur with 800+ scientists — plus operations in Italy/Europe and the US; full-year FY26 revenue was INR 67,137 million with a 25% EBITDA margin and INR 34,265 million of net cash.

**The parts of the business:**

- **CSM AgChem exports (custom synthesis and manufacturing)** — Makes patented agrochemical molecules under contract for global innovator companies, handling process research through commercial-scale manufacture; this is the largest business (roughly three-quarters of revenue in the FY23 quarter shown), commercializing 5–10 new molecules a year, with new products contributing 18% of AgChem export revenue in FY26.
- **Domestic agri brands (PI AgSciences)** — Sells PI's own branded insecticides, herbicides and fungicides to Indian farmers — 4 new products launched in FY26 (3 herbicides, 1 insecticide) — and was about a quarter of revenue in the FY23 quarter shown; it has been weak recently on high channel inventory and low crop prices, with recovery guided from FY27.
- **Jivagro (horticulture)** — A separate branded arm described as India's only horticulture specialist, serving fruit and vegetable growers with around 60 brands; positioned as a second growth engine alongside the main domestic portfolio.
- **Biologicals and bio-nutrients (Global Biologicals)** — Bio-based crop health products — bio-fungicides, bio-nutrients and an acquired peptide-based technology platform (Harpin αβ approved in India, filings in the US and Latin America) — described as one of the fastest-growing parts of the portfolio, compounding at over 20%.
- **PI Health Sciences (pharma / CRDMO)** — Contract research, development and manufacturing for pharma innovators and biotechs, built on an integrated India–Italy operating model; small but the fastest-growing platform, up 50% over nine months of FY26 and 40% for the full year.
- **Electronic chemicals and other specialty chemicals** — Non-agrochemical custom manufacturing for high-quality specialty and electronics markets, a deliberate diversification of the CSM order book — non-AgChem molecules made up a growing share of new enquiries and of the 40–50 product R&D pipeline.
- **In-house NCE programme (new chemical entities)** — PI's own discovery effort to invent new molecules rather than only make others' — its first, Pioxaniliprole, is under registration in India and described as a defining platform for the coming decade, shifting PI from manufacturer to innovator.

### The story in depth

PI Industries earns its keep on invention. The quality framework gives top marks to research and development, technology and long-term tenacity: roughly 800 scientists and 155 patents at a single integrated research site in Udaipur, a pipeline of around 50 molecules, five to ten molecules commercialised every year, and new products now making up 18% of crop-protection exports. That same evidence is what makes Innovation Dominance the one clear pattern fit in the multibagger screen, and it is what sits behind the Cost to Replicate case: decades of trust with the world's largest patent-holding customers, regulatory-approved sites and a product development cycle measured in years are things a well-funded newcomer cannot simply buy. The customer-facing version of this is the 'assurance' the framework scores highest of all — the export business is sold on certainty, respect for others' intellectual property and meticulous delivery, which is why relationships deepened rather than moved on price when customers were reconsidering China. It shows up in the numbers as unusually steady economics: ₹26 of operating profit per ₹100 of sales, moving only about ±2.6 points a year, and sales growth averaging about 12% a year.

The awkward part is that the very design that creates the moat also creates the concentration. Because the business is built around a small set of global innovator relationships and, within them, one dominant molecule, the risk review marks Stakeholder Concentration as elevated — analysts asked directly what growth looks like excluding that single product, and management conceded newer molecules are flat to negative in absolute terms. The same molecule-by-molecule model explains why the screen finds no pricing power at all: patented molecules eventually go generic, and the company itself has cited 10–15% price erosion when that happens, with recent gross-margin gains attributed to product mix rather than to raising prices. That is also why Toll Roads only partly fits — the stickiness is real, but what is actually sold is the active ingredient, not a position customers must pay to pass through. Cheap Chinese capacity and generic equivalents (the New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks) press on the same seam, and the industry's drift from synthetic chemistry toward biological products threatens the older half of the portfolio. Management is visibly answering all of this at once — own brands, a direct-to-farmer app, own biologicals in Brazil, Mexico and the US, and taking its own newly discovered molecule to market — which is why Forward Integrators reads as a likely fit and biologicals, peptides and electronic chemicals appear as the silver lining under almost every risk. But the answer is not yet in the results: Brand Strength stops short of a full fit because there is no evidence of charging more and gaining share, with domestic revenue down 6% year on year.

The financial scorecard is where the story is weakest today, and it is cyclical rather than structural in character. Capital allocation scores lowest of all the quality areas, and return on capital is close behind: every ₹100 of shareholders' funds and borrowings produced only about ₹4 of operating cash this year, and about 119 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers — a cycle that has lengthened from 102 days. Roughly ₹2,600 crore of capital spending has gone in while sales per rupee of assets fell from about 2.5 times to 1.5 times, and capacity is running at 80% in an acknowledged multi-year downturn of falling volumes, customer destocking and soft prices. This is the same countercyclical investment that earns the company its top marks for tenacity, funded by a debt-free balance sheet holding roughly ₹34–35 billion of net cash — which is also the main evidence behind the partial Corporate Culture fit and the reason the overall resilience reading is sound. So the picture is coherent: a genuinely hard-to-copy innovation engine, spending heavily through a trough, with returns and working capital paying the bill in the meantime. The combined rating of 62 out of 100, or Decent, is exactly that trade — 67 for business quality, 56 for multibagger fit, 60 for risk safety.
