# Lenskart Solut. (LENSKART) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Lenskart sells eyeglasses, lenses and sunglasses to everyday consumers, and gives free/low-cost eye tests to find the people who need them — it conducted 6.8 million eye tests globally in the March 2026 quarter, roughly half of them first-ever eye exams. It makes money mainly by selling eyewear units through its own network of small neighbourhood stores (3,327 stores globally as of March 2026) backed by an app and website, with about 45% of India sales digitally influenced; it manufactures a large share of frames and lenses in-house, which is why product margins run around 64% in India and 75.7% internationally. Extra revenue comes from a paid 'Gold' subscription/loyalty programme with 8.8 million active members and from premium lens and frame ranges. FY26 revenue was over ₹9,000 crores with ₹530 crores of PAT; India is the bigger half (₹5,265 crores) and the rest comes from Singapore, Japan, Thailand, the Middle East (UAE, Saudi Arabia) and the rest of Southeast Asia.

**The parts of the business:**

- **India business** — The core segment — 2,609 stores at March 2026 selling glasses backed by eye testing, delivering ₹5,265 crores of FY26 revenue (₹1,475 crores in Q4, up 44%) at a 14.3% pre-Ind AS EBITDA margin, growing mostly on volume (29 million eyewear units in FY26) plus deeper expansion into Tier 2 and smaller towns.
- **International business** — 718 stores at March 2026 across Singapore (where management says it is the largest eyewear player), Japan, Thailand, rest of Southeast Asia and the Middle East; Q4 revenue of ₹1,054 crores up 35% (25% in constant currency), with FY26 pre-Ind AS EBITDA margin improving from 3.6% to 7% as each market is plugged into the Indian supply chain.
- **Eye testing / remote optometry** — Free-standing eye exams inside stores, increasingly done by an optometrist sitting hundreds of kilometres away over an AI-assisted video platform — rolled out from 168 stores in March 2025 to 623 India stores by March 2026, and described as the engine that creates new customers rather than a separate revenue line.
- **In-house manufacturing / centralised supply chain** — Company-owned frame and lens factories (including a new Hyderabad plant, with ₹267 crores invested in nine months) feeding a central warehouse that ships next-day to 58 Indian cities, giving management a claimed 35–40% cost advantage and keeping stores small, asset-light 'order booking' points rather than stockrooms.
- **Gold membership** — A paid subscription programme — described as the first of its kind in eyewear — with 8.8 million active members in India, driving repeat purchases at zero extra acquisition cost; 37% of Q3 sales came from members signed up in earlier quarters.
- **Owndays** — An acquired premium brand whose lenses now account for 38% of India revenue, plus Owndays frames — one of the main ways customers trade up in price without leaving Lenskart.
- **Meller** — An acquired sunglasses brand, consolidated into the international numbers during FY26 (it was the reason reported international growth of 34% overstated the underlying 26% in Q2).
- **Dealskart** — The former master franchisee for India stores, since acquired; before consolidation, sales to it showed up as B2B 'primary' sales that distorted reported growth versus actual customer sales.
- **GeoIQ and in-house technology** — An acquired AI location-intelligence platform, plus Tango Eye store analytics, virtual try-on and in-store face scan (100,000+ scans a day), used to pick store sites from 3,000+ variables, predict cannibalisation and recommend frames — supported by 500+ engineers and treated as the backbone of both growth and margin, not as a product sold to outsiders.
- **B by Lenskart** — A premium-priced smart eyewear product being rolled out, mentioned in the May 2026 call as a new launch with no size figures given yet.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Lenskart earns a Strong rating (77 out of 100), and the reason is that one structural choice — making its own frames and owning the shop the customer walks into — pays off in several places at once. The company manufactures at Bhiwadi and now Hyderabad with 75% factory automation, and sells through 3,327 stores plus its own app and website, where 45% of India sales are digitally influenced. That combination scores a strong fit on both the Forward Integrators and Low Price Plus patterns: management states a 35–40% cost advantage at 64–69% product margins, and the quality framework's 'Intangible benefits' check notes the company keeps ₹71 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs. It also explains why the risk review scores Good Enough Goods as low — Lenskart is the cheap, scaled attacker taking business from unorganised opticians, not the incumbent being undercut. Growth is being paid for out of the business itself: 542 net new India stores in FY26 (twice the prior year) and the new plant were covered by ₹887 crore of operating cash flow (91% of EBITDA), the balance sheet holds ₹3,881 crore of net cash, return on capital rose 9 percentage points to 23%, and stores pay back in roughly ten months.

The share-gain story sits on top of that cost base and is the most concrete evidence in the records. India share is under 5% of a ₹79,000 crore market that is projected to reach ₹4 lakh crore by FY45, 47% of surveyed customers came across from the unorganised trade, and sales in pincodes where Lenskart already operates grew 26% over nine months and 31% in the fourth quarter — faster than same-store growth, which suggests adding stores in a locality brings in new demand rather than splitting existing demand. Three things feed this. First, brand: absolute marketing spend keeps rising but has fallen from 9.7% of revenue in FY23 to 7.5%, with customer satisfaction scores climbing from 70 to 81.4 — cheaper demand, not bought demand. Second, the eye test: roughly 6 million tests a quarter in India, about half of them first-time exams, with remote-optometry stores up from 168 to 623 in a year. This is why the Friendly Middleman pattern is only a partial fit — the expert who writes the prescription is Lenskart's own employee rather than an independent professional, which makes the funnel more controllable but less of a genuine third-party endorsement. Third, long-lived research bets: 500+ engineers, and management traces today's results to decisions made seven years ago (location-intelligence tool GeoIQ, frame manufacturing) and 36 months ago (remote optometry).

The risks are largely the same traits viewed from the other side, which is what makes them worth watching rather than dismissing. The brand that lowers marketing cost is increasingly being carried by trend-led assets — Meller fashion sunglasses and a rolling slate of pop-culture tie-ins (Batman, Harry Potter, Hello Kitty, Pop Mart, Stranger Things) — so Fashion Risk is rated elevated, softened only by the fact that the underlying demand is prescription correction, with eye tests up around 50% and units up 25%. The innovation record that earns a strong pattern fit is simultaneously the reason Technological Innovation is on watch: management is itself steering toward a hardware and AI platform shift ('B by Lenskart', becoming an 'intelligence company'), which invites comparison with tech-first entrants, though prescription lenses and fitting remain the gate any such entrant must pass. And the cost advantage that makes the low-price pattern work is precisely why Pricing Power is a clear no-fit: management repeatedly denies taking price increases, the 7% average selling price move was product mix, and the 64% fourth-quarter margin held only because vertical integration absorbed rupee depreciation — margin here is an efficiency outcome, not a pricing right. The framework's own soft spots line up with this: 'Persistence of growth' scores negative because roughly 46% average annual sales growth has been lumpy year to year, and the growth, industry-structure and return-on-capital areas score lowest, reflecting a short and uneven record rather than a broken one. Cyclicality, government dependency, customer concentration, new entrants and channel shift all score low, giving a risk safety score of 86 and an overall resilience read of sound.

## What to watch

- Fashion Risk (elevated): if Meller and the pop-culture collaborations grow into a larger share of sales than repeat prescription demand, the earnings base becomes trend-dependent.
- Pricing Power (no fit): the 64–69% product margin is held without price increases, so any input-cost or currency move that vertical integration cannot absorb hits margin directly.
- Capex for growth check and Market Share Gainers: watch whether ~10-month store paybacks, 23% return on capital and same-pincode growth above same-store growth hold as the store count keeps doubling — and whether expansion stays funded from operating cash flow rather than the ₹3,881 crore net cash pile or new borrowing.
- Persistence of growth (scored negative): year-to-year growth has been lumpy, so a single soft year would test whether the compounding story management describes is actually steady.
- Technological Innovation (watch): the shift toward 'B by Lenskart' smart glasses and an 'intelligence company' framing is a new spending line whose returns are unproven, unlike the older GeoIQ and remote-optometry bets that have already converted.
