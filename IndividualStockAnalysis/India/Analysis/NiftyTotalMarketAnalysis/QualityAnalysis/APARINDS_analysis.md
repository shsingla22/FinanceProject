# Apar Inds. (APARINDS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

APAR Industries is a roughly six-decade-old Indian manufacturer, founded in 1958 and still run by the founding family (Kushal and Chaitanya Desai), that makes the physical hardware used to move electricity around: overhead power conductors, electrical cables, and transformer oils and lubricants. It sells mainly to electric utilities, transmission companies and EPC contractors, renewable (wind and solar) developers, transformer and equipment makers, distributors, and increasingly to US data-centre contractors, earning money by manufacturing and shipping these products in volume — profitability is tracked per tonne of conductor and per kilolitre of oil, and improves when the mix shifts to higher-priced 'premium' products. It operates about eight plants (three for oils, two for conductors, two for cables, one of the oil plants in Sharjah/UAE free trade zone), is based in Mumbai with its registered office in Vadodara, and exports to 140-plus countries. Exports have run around 27%-50% of revenue over the period, with consolidated revenue growing from Rs. 9,346 crores in FY22 to an all-time high of Rs. 22,902 crores in FY26 (about 29% CAGR over five years) and FY26 profit after tax of Rs. 977 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Conductors** — Makes the aluminium and alloy overhead wires that carry electricity on transmission lines — including 'premium' higher-margin products, reconductoring (replacing wire on existing lines) and OPGW fibre-optic ground wire — and is by far the biggest division, at Rs. 12,712 crores of FY26 revenue (up 33%, crossing the Rs. 10,000 crore mark) with a pending order book of about Rs. 10,190 crores as of Q1 FY27; management says APAR is among the largest such manufacturers globally.
- **Specialty Oils & Lubricants (Oil division)** — Produces transformer oils plus automotive and industrial lubricants, with capacity of about 5.5 lakh kilolitres a year; management calls APAR the world's third largest transformer oil maker, and the division did roughly Rs. 3,500 crores in FY22 and Rs. 1,701 crores in the single quarter Q1 FY27 — its earnings swing sharply with crude and base-oil prices.
- **Cables** — Makes power and specialty cables, where APAR says it is the largest domestic supplier to India's renewable sector (about a 60% share in wind and a high share in solar) and is now approved in the US for cables going into Meta, Microsoft and Google data centres; revenue grew from about Rs. 2,000 crores in FY22 to Rs. 1,838 crores in Q1 FY27 alone.
- **Light Duty Cables (LDC), within the Cable division** — A consumer/retail-facing housing-wire and light cable business the company is deliberately building out with its own sales team, distribution channels and brand advertising (it signed Sonu Sood as brand ambassador in 2022) — described as a growth push rather than a large contributor at the time.
- **Polymers and Automotive solutions** — A small division mentioned alongside the main three; management describes it as minor, with oils, conductors and cables accounting for essentially all of the business.

### The story in depth

Apar's record reads as one connected story about premiumisation. A decade-long push into higher-specification products — E-beam wires, HTLS and OPGW conductors, CTC and busbars — lifted the premium share of conductors from 25-30% in FY19-21 to 45.8% in FY26 and 50.3% in Q1FY27, and conductor profit per tonne rose from Rs.17,095 in FY22 to Rs.53,418 in Q1FY27. That single trait explains most of the good news across all three analyses: it is why revenue compounded about 29% from FY21 to FY26 while margins rose rather than fell, which is what earns the STRONG FIT on the Market Share Gainers pattern; it is why the quality framework gives top marks to Pricing, Mix and Volume, noting that in Q1FY27 conductor volumes actually fell 6.7% while revenue rose 19.9%; and it is why the Good Enough Goods and Technological Innovation risks stay contained at the premium end, where utility qualification and installation capability are genuine barriers. Alongside it sits a second strength that the records treat as equally durable: the people. Stable promoter-family leadership with the same CFO throughout, promises on cables and premiumisation delivered as stated, and management that discloses bad news with specifics — the FY24 base-oil supply failure, a Rs.31cr one-off provision, an admitted 12.2% versus a 15% target — produce both a top score on candor and the second STRONG FIT, on Corporate Culture.

The awkward part is that several of the same traits that create the strengths also create the risks. The business is deliberately asset-light and high-turnover — every Rs.1 of assets generates Rs.1.67 of sales, on EBITDA margins of 9-12% on FY26 revenue of Rs.22,902 crores — and that low-cost, high-throughput model is exactly what supports the Low Price Plus pattern. But thin margins on a commodity input base are also why Cyclicality is the one ELEVATED risk: conductor volumes fell 6.7% in Q1FY27 because customers withheld manufacturing clearance on unhedged aluminium, and oil profit per kilolitre swung between Rs.7,004 and Rs.25,482 on crude and gas-oil moves. The same doubling applies to demand. The structural growth the quality framework rewards — India's transmission network going from about 5 lakh to 6.5 lakh circuit km by 2032, record 50GW+ renewable additions, data centre capacity heading from 1.5-1.7GW to 5-8GW — is largely state-directed, which is precisely why Government Dependency lands on WATCH, with US Section 232 tariff classifications having frozen ordering for a quarter. Management's candid supplier disclosures are the source of the Stakeholder Concentration warning: one major refinery's turnaround forced spot buying and hit oil profitability, contracted refinery supplies were cut 50% in April 2026, and specialty cable polymers come from a narrow base. And the pricing gains, on close reading, are mix gains rather than the ability to raise prices above cost inflation — which is why Pricing Power is only PARTIAL and why management itself flagged domestic capacity pressure on the same call.

Netted out, the rating is 63 out of 100 — Decent, three stars — built from 72 on business quality, 48 on multibagger fit and 66 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged STRAINED. The gap between the strong quality score and the weak pattern score is explained by how many patterns stop at PARTIAL: the toll-road-style approval barriers, the brand, the innovation lead, the move toward the end customer and the cost to replicate are all real but incomplete, and Recurring Revenue is a clear NO FIT — every rupee is order-book execution and physical shipment, with nothing renewing on its own and working capital funded rather than customer-funded. That absence matters more than usual because spending is climbing fast: capex has gone from roughly Rs.350-400cr in FY24 to Rs.740cr in FY26 with Rs.1,500cr planned for FY27, all of it for new capacity, while the business generates Rs.15 of operating cash per Rs.100 of capital employed and runs a roughly 30-day cash cycle. The weakest area in the whole quality framework is Industry Structure, scoring 0.17 out of 2, and it lines up exactly with the two competitive risks on WATCH: domestic players adding conductor and cable capacity, Chinese competition helped by a $330-360 per tonne aluminium price gap, and competitors already copying the B2C wire playbook. So the story is a well-run, honest, genuinely improving manufacturer climbing the value ladder inside an industry that keeps trying to pull it back down — the execution deserves credit, but the structure is doing none of the work for it.

## What to watch

- Premium conductor mix and profit per tonne (Pricing, Mix and Volume; Market Share Gainers): if premium share stalls around 50% or EBITDA/tonne slips back toward the Rs.43,688 level, the mix-led growth engine stops, since price rises above cost inflation were never the source.
- Base-oil and specialty polymer supply (Stakeholder Concentration): after the 50% cut in contracted refinery supplies in April 2026, another disruption would hit oil profitability the way the earlier refinery turnaround did.
- The Rs.1,500cr FY27 capex plan versus internal cash generation (Capex for growth; Return on capital): with a 15% cash return on capital, no recurring revenue stream, and working capital already funded internally, a bigger spend on softer demand is what turns STRAINED resilience into actual strain.
- New domestic and Chinese capacity pressing on price (New Entrants; Good Enough Goods; Industry Structure at 0.17): watch whether the commodity end of conductors and standard cables starts dragging blended margins down faster than premiumisation lifts them, and whether B2C wire competitors copying the playbook erode that channel.
- Policy-driven order timing (Government Dependency; Cyclicality): another tariff-classification or clearance pause like the Section 232 freeze or the withheld manufacturing clearance on unhedged aluminium would show how much of the demand story depends on decisions the company does not control.
