# Travel Food (TRAVELFOOD) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Travel Food Services (TFS) runs food-and-drink outlets and passenger lounges inside airports. Its customers are air travellers passing through the terminals, and its clients are the airport operators who award it long-term concession contracts (often a 'master concession' bundling many units at one airport). It makes money by selling food, beverages and lounge access at these outlets, paying the airport a concession fee out of that revenue; some airports are run through wholly-owned long-term contracts that sit fully in its own P&L, and others through joint ventures with large multi-airport operators, where it earns a management fee plus a share of the JV's profit. It operates at 20 airports in India plus international lounges in Malaysia and Hong Kong, with system-wide sales of INR 32 billion and adjusted PAT of INR 4.5 billion in FY26.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Travel QSR** — Quick-service food and beverage outlets in airport terminals, run under 130-145+ brands (global partners like Nando's, Gordon Ramsay and Wagamama, regional partners like Bikanervala and Sri Krishna Sweets, and in-house brands like idli.com, Cafeccino and Delhi Street); management calls TFS the leading player with about 26% market share, and travel QSR plus lounges together exceeded 550 units by FY26, with 76 new QSR units opened during the year.
- **Lounges** — Paid airport lounges where passengers wait, eat and rest, typically accessed via credit-card and bank tie-ups; TFS says it is the country's leading lounge operator with about 45% market share, running 37 lounges as of June 2025 across India, Malaysia and Hong Kong, and it is pushing premium add-ons such as sleeping pods and new Kyra lounges internationally.
- **Joint ventures / strategic partnerships** — Airport contracts held jointly with two of India's largest multi-airport operators, covering QSR and lounges at Mumbai, Hyderabad, Ahmedabad, Navi Mumbai, Lucknow, Goa Mopa and others; these do not show in consolidated sales but are counted in 'system-wide sales' and contribute a growing share of profit via management fees and equity pickup.
- **Lounge aggregation / EATS technology platform** — A technology platform giving banks and credit-card issuers direct lounge access for their customers without intermediaries; launched in FY26, it is described as small but growing, adding to gross margin and other expenses, with plans to layer on more ancillary services for incremental revenue.
- **International expansion** — Lounge operations outside India — currently Malaysia and Hong Kong (a second Kyra Lounge opened at Hong Kong airport with partners SSP and Airport Dimensions) — with management actively chasing more lounge opportunities in Asia-Pacific and the Middle East; still a small part of the business but the stated growth avenue.
- **Highway / wayside amenities** — Food and beverage stops along access-controlled expressways in India, which management repeatedly calls a 'very nascent' opportunity it is only exploring, with no meaningful revenue yet but seen as a growth area over the next decade.

### The story in depth

Travel Food Services earns an overall 81 out of 100 — an "Outstanding" five-star rating built from 77 on business quality, 94 on multibagger fit and 72 on risk safety — and the reason is that almost everything good about the company traces back to one thing: it holds long-term master concessions for the food and lounge space inside airports. That single structure shows up as a "Mini-monopolies" strength (effectively the only food choice for a passenger who has already cleared security, with 26% of travel quick-service and 45% of the lounge market nationally), as a "Distribution advantage" (550+ outlets and lounges across 20 airports, all three Delhi terminals, backed by central kitchens and central stores), and as the "Cost to Replicate" pattern rated a strong fit, because airside space is finite, awarded by tender, and needs security clearance and back-of-house capital that a newcomer cannot easily assemble. The financial signature matches: after direct input costs the company keeps ₹85 of every ₹100 of sales, gross margin rose to 84.7% for FY26 and 87.3% in the fourth quarter, cash comes back from customers 438 days before suppliers have to be paid, and the balance sheet carries no debt against ₹8.4 billion of cash. Growth capex — 76 quick-service units mobilised in FY26, roughly 70 outlets still under construction, new airports at Navi Mumbai, Noida, Cochin and Delhi T1 and T2 — is paid for out of that cash at about a 50% return on capital employed.

The patterns and the risks are two readings of the same facts. "Market Share Gainers" is a strong fit because the network went from 491 units at 19 airports to 550+ at 20, with wins at Delhi T1 (33 units over 11 years), T2, Cochin and Navi Mumbai — but every one of those positions sits with a small number of counterparties, which is why Stakeholder Concentration is rated elevated: master concessions with two large multi-airport operators and joint-venture structures, Delhi and Mumbai dominant, and Delhi T3 currently running on an extension to 30 September before going to open tender. The same landlord relationship produces the Government Dependency watch flag, since growth depends on the state's airport privatisation and greenfield tender pipeline and policy decisions land directly on revenue. "Friendly Middleman" is rated a partial fit precisely because banks and card issuers pay for lounge access on the traveller's behalf, a payer who does not mind the price — and that same arrangement is the Shifting Consumer Preferences risk, because those banks have spent 18 months tightening card-funded lounge entry on a stream worth about 41% of revenue. The EATS platform sits on both sides too: it is the evidence for the "Forward Integrators" pattern and part of "Innovation Dominance", and it is also management's answer to that lounge risk, selling bank-to-lounge access directly instead of through aggregators. Corporate culture scores as a strong fit on plain follow-through — about 50 units guided became 76 mobilised, Cochin opened on schedule, and bad news such as the labour-code charge and a litigation provision was disclosed promptly — which is the same discipline the checks describe as zero debt, no acquisitions and a returns-gated approach to the highway opportunity.

The soft spots cluster in one area: how dependable the growth is. Persistence of growth is the only badly scored check at -2 — sales have averaged about 19% a year but arrive unevenly — and the growth and industry-structure scores are the weakest of the quality areas, with return on capital lower still. FY26 showed why: passenger traffic at the company's airports grew just 1.2% against 8-9% historically after the India-Pakistan conflict, the Air India crash groundings and crew-rest rule changes, which is the basis for the elevated Cyclicality rating. The company delivered anyway, with like-for-like sales up 9.4% for the year and 12.5% in the third quarter, but that came almost entirely from premium formats, higher spend per passenger and better mix rather than more travellers — the same fact that makes "Pricing Power" only a likely rather than strong fit, since management says it raises prices only in line with inflation. The three lowest risks — technological change, new entrants and cheaper good-enough substitutes — are all low for the same reason the strengths are high: a competitor cannot simply appear beside the outlet when the concession decides who is allowed to sell there. Overall resilience is judged sound, and the honest read is a business whose returns are excellent and whose main uncertainty is not competition but whether footfall recovers and whether the concessions keep being renewed at the 94% rate they have held since 2009.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Stakeholder Concentration. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.2× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 78% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 14% to 40% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 3 numeric fingerprints reinforce it.

**What the calls show:** Demand is a flow of passenger footfall rather than customer capex, but FY26 showed how event-sensitive it is: traffic at TFS airports grew just 1.2% for the year (vs 8-9% historically) after the India-Pakistan conflict, the Air India crash groundings, FDTL crew-rest curtailment and the Middle East conflict, with Q2 traffic actually declining ~1%. Sales and PAT still compounded ~25% and ~21% on unit additions and LFL, showing the swing is in the demand driver, not in a commodity price or a customer's investment cycle.

> "passenger traffic at TFS managed airports saw a muted growth of 1.2% year-on-year for the full year FY26" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Operations-linked, repeat consumable demand (food at airports) with quick post-disruption recovery; countercyclical build-out — 76 QSR units mobilised and network taken past 550 outlets in the weakest traffic year; zero debt and ~INR8.4bn cash funding expansion through the downturn*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±86 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from -22% to 39% (swinging ±20.1 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 23% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 6% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Stakeholder Concentration — ELEVATED
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but the only computed fingerprint does not confirm it — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Results hinge on a handful of counterparties: master concessions with two large multi-airport operators (GMR JV, Adani-linked airports) and JV structures, with Delhi and Mumbai the dominant hubs; Delhi T3 is on an extension to 30 September and goes to open tender with management unable to say who will win, and analysts pressed on both that renewal and Kolkata potentially moving to Adani. The lounge half of the business (41% of consolidated revenue) additionally depends on banks and card issuers who are unilaterally tightening lounge access.

> "The contract, as you are aware, has been extended till September 30th. When the contract will come up for renewal, people will bid for it… I can't say where it will land because it's an open tender." — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: 94% contract renewal rate since 2009; broadening base — 20 airports, 550+ outlets, 145 brands, three Delhi terminals so terminal-level traffic shifts are internalised, plus international lounges in Malaysia and Hong Kong*

- ✅ Customers pay in about 36 days (was 41) — no sign of weakening bargaining power in collection terms.
