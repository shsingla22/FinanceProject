# Piramal Finance. (PIRAMALFIN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Piramal Finance Limited (formerly Piramal Capital & Housing Finance) is an Indian non-banking finance company (NBFC) listed on the BSE and NSE that lends money to individuals and small businesses, mostly across semi-urban India, and serves about 6 million customers through a network of roughly 780 branches combined with technology and AI-driven processes. Its main products are home loans and loans against property, plus used car loans, personal loans, business loans, digital loans, gold loans and rural micro loans; alongside this it also lends to real estate developers and mid-market companies. It earns money the way a lender does — borrowing funds (cost of borrowing about 8.8%) and lending them out at higher yields of roughly 13.2%-13.4%, leaving a net interest margin of about 6.5% — supplemented by fee income such as loan processing fees, insurance distribution commissions (via partner Pramerica Life) and occasional loan sell-down (direct assignment) transactions. Total assets under management were about INR 1,06,940 crores as of the June 2026 quarter, growing around 25% year-on-year, with FY26 consolidated net profit of INR 1,506 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Retail lending** — Loans to individual and small-business customers across six-plus product categories, at INR 91,249 crores of AUM (Q1 FY27) and roughly 85% of the company's total book — management calls it the fastest retail scale-up among Indian NBFCs, going from about INR 20,000 crores to INR 85,000 crores in four years.
- **Mortgages (housing loans and LAP)** — The single biggest product line — home loans and loans against property, including 'mass affluent' home loans and larger-ticket 'LAP Plus' — at INR 61,199 crores, about 57% of the whole company's AUM and 67% of retail.
- **Unsecured lending (personal loans, unsecured business loans, digital loans, rural micro lending)** — Loans given without collateral, at INR 21,412 crores and about 20% of company AUM / 23% of retail, growing 45% year-on-year, with credit risk described as being at its best levels in over two years.
- **Used car loans** — A secured retail product financing second-hand cars; small enough that no separate size is given, but it was singled out for a rise in bad loans in the first half of FY26 before newer loans stabilised the risk.
- **Gold loans** — A newly launched business lending against gold, started with 22 branches in Maharashtra and Telangana in Q4 FY26 and expanded to 67 branches by June 2026, with a target of about 200 branches by March 2027 — described as one of two 'white spaces' the company is filling.
- **Rural / micro loans** — Very small loans sold through a dedicated rural branch network (178 branches by June 2026), with AUM of INR 1,384 crores in FY26 — small in absolute terms but growing over 40% and named as the other major 'white space'.
- **Wholesale 2.0 (real estate and mid-market lending)** — The newer corporate lending book — loans to real estate developers and mid-sized companies split roughly 70:30 between the two — at INR 13,238 crores, growing 27-38% year-on-year, with zero non-performing assets reported.
- **Legacy (discontinued) wholesale book** — The old corporate loan portfolio the company has been deliberately winding down, cut to INR 2,807 crores and under 3% of total AUM, versus 66% of AUM four years earlier.
- **Investments and stake monetisation** — Non-core holdings the company is selling off for cash rather than a continuing operating business — including the INR 600 crores sale of its Shriram Life Insurance stake and $148 million of deferred proceeds from the earlier Piramal Imaging sale, with more monetisations flagged as coming.
- **Piramal.ai / technology** — Not a revenue line but an internal capability management highlights heavily — in-house AI used across sales, underwriting, collections, audit, customer service and HR (token usage grew from 63 billion to 320 billion per quarter in a year), credited with cutting the retail operating-cost ratio to 3.5% of AUM and the cost-to-income ratio to 53%.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Piramal Finance comes out of these three reviews as a genuinely well-run lender that is doing what it said it would do, inside an industry that does not reward it very much. The quality framework gives full marks to Management and Capital Allocation: the legacy wholesale book has been run down from 66% of the loan book four years ago to 2%, capital adequacy sits at 18.85%, the liquidity ratio at 450%, short-term commercial paper is under 1% of liabilities, and growth has been built in-house rather than bought. The multibagger screen agrees from a different angle — Corporate Culture is the single STRONG FIT in the whole screen, for the simple reason that promises made on one earnings call show up as results on the next: 100 branches guided in Q3 were opened in Q4, the legacy book guided to INR 3,000-3,500cr landed at INR 2,807cr, and full-year profit guidance of INR 1,300-1,500cr came in at INR 1,506cr. The same discipline shows in the decision to pause branch and AI spending for seven quarters and then restart it at scale — 100 branches in Q4FY26 and 79 in Q1FY27 toward roughly 200 gold branches. Against that, the risk review finds no single-customer or single-partner dependency worth worrying about, and the overall resilience read is SOUND.

The more interesting finding is that the traits earning the highest marks are, in several cases, the very traits creating the biggest risks. Growth is the clearest example: the company is taking share far faster than the market (retail loan book up 33% and disbursements up 44% year-on-year, customers from 5.4mn to 6.0mn, total book INR 1.07 lakh crore), which is why Market Share Gainers is a LIKELY FIT and the share-gain check scores top marks. But the machine that produces that growth is a balance sheet being leveraged harder — assets to equity moving from 3.5x to 3.7x — and that is exactly why the asset-turn check scores at the bottom, with every INR 1 of assets producing only INR 0.11 of sales. Pricing is the second example. The company refused to cut its retail lending rate through the entire down-cycle and holds personal-loan yields above 17%, which reads as pricing discipline in the screen (Pricing Power, PARTIAL) and as exposure in the risk review, where Good Enough Goods is ELEVATED precisely because competitors are offering single-digit rates on a product that is easy to substitute. The cost story ties the same knot: costs as a share of the loan book have fallen for three and a half straight years to 3.5% and the cost-to-income ratio has gone from 72% to 53%, helped by an AI build that is deep rather than cosmetic — five times the processing volume at flat cost, home-grown models, collection bots matching human performance. That effort keeps Technological Innovation risk LOW and supports the Innovation Dominance and Low Price Plus patterns, but the screen notes those savings are being kept as margin rather than handed to customers, which is what leaves the price-competition risk open.

The weak links sit in the industry rather than in the company. Industry Structure is the worst-scoring quality area, with profit margins swinging by nearly 99 points and the observation that there is little to keep newcomers out beyond capital and a licence — which is the same finding the risk review reaches when it marks New Entrants as ELEVATED, citing mid-sized banks crowding into retail and small-business lending and a tightening rate environment that already dented one quarter's disbursements. The screen's Cost to Replicate verdict lands in the same place: the AA+ rating worth 50-80 basis points of funding cost, the 780-branch semi-urban network and the proprietary technology stack are real advantages, but rebuildable ones. Cyclicality is also ELEVATED, partly self-inflicted — management describes margin pressure from rate cuts as cyclical rather than structural, calls one quarter's flat disbursements internal process-driven, and treats the fintech partner channel behind the Friendly Middleman pattern as something it can cut back at will. Return on Capital is the heaviest single drag anywhere in the records: every INR 100 of shareholder funds and borrowings produced negative INR 15 of operating cash this year, and long-run sales growth of about 6% a year has been lumpy. That arithmetic is what produces the Mixed verdict of 47 out of 100 — decent business quality at 54, solid risk safety at 62, but a multibagger fit of only 23, because outside Corporate Culture and share gains, six patterns are only PARTIAL and three do not apply at all.

### Corporate Culture — STRONG FIT

## What to watch

- Whether personal-loan yields can stay above 17% with the retail lending rate uncut — if borrower attrition breaks above the sub-10% level, the Good Enough Goods risk (ELEVATED) has beaten the Pricing Power pattern.

- Delivery of the remaining branch build-out toward roughly 200 gold branches, after 100 in Q4FY26 and 79 in Q1FY27 — the Corporate Culture STRONG FIT rests entirely on guided numbers arriving as promised.

- Cost-to-income holding at or below 53% and the AA+ funding benefit of 50-80 basis points as mid-sized banks push into retail — this is the only real defence against the ELEVATED New Entrants risk and the bottom-scoring barriers-to-entry check.
