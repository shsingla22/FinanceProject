# BlackBuck (BLACKBUCK) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

BlackBuck (legally Zinka Logistics Solutions Ltd, renamed BlackBuck Limited) runs India's largest digital platform for truck owners — mostly small operators who own fewer than five trucks in a highly fragmented, largely unorganised trucking market. Through its mobile app, backed by an offline network of more than 10,000 touchpoints (salespeople, technicians, channel partners such as garages and lubricant shops, and call centres), it sells truckers a bundle of services: toll and fuel payments, GPS and mandate-driven vehicle tracking devices, fuel sensors, digital fleet documents, help finding loads, and loans. It earns money from subscription fees collected upfront from customers, from commissions/spreads on the payments flowing through the platform (about ₹6,800 crore of payment GTV in a single quarter), from selling telematics hardware and services, and from lending. It operates only in India, served about 8.2–8.5 lakh monthly transacting truck operators (roughly a quarter to 30% of the country's ~3.5 million truck operators) and reported FY26 total income of about ₹715 crore, up 55%, with ₹190 crore EBITDA and ₹160 crore PAT — its first full year of profit.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Core businesses** — The established, cash-generating half of the company — payments/tolling plus telematics — which grew about 30–34% in FY26 and funds the investment in the newer lines.
- **Payments (tolling and fuel payments)** — Lets truckers pay tolls and buy fuel digitally through the app; management calls it a core revenue lever, with toll GTV up 27% in FY26 versus industry growth of about 16%, so its market share keeps rising.
- **Telematics** — Sells and services vehicle-tracking hardware — basic GPS, regulator-mandated AIS tracking devices, and fuel sensors — described as the second core vertical, with record incremental sales in Q3 FY26 and AIS unit sales doubling in Q4 FY26.
- **Growth (newer) businesses** — The loss-making, heavily invested expansion bucket made up of Superloads and vehicle finance; small in absolute terms but growing roughly 271% year-on-year in Q3 FY26 and about 300% in Q4 FY26.
- **Superloads** — A load-matching business connecting cargo with truckers, still in what management calls the playbook-building phase — expanded from four cities to nine and then to ten new cities on top of the original Bangalore and Hyderabad hubs, with the newer cities scaling faster than the first ones.
- **Vehicle finance** — Lends to truck operators to buy vehicles; the older of the two growth businesses, with disbursals up 30% and then a further 25% in consecutive quarters, and management expects it to stop consuming cash and start generating it by the end of FY27.
- **The BlackBuck app (platform) and distribution** — Not a revenue line but the two assets management calls the crown jewel and the moat — truckers spend about 45 minutes a day in the app, and the 10,000-plus offline touchpoints mean each new offering can be launched at close to zero incremental cost; both were largely built out before FY24.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

BlackBuck earns a middling overall grade of 55 out of 100, or "Decent," and the reason is a wide gap between what the business does well and what the numbers so far show. On the quality side, its strongest areas are competitive advantage (1.33 out of 2), customer benefits (1.25) and management (1.17), while growth (0.5) and return on capital (0.0) score at or near the bottom. Almost every strength traces back to one asset: an omnichannel network of 10,000 physical touchpoints — salespeople, technicians, channel partners, call centres — reaching every significant trucking village, backed by an app truckers spend 45 minutes a day inside. That same asset is what the checks credit for convenience, distribution advantage and above-industry growth, and it is also what management points to as a seven-to-eight-year replication barrier that is now largely paid for. Growth has been entirely organic and funded from the core business's own cash, with the lending book kept asset-light at roughly 10% on own books, and about 15 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers.

The patterns line up behind the same two engines and then run out of road. Corporate culture is the only strong fit: the same three-pillar strategy has been repeated unchanged for five-plus years through the IPO, the operating-leverage promise made in December 2024 turned into FY26 EBITDA of INR190cr and a first full-year profit of INR160cr, and bad news is volunteered — loss-making new verticals, an unproven SuperLoads, the OMC loyalty suspension. Recurring revenue and market share gains are likely fits, both well evidenced: subscriptions are prepaid at roughly 93% contribution margin with minimal churn, which is why Q1FY26 operating cash of INR63cr came in above INR40cr of EBITDA, and tolling volumes grew 27% against 16% industry growth with share in the late 40s and acquisition share running higher. Six further patterns — friendly middleman, toll roads, low price plus, innovation dominance, forward integrators and cost to replicate — land only as partial fits, each for the same reason: the qualitative story is there but the specific proof is not (no itemised savings passed to price, no R&D disclosure, no owned stores, no claim that the product is a trivial share of a customer's cost). Pricing power appears only as a screening hint from the numbers with nothing in the calls behind it, and brand strength does not fit at all. Against this, one check sits sharply out of place: persistence of growth scores -2, with sales down about 6% a year on average and lumpy year to year — a reported revenue line that does not yet match the share-gain story, and the main arithmetic reason the growth and return-on-capital areas drag the rating down.

The risks are largely the strengths seen from the other side. The tolling business that produces the clearest market-share evidence rides on the government's NETC/FASTag rails, and the fastest-growing telematics category sells on the back of AIS mandates — so the very regulation that anchors the toll-roads pattern is also the single high risk, government dependency. The same dependency shows up again as a technology watch item: the government's MLFF postpaid tolling rollout, targeting 200 corridors and nationwide by 2029-30, would undo the prepaid wallet format that makes the recurring-revenue pattern work in the first place. Prepaid, flow-linked revenue cuts both ways too: it funds the business with customer advances, but management itself indexes to the truck cycle and calls the second half the positive season, leaving cyclicality elevated. Customer concentration is effectively nil at roughly 8.2 lakh monthly transacting operators, but the partner side is concentrated — the fuel business depends on OMC loyalty programmes, whose suspension already dented Q4 FY26 profitability. And the 10,000-touchpoint network is the stated defence against both remaining watch risks: new entrants, which management openly expects the attractive space to keep drawing, and good-enough goods, since basic GPS trackers and FASTag are standardised products a cheaper rival could functionally match. Only fashion risk and shifting consumer preferences rate low, because demand is compulsory operational spend and the shift to cashless trucking runs in the company's favour. Overall resilience is judged sound; the verdict is decent rather than strong because the durable moat and candid management are not yet showing up in reported growth or returns.
