# Healthcare Global (HCG) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Healthcare Global Enterprises (HCG) runs a chain of specialist cancer hospitals across India, describing itself as the only organized, pan-India cancer care chain, with centres in roughly 18-20 cities spanning metros and tier-2/tier-3 towns plus one overseas operation in Kenya. It makes money by charging patients (and their payors — insurance, government-sponsored schemes, and cash-paying and overseas patients) for cancer diagnosis and treatment: medical oncology including chemotherapy, immunotherapy and CAR-T, surgical oncology including robotic surgery, radiation therapy on machines such as CyberKnife and MR-LINAC, plus supporting diagnostics like PET-CT, digital pathology and genomic sequencing. Revenue comes from patient volumes multiplied by average revenue per patient, which was about INR 84,000 in Q3 FY26; full-year FY26 revenue was INR 2,545 crore, up 15%, with an adjusted EBITDA margin around 17-19%. Historically it also owned a fertility/IVF business (Milann) and a few multispecialty hospitals, but in FY26 the board approved selling Milann to focus purely on oncology.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Oncology hospital network (the core business)** — Cancer hospitals and day-care centres offering medical, surgical and radiation oncology under one roof, tumour-board-led care and complex/high-acuity cases; this is essentially the whole company — about INR 618 crore of the INR 633 crore Q3 FY26 revenue came from it excluding fertility.
- **South cluster** — Centres led by the Bangalore Centre of Excellence plus Vizag and other tier-2 sites; about 39% of FY26 revenue, growing ~13%, and home to the newly opened North Bangalore hospital (120+ beds, first MR-LINAC in the city).
- **West cluster** — Hospitals across Gujarat and Maharashtra including Ahmedabad and Mumbai; the largest contributor at about 45% of FY26 revenue, growing ~14% on capacity expansion and new clinicians.
- **East cluster** — Centres in Cuttack, Ranchi and Kolkata; about 11% of FY26 revenue growing ~11%, with Kolkata still ramping up after turning EBITDA-positive.
- **International business (Kenya)** — An overseas oncology operation offering radiation oncology and PET services; management calls it a smaller part of the portfolio but it grew 71% in FY26.
- **Medical value travel** — Treating international patients who travel to HCG's Indian hospitals, supported by overseas information centres; a growth initiative rather than a large segment, and it was noted as soft in Q4 FY26.
- **Milann — fertility business** — An IVF/infertility chain that was roughly 4% of revenue in FY23 (about INR 15 crore of INR 633 crore in Q3 FY26) and is being sold to Inviga Healthcare Fund at an enterprise value of INR 63 crore so HCG can focus on oncology.
- **Digital patient acquisition** — Website, mobile app and marketing campaigns that bring patients in directly and reduce reliance on third-party aggregators; digital revenue grew 26% year-on-year in Q3 FY26, with the website contributing 67% and the app 13% of digital revenue.
- **Research, genomics and clinical trials** — Genomic sequencing, molecular/precision-medicine capability, an in-house research committee and pharma-partnered clinical trials, presented mainly as a clinical differentiator rather than a separately sized revenue line.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Healthcare Global comes out as a Strong business — 67 out of 100, or four stars — and the score is built from three uneven parts: a business-quality mark of 63, a multibagger-pattern fit of 58, and a much higher risk-safety mark of 84. The strength sits almost entirely on the customer and demand side. The company's promise is certainty of outcome rather than a cheap price — sub-1% mortality, tumour-board-led protocols, repeated success in cases that fail elsewhere — and it sells that promise into a market that is expanding permanently, with Indian cancer incidence rising and far too few comprehensive cancer centres to meet it. That combination shows up in the numbers: after direct input costs the company keeps ₹73 of every ₹100 of sales, ₹18 of every ₹100 ends up as operating profit, margins have moved only about two points a year, and revenue has compounded steadily across fifteen quarterly calls from 2022 to 2026 (26% growth in Q1 FY23, 13% in FY24, 15% in FY26) with operating margin grinding up from roughly 17.7% to 18.5%. Best of all, customers pay 93 days before suppliers need to be paid, so the customer base effectively funds the working capital of the business — a genuinely rare feature. This is also why the risk review is so calm: cancer treatment is non-discretionary and repeat, demand is clinical rather than fashionable, and revenue is spread across 20-plus cities and four clusters (West about 45%, South about 39%, East about 11%, and Kenya), so cyclicality, fashion risk, shifting preferences and stakeholder concentration are all rated Low.

The eleven-pattern screen finds six likely fits, and they are best read as one story rather than six. Growth is bought by adding doctors and strengthening referral networks — 23 oncologists in a single quarter in Q4 FY26 — rather than by advertising to patients, which is the Friendly Middleman pattern; the referring oncologist decides where the patient goes. On top of that sits Innovation Dominance: every call names a new clinical capability, from genomics and Siemens imaging systems in FY23, to robotics and MR-LINAC in FY24, to CAR-T and the first MR-LINAC in Bangalore in FY26. Those capabilities, plus two decades spent building the only pan-India cancer-focused network and market-leading positions in 16 of 18 cities, are what give the Brand Strength and Market Share Gainer readings, and they are also why Cost to Replicate scores as a partial fit — the barriers are real and time-based, but management itself points to competitive intensity. Forward Integration is the one clean, self-funded advance: the company owns its patient-facing network and is building its own booking channels, with digital revenue up 75% in FY24 and 26% in Q3 FY26, the app scaling 4.5 times, day-care centres roughly tripling from five, and dependence on third-party aggregators cut from 12% to 9% — the same effort that turns the one genuine change in patient behaviour, researching and booking online, from a threat into a channel. Where the patterns break is price. Pricing Power is only a likely fit with a large caveat: growth is two-thirds volume-led, realisation rises only 3-5% a year, and government scheme and third-party administrator rates set the price rather than the brand — which is exactly why the Toll Road pattern is a clear no-fit. And because the company deliberately sits at the premium, complex end and exited low-margin volume in FY26, the low-price-plus route to scale is closed too.

The risks are mostly the same traits viewed from the other side, which is the key to reading this company. The technology that powers Innovation Dominance is also a heavy, depreciating installed base — linear accelerators, PET-CT, CyberKnife, robots — that must be refreshed forever; four new linear accelerators in FY24 temporarily dropped utilisation from 65%. That refresh cycle, plus new centres in Ahmedabad, Whitefield and North Bangalore and 200-plus added beds, is why capital spending is genuinely for growth rather than upkeep, why return on capital has improved from 10% in FY24 to 14% in FY26 but incremental returns are still only modest, and ultimately why shareholders received no dividend or buyback and were instead asked for ₹425 crore through a rights issue whose purpose management initially declined to specify — the single negative score in the whole quality framework. Likewise, the referral network that drives volume is the same intermediary that, alongside price-sensitive payers, caps what the company can charge, which is the Good Enough Goods watch item; and the government schemes that broaden the patient base are the acknowledged swing factor behind the Andhra Pradesh volume disruption in Q3 FY26 and the 3% fall in East realisation on the Odisha scheme transition. New entrants — large multi-specialty chains adding cancer blocks in HCG's own metros — are the reason management guides only 15% growth. None of these are rated worse than Watch, and the overall resilience reading is Sound. The soft spot is the internal scorecard rather than the market: competitive advantage, industry structure, management and return on capital all score at the bottom of the quality areas, and culture is only a partial fit because, alongside promises kept (Kolkata turning profitable as guided, new centres commissioned, net debt down from about 2.2 times earnings to 1.4 times, Milann divested to sharpen focus), leadership has churned heavily with three chairmen in five years. In short: an excellent demand position and an unusually kind cash cycle, held back by weak control over price and a still-unproven ability to convert heavy investment into high returns.

## What to watch

- Realisation, not volume: if the 3-5% a year gain in revenue per patient stalls or turns negative again as it did in the East, the Pricing Power and Brand Strength patterns weaken toward the Toll Roads no-fit reading.
- Return on capital: the improvement from 10% in FY24 to 14% in FY26 needs to keep going as Ahmedabad, Whitefield and North Bangalore fill up — flat or falling returns would undercut the 'capex for growth' check and the Innovation Dominance case at once.
- How the ₹425 crore rights issue is actually spent, and whether more dilution follows — the Distribution to Shareholders check is already the only negative score in the framework.
- Government scheme exposure: further disruptions like Andhra Pradesh or the Odisha transition would move the Government Dependency risk beyond Watch and pressure both volumes and realisation together.
- Leadership stability: another turn at the top would harden the partial Corporate Culture rating and the low Management score into a genuine problem, given how much of the growth plan depends on execution of new centres.
