# Yes Bank Ltd. (YESBANK) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

YES Bank is an Indian commercial bank, headquartered in Mumbai and listed on the NSE and BSE, that takes deposits from the public and lends the money out. It serves individual savers and borrowers, small and mid-sized businesses (MSME/SME), and large corporates and institutions across India, and also handles trade, foreign-exchange and cash-management flows for them, including cross-border business helped by its shareholder SMBC. It earns money mainly on the gap between interest charged on loans and interest paid on deposits (net interest income of about INR 9,776 crore in FY26, at a margin of roughly 2.6%-2.7%), plus fees from cards, payments, third-party product sales, wealth offerings, forex, trade and transaction banking (about INR 6,759 crore in FY26). By June 2026 it held about INR 3.15 lakh crore of deposits and INR 2.85 lakh crore of loans, and reported FY26 net profit of INR 3,476 crore; the bank was rebuilt after a 2020 reconstruction scheme backed by the Government of India, RBI and SBI, and now counts SMBC, SBI and Advent International as major shareholders.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Retail Banking** — Loans and everyday banking for individuals - home, vehicle, personal and card lending plus branch services - which grew fast early on (42% in Q1 FY23) but was deliberately slowed later for profitability and risk reasons, growing only 6.9% year-on-year by Q1 FY27 even as disbursements rose 27.5%.
- **Deposits / liability franchise (CASA and retail term deposits)** — Collecting current, savings and fixed-deposit money from customers - the bank's main funding source at about INR 3.15 lakh crore, with the cheaper current-and-savings (CASA) share raised from roughly 31% to 32%+ and retail/branch-led deposits now close to 60% of the total.
- **Commercial Banking / SME and Mid-Corporate** — Lending and banking services to small, medium and mid-sized companies, a priority growth engine that repeatedly grew about 26% a year in FY25 and 17% year-on-year by Q1 FY27.
- **Corporate and Institutional Banking (Wholesale Banking)** — Loans and banking for large companies and institutions; the book was shrunk deliberately after the reconstruction (down 9% in Q1 FY23) and later returned to strong growth, and it is where SMBC helps on corporate and cross-border business.
- **Transaction Banking (trade, cash management, forex)** — Fee-earning services that move companies' money - trade finance, cash management, foreign exchange and interchange on digital transactions - described as a key driver of the bank's growing fee income.
- **Digital payments and embedded banking** — Providing payment rails and banking plumbing to e-commerce and fintech partners, credited with a 50% jump in average daily current-account balances in FY23 and described in FY26 as the bank's leadership position in the digital payments ecosystem.
- **Stressed Asset Management / Security Receipts portfolio** — Recovering money from bad loans, including the sale of a roughly INR 48,000 crore pool of stressed assets to ARC partner JC Flowers in 2022; residual Security Receipts still produce recovery gains, guided at INR 800-1,000 crore for FY27.
- **Financial Markets / Treasury** — The bank's own investment and trading book, which produces treasury gains that management treats as non-core and separates out when explaining underlying earnings.
- **Priority-sector-related deposits (RIDF and mandated deposits)** — Low-yielding deposits the bank was forced to place because of past priority-sector-lending shortfalls; now down to about 6% of total assets from 9%, with running these off a stated lever for improving margins toward 3%.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

## The verdict: Decent — 51 out of 100 ★★★☆☆

### The story in depth

Yes Bank comes out of this review as a genuine repair story rather than a great business, and the combined rating says as much: 51 out of 100, or Decent, built from a business-quality score of 46 and a risk-safety score of 60. The strengths are real and they cluster in one place — growth and the people running it. The bank entered the window at its own private bottom, with bad loans at 13.4% of the book and management guiding to a 0.75% return on assets in July 2022, and has climbed to bad loans of 1.3% and a 1% quarterly return on assets. Deposits crossed INR 3 lakh crore, the share of cheap current and savings money rose from 30.8% to 35.1%, and retail and branch-led deposits are around 60% of the total. Management earned credit for doing this the slow way: no acquisitions, a deliberate slowdown in unsecured retail lending in FY25, full priority-sector compliance for two straight years, and a stated refusal to chase loans it has not funded with deposits. It also refuses to talk about the share price or takeover rumours on calls. The scoring reflects that split cleanly — growth scores positively, management and competitive advantage mildly positively, while returns on capital score the worst possible mark and industry structure is close behind.

The pattern screen shows why the good traits stop short of being an advantage. Four of the eleven patterns come back as partial and four as no fit, and in most cases the very thing that earns the partial credit is what blocks the full one. Deposit gathering is the clearest example: winning deposit and CASA share faster than the system is what makes Market Share Gainers a partial fit, but the bank wins those deposits by paying up for them, which is exactly why Pricing Power and the low-cost operator pattern are outright no fits — the CFO describes the competitive landscape as the thing that will ultimately determine the spread, margins sat at 2.4% for years and reached only 2.7%, costs still ate 66.7% of income in FY26 against a 62.8% exit rate, and return on equity is 8.3%. The same story repeats on brand: rating upgrades, awards and a premium sub-brand support a partial Brand Strength score, but the brand was destroyed in 2020 and is being rebuilt rather than charging a premium, which is why the intangible-benefits check scores negative and the bank runs the lowest margin in its peer group. Nothing in four years of calls explains why a well-funded newcomer could not copy the franchise — the stated strengths are technology, deposit granularity and sponsor backing, all of which can be bought — so Cost to Replicate is a no fit too. Capital allocation adds to the drag: four years of rising profits with no dividend or buyback, a dividend question deflected in November 2024, dilution from SMBC and Advent, and a fresh enabling resolution to raise more capital in July 2026.

The risk review points back at the same traits from the other side. The one high risk is government dependency, and it is not incidental — the bank exists in this period because of an RBI-Government reconstruction scheme, with an alternate board and SBI as anchor shareholder, and it carries mandated RIDF and priority-sector deposits that were 11% of assets and are still around 6%. Those mandated deposits are precisely what the asset-turn check blames for the thin margin, so the government's role is simultaneously the reason the bank survived, the reason its earning power is weak, and the identified lever for improvement as the balance runs below 5% by FY27. Five further risks sit on watch. Deposit competition is flagged as persistent and elevated, which is the New Entrants risk and the ceiling on the market-share pattern at once; undifferentiated products with no pricing power is the Good Enough Goods risk and the reason two patterns fail; and although the deposit book is granular, individual relationships still move the numbers — one infrastructure group behind a Rs.1,700 crore jump in overdues, a single ARC counterparty handling a Rs.48,000 crore stressed pool. Credit and rate cycles still show through in unsecured retail and microfinance stress, and the new MD flags artificial intelligence as a macro factor he is watching. Only shifting customer preferences scores low risk, because the move to digital is one the bank is on the right side of. Resilience is recorded as unknown. Put together: a small, improving, well-run bank riding a real recovery and a growing Indian credit market, with no durable pricing advantage, weak returns on capital, capital still flowing in rather than out to shareholders, and an unusual amount of public attention — one of India's most-watched stocks, which the framework marks as a negative even while praising management's quietness.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** YES Bank's very existence in this period is a government/RBI construct — the FY23 call opens with exit from the RBI-Government reconstruction scheme, an 'alternate board' with SBI- and RBI-nominated directors, and SBI as anchor shareholder; separately, regulator-mandated PSL shortfall deposits (RIDF) were ~11% of total assets and dragged NIM by ~70bp, with loan pricing itself repo/EBLR-linked.

> "there is one big drag that comes from the RIDF assets that we are holding in view of the PSL shortfall of the past. That's almost 11% of our Total Assets... pulling down the margins by almost 70 basis points" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Partial and gradual: RIDF/mandated deposits reduced from ~11% to ~6% of assets by FY26 with a path below 5% by FY27, and two straight years of 100% PSL compliance removing fresh shortfall*

### Cyclicality — WATCH
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.
