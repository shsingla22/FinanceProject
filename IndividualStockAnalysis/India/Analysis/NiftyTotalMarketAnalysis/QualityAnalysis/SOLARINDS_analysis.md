# Solar Industries India Ltd. (SOLARINDS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: CHEMICALS*

Solar Industries India Limited makes and sells industrial explosives and the accessories used to set them off (detonators and other 'initiating systems'), plus a fast-growing range of defence products such as ammunition, energetic materials, rocket/artillery systems like Pinaka, drone-based loitering munitions, and propulsion motors for space launches. Its customers are coal and mineral miners — chiefly state-owned Coal India (CIL) and Singareni Collieries — other non-CIL institutional and mining customers, housing and infrastructure contractors, overseas customers in many countries, and India's armed forces and defence agencies. It earns money by manufacturing these products in India and abroad and selling them on multi-year supply contracts and order books (roughly Rs. 21,300 crores of orders at March 2026), with explosive prices contractually linked to raw material costs like ammonium nitrate and fuel oil so cost swings are largely passed through. Consolidated revenue was about Rs. 9,838 crores in FY26 with EBITDA margins near 28%, and the company operates across India — including newer plants at Dhule (Maharashtra) and Dholpur (Rajasthan), with more planned in the east and south — alongside a large international/export business.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Explosives (domestic)** — Bulk and packaged industrial explosives sold to miners and infrastructure users in India; historically the core of the business (domestic volumes of roughly 1.2 lakh tonnes a quarter in FY23), with pricing tied to ammonium nitrate and fuel oil costs, though management says domestic mining markets showed no growth in FY26.
- **Initiating Systems** — Detonators and related accessories that trigger the explosives — a smaller add-on line (about Rs. 133 crores in one FY23 quarter) that grows only in low single digits recently.
- **Coal India (CIL)** — Supplies to the state-owned coal miner under roughly two-year tender orders; once a major slice, it has shrunk to about 9% of the sales basket in FY26 from 13%.
- **Non-CIL and institutional** — Explosives sold to other institutional and mining customers such as Singareni Collieries (an Rs. 887 crore two-year order won in FY25); about 12% of FY26 sales.
- **Housing and Infrastructure (H&I)** — Explosives used in construction, roads and other infrastructure work, supported by government infrastructure spending; about 12-15% of the sales basket.
- **Exports / International (overseas)** — Explosives and technical services sold through overseas operations and exports across many geographies — the largest single block for most of the period at roughly 33-46% of sales, crossing Rs. 1,000 crores in a single quarter in Q3 FY26 and growing about 32% in FY26.
- **Defence** — Ammunition, energetic materials, Pinaka rocket variants, loitering munitions/drones and space propulsion motors (including motors static-tested for ISRO); the fastest-growing part, up from about Rs. 400 crores in FY23 to Rs. 2,634 crores in FY26 (33% of Q4 sales), with an Rs. 18,000 crore order book and guidance to exceed Rs. 4,500 crores in FY27.

### The story in depth

Solar Industries is a maker of commercial explosives and defence products whose combined score is 78 out of 100, or Strong, built from 72 on business quality, 100 on multibagger pattern fit and 64 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged sound. The quality work marks its strongest ground as competitive advantage, and the reasons are concrete: in-house research keeps turning into approved products with revenue behind them (the SEBEX explosives family approved by the Navy, India's first chaff and flare facility, 23 mm, 30 mm and 155 mm ammunition, Bhargavastra and loitering munitions); a decade of building plants in South Africa, Zambia, Tanzania, Zimbabwe, Nigeria, Ghana, Sierra Leone, Turkey, Kazakhstan, Thailand, Indonesia and Australia has moved international sales up sharply; and out of every Rs.100 of sales, Rs.28 is operating profit, with margins moving only about three points a year either way. Management is spending into that, lifting capital spending from roughly Rs.450-500 crore a year in FY23 to Rs.1,200 crore in FY25 and a planned Rs.2,050 crore in FY27, all on new plants, new countries and defence capacity, all built from scratch rather than bought, and carried on modest debt of Rs.134 crore of interest against Rs.2,750 crore of operating profit.

The pattern screen reads the same facts from the other side and lands on three strong fits. Innovation Dominance is the research pipeline above. Cost to Replicate is the licensing and multi-year customer qualification that new suppliers must clear, the same patience the quality work credits as tenacity: the export build-out took roughly 2018 to 2022 of loss-making greenfield plants before revenue moved, and defence products need years of service approval. Corporate Culture is the promoter-led, unusually stable team and the willingness to keep reinvesting on a long horizon. Four more patterns fit only partly, and the gaps matter. Explosives are a small but essential consumable for miners in a two- or three-player field, which is the Toll Roads idea, but it is not a contracted toll. Pricing follows ammonium nitrate up and down through rise-and-fall clauses in supply contracts, so Pricing Power tracks input costs rather than beating them; the 18-20% growth guided for FY27 is largely price-led on that basis. The brand is a business-to-business reputation, backed by 25 years of operation and public recognition, but there is no consumer following. Four patterns do not apply at all: there is no subscription or renewal revenue, no dealer or installer channel between the company and its buyers, no low-cost positioning (margin guidance was raised from 20-22% in FY23 to a delivered 27.95% in FY26 on higher-value products), and no company-owned retail.

The risk review largely re-examines the same traits and finds their other edge. The structural growth the quality work praises is government-set: Coal India and Singareni contracts are re-tendered every two years, and defence, which reached 33% of fourth-quarter FY26 revenue, accounts for Rs.18,000 crore of the roughly Rs.21,000 crore order book, which is why Government Dependency is the one channel rated high risk, softened only by Coal India falling from 14% to 9% of the mix and exports running near 39% of sales. The rise-and-fall pricing that limits the Pricing Power pattern is the same mechanism behind the Cyclicality watch, though it passes costs through in both directions with a one-to-three-month lag. The commoditised end of the range, bulk explosives sold on tender against the world's number one and two players, sits behind the Good Enough Goods watch, and the deliberate shift toward high-value defence and specialised products is the answer to it. Stakeholder concentration and the arrival of a possible new entrant, the Kalyani Group, are watch-level, with margins having widened rather than narrowed through that period. Technology obsolescence and fashion risk are low, since demand is functional, repeat and contracted. The weaker quality scores fill in the rest of the picture: capital allocation, return on capital and industry structure all score low, with about 152 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and the company is heavily followed rather than overlooked, with calls hosted by large brokers and large funds asking questions.

## What to watch
