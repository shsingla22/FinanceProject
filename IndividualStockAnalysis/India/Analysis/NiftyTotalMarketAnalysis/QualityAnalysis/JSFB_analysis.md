# Jana Small Fin. Bank (JSFB) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Jana Small Finance Bank is an Indian bank, headquartered in Bengaluru, that lends small amounts to people and tiny businesses that mainstream banks tend to skip — what management calls "serving the underserved." It grew out of a microfinance lender, became a bank in 2018, and has spent the years since shifting from unsecured group loans to loans backed by collateral: home loans, small-business loans against property, gold, two-wheelers and used cars. It funds this mainly with customer deposits gathered through its branch network (Rs. 35,784 crores of deposits and 822 branches by March 2026, spread across ~25 states with deliberately low concentration in any one state), and earns its money from the gap between the high rates it charges borrowers and what it pays depositors — a net interest margin around 7.5%, plus fees. As of FY26 about 72.6% of its loan book was secured and the rest unsecured microfinance, most of which is now covered by government credit-guarantee schemes.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Microfinance / Retail Financial Services (RFS) — unsecured advances** — Small unsecured loans to low-income, largely rural borrowers, the bank's original business and still its single biggest unsecured exposure — it fell from ~40% of loans in FY24 to ~30% in FY25 (around Rs. 8,900 crores) and to roughly 27% by FY26 after a sector-wide stress period, with 77% of it now sitting under the CGFMU/CGTMSE credit-guarantee programs.
- **Affordable housing and micro housing loans** — Home loans for lower-income buyers, described in FY27 Q1 as one of the businesses "growing superbly well" and a key reason the bank relocates branches to better locations.
- **MSME loans** — Lending to small and medium businesses; management lists it among the strongly growing secured lines in FY27.
- **Micro LAP (loan against property)** — Small loans secured against a borrower's property; the one line that did not grow in Q1 FY27, deliberately, as the bank shifts it from third-party to direct sourcing.
- **Gold loans** — Loans against gold — an incubated product management said in FY25 had "grown up" from an investment into a profitable, fast-growing business.
- **Two-wheeler and vehicle loans** — Financing for two-wheelers, described alongside gold as having matured into a strong self-standing business, with vehicle loans named again as a growth driver in FY27.
- **Used car loans** — A newer secured product the bank is deliberately spending money to build up, named in FY25 as the next line (with supply chain) it expects to turn into a meaningful business in a year or two.
- **Supply chain financing** — Another newer, still-being-invested-in secured line, grouped with used cars as a future growth engine rather than a current earner.
- **Term loans to NBFCs** — Wholesale lending to non-bank finance companies, which the bank is intentionally keeping flat because its own retail products can deliver 20–22% growth on their own.
- **Branch banking and liabilities (deposits/CASA)** — The deposit-gathering side — current and savings accounts plus term deposits — which funds the lending; deposits grew 29% in FY25 and 23% in FY26 to Rs. 35,784 crores, with CASA up 31% year-on-year in Q1 FY27 and cost of funds falling to 7.46%.
- **Treasury and capital markets** — Manages the bank's liquidity, borrowings and refinance lines; referenced through metrics like the CD ratio (93.3% in FY25, 82.9% including refinance) and a liquidity coverage ratio being deliberately brought down from 391% to 253%.
- **Business Correspondent (BC) partnerships** — Local partner firms that originate and collect part of the microfinance book under service guarantees — about 10% of the total loan book in FY24, which management says reduces the bank's own unsecured risk.
- **New / upcoming products** — Credit Line on UPI (in testing, due to go public in Q1 FY27), loans against shares and mutual funds, and foreign-exchange services under a new AD1 license — all small and not yet material.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Jana Small Finance Bank comes across, first and foremost, as a well-run bank rather than an unusual business. The strongest marks in the quality work all sit with the people running it: management gives specific numeric targets and then reports against them, openly owns the misses (the cost-to-income ratio, product penetration) and dealt with the promoter holding company's rating downgrade directly instead of glossing over it. That same behaviour is the one place the multibagger screen records a full fit — Corporate Culture — because the guidance given in Q3 FY26 was met item by item in Q4 on profit, credit cost and stressed-asset numbers, and the 2024 promise to keep growing the secured book at roughly 40% a year was actually delivered straight through the microfinance stress. The eight-year push to move the loan book from unsecured group lending to property-backed lending (about 59% secured in 2024 to 72.6% by FY26) is the single decision that ties most of the record together: it earns top marks for tenacity and for disciplined stewardship (no acquisitions, deliberate spreading of lending across 25 states, Rs.305 crore of provisions taken early, capital adequacy held above 20% through the stress), and it is also the main defence cited against two of the three biggest risks. It is likewise the bank's answer to the erosion of its original model, where customers simply stopped turning up to group meetings and wanted individual products — a demand-side shift the risk review flags as one to watch, and which the bank has met by moving customers into a Rs.5,000 crore individual loan book with credit bureau checks.

The growth on display is real but it is worth being clear about where it comes from, because the same fact that produces the best scores also produces the biggest risk. Jana is growing loans and deposits at multiples of the banking system (advances 19% against industry 11% in FY25, 23% in FY26, deposits 29% against 11%) and is having its best unsecured quarter in eight years without adding branches or salespeople. The quality work rewards this twice, once for gaining market share and once for picking up business handed over by weaker microfinance lenders that withdrew after the stress cycle, and the screen registers it as a partial fit on Market Share Gainers, noting that credit standards were tightened rather than loosened while the share was being taken. But management itself describes this as an 'advantage period till other players really come back'. That is precisely why the risk review rates new entrants and competition as low today while rating cyclicality as high: the tailwind is the recovery leg of a credit cycle whose downswing shrank the microfinance book by 11%, forced Rs.305 crore of accelerated provisions (profit of Rs.501 crore against an adjusted Rs.779 crore), pushed cost-to-income from 57.4% to 61.3% and cut return on equity from over 20% to around 13%. The absence of price competition today, which is what keeps the Pricing Power reading from being worse, rests on the same temporary emptiness in the market — and the screen is explicit that recent margin improvement came from cheaper deposits rather than from the bank charging more.

What holds the overall verdict to 51 out of 100, or 'Decent', is the economics underneath the growth and the thinness of the moat. This is a balance-sheet lender, so it is structurally asset-heavy, and with return on assets of only 1.3-1.4% the return-on-capital area scores worst of all. It consumes capital rather than returning it: there is no dividend or buyback policy discussed, and the FY25 statement that no further capital was needed was followed by a Rs.728 crore raise in FY27 — a note that sits awkwardly beside the otherwise strong candour score. The screen finds no fit at all on the low-cost model, since the bank funds itself at 7.4-8.0% and runs a cost-to-income ratio that worsened to 61.3% against its own 54% ambition, and no fit on being a useful middleman, because it is deliberately cutting intermediaries out (business correspondents down to 5% of the microfinance book, micro-LAP growth given up in one quarter to move to direct sourcing). Almost everything else — the anchor-bank relationship of roughly 3.9-4.0 products per customer, the 16-year, 12-million-customer franchise, 822 branches, the proprietary secured-lending scorecard, the steady cadence of new products such as gold, two-wheeler, used car, Credit Line on UPI — registers as partial rather than decisive, and the screen says plainly that none of it is beyond a well-funded competitor holding a small finance bank licence. That connects directly to the second watch-level risk: affordable housing, micro-LAP and MSME loans are largely interchangeable products, and management's own framing of the anchor-bank pitch as a defence against 'balance transfers out' concedes that cheaper lenders can refinance these customers away. Layered on top is heavy policy exposure, rated high risk: the regulator sets the bad-loan thresholds that gate a universal bank licence and must approve both the TVS stake and the AD1 licence, and roughly 80% of the downside on the remaining unsecured book now rests on government guarantee schemes. Long-run durability is recorded as unknown.

## What to watch

- Cyclicality (high risk): whether stressed-asset and slippage measures keep improving from their eight-quarter lows, or the microfinance book slips back toward the 11% de-growth that triggered the Rs.305 crore of accelerated provisions.
