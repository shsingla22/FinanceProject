# Jindal Saw Ltd. (JINDALSAW) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: METALS*

Jindal Saw Limited makes large steel and iron pipes and tubes, and sells them mostly to government water-supply projects and to oil and gas companies. Its main products are ductile iron pipes for drinking-water and sewage networks (including India's Jal Jeevan Mission and state water schemes), welded SAW pipes (LSAW/HSAW) for oil, gas and water pipelines, and seamless pipes and tubes — including higher-grade API-certified, stainless and alloy pipe — for oil and gas drilling and production. It makes money by winning tenders and contracts, holding a large order book (about USD 1 billion in 2022, USD 1.6 billion by late 2024, plus a 6 lakh tonne Saudi work order in 2026) and converting it into shipments; profitability swings with raw-material costs (steel, coal, iron ore, pig iron), the rupee, and the mix between exports and domestic sales, since exports carry higher margins. It operates plants in India (Nashik is named for seamless pipe) and, through subsidiaries and joint ventures, in Abu Dhabi and Saudi Arabia, selling domestically in India and exporting to the Middle East, the US, Europe and elsewhere — with standalone revenue of about INR 14,745 crore and consolidated about INR 17,987 crore in FY26.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Ductile iron (DI) pipes / water segment** — Iron pipes for municipal and rural water supply and sewage, sold largely to government-funded water infrastructure programmes such as the Jal Jeevan Mission and state-level projects (some multilateral-agency funded); management describes it as the main growth driver in India in 2022 and the biggest single drag in FY26 when project execution and fund releases slowed, so it is clearly one of the largest parts of the business.
- **SAW pipes (LSAW / HSAW), large diameter** — Large-diameter welded line pipe for oil and gas transmission and bulk water, sold on project contracts in India and export markets; management repeatedly points to 'large dia' wins in both water and oil and gas as a key order-book contributor, and is building dedicated 300,000 tonne-per-annum LSAW and HSAW mills in Saudi Arabia.
- **Seamless pipes and tubes (carbon, stainless, alloy; OCTG)** — Higher-grade drilling and well pipe for oil and gas customers such as ONGC, including deep-sea and deepwater work; described as a newer, higher-margin basket (API-certified business earns better margins than non-API), and its importance shows in the fact that the suspension of the API monogram at the Nashik plant from January to mid-June 2026 was singled out as a material hit to quarterly results.
- **Abu Dhabi operations (subsidiary)** — An overseas manufacturing base described as a ductile iron pipe facility to which a 3 lakh tonne, roughly USD 300 million seamless pipe plant is being added (commercial operation expected FY29), serving MENA energy and water customers; its results flow through the consolidated numbers and its profits swung with pig iron prices in 2022, so it is the most significant overseas operating unit.
- **Saudi Arabia joint venture** — A 51:49 JV with Buhur of Saudi Arabia to build LSAW and HSAW pipe mills of 300,000 tonnes each for regional water and energy demand; still under construction with land secured and equipment LCs opened, so it contributes nothing to current earnings but is presented as a main future growth plank.
- **Technology partnerships / joint ventures for oil and gas products** — A joint venture with Hunting and an exclusive technology transfer with OSI USA to make specialised oil-country tubular goods in India, mentioned in 2022 as a way to bid for ONGC tenders; described as an add-on to the pipe business rather than a large revenue line at the time.
- **Iron ore pellets** — A raw-material-linked product whose prices management tracks and comments on ('a little softening on the pellet prices'), noted as a smaller swing factor that gets more than offset by the pipe business.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

The most reliable thing about Jindal Saw is the people running it. Management scores the highest of any area in the quality work, and the reason is unusually plain: they opened the FY26 call by pointing out that they had guided for a better fourth quarter than third, and the opposite happened. They put a number on every piece of bad news — the API monogram suspension, the currency hit, the force majeure in the Middle East — rather than burying it. The calls are run by functional executives with numbers-first commentary and no promoter showmanship, detail is deferred to written follow-up instead of talked around, and the balance sheet has been steadily de-risked: standalone net debt down to about INR2,453cr from INR3,154cr, long-term debt down to INR529cr against a net worth of INR6,000-7,000cr, with capex deliberately staggered so it does not all land at once. That same honesty is what earns the 'Corporate Culture' pattern a partial pass — and it is also what forces the rest of this report to be sober, because the candid disclosure is precisely what tells you FY26 EBITDA fell 47% and profit fell 58%.

The growth story and the risk story are largely the same story told twice. Between FY23 and FY25 earnings stepped up because of mix, not volume — EBITDA margin broke out of its old 12-14% range to 18-19% on higher grades, exports and value-added products while volumes stayed flat. Behind that sit real moves up the value curve: the Hunting joint venture in premium connections, an exclusive technology transfer from OSI USA, and entry into seamless, stainless and alloy pipe for defence, nuclear and deep-sea work. Capacity is being added cheaply through debottlenecking (Nashik up 40-50% for roughly INR200cr, Haresamudram to 3 lakh tons) and now genuinely expanded abroad through a US$300mn Abu Dhabi seamless plant and a 51% Saudi joint venture. The end markets are structurally expanding too — state water infrastructure, ONGC's roughly $20bn deepwater programme, the March 2026 piped-gas mandate. But every one of those strengths carries its own shadow. The end-market growth that looks structural is hostage to government decisions, and about 60-65% of the order book is government, which is why the water business is described as plateauing and then hit by delayed release of central funds and unpaid state dues. The Middle East corridor that diversifies away from India is itself 29-30% of the order book, with one 6 lakh tonne Saudi job underpinning much of the export book. The premium-connections business — the one place management can honestly say they are the only player in India in that format — runs entirely through a single partner whose own revenue fell. And the API certification that let the company into higher-value work became the thing that shut the seamless business down when it was suspended: the company is the one being certified, not the one owning the standard.

That is why the combined rating lands at 39 out of 100, or Mixed — 45% weight on business quality at 57, 30% on multibagger fit at just 10, and 25% on risk safety at 42. Eight of the eleven growth patterns are outright no-fit, and the three that pass do so only partially. There is no pricing power: the company says raw-material volatility sits with the manufacturer, and it only persuaded government customers into price-variation clauses that cover about 80-85% of pig-iron moves on roughly 60% of the book. There is no meaningful barrier to someone else doing this — management's own project timelines are the disproof, with a greenfield welded-pipe mill taking 18-24 months and a seamless plant about $300mn and 18-20 months, both within reach of any funded entrant. Revenue is made-to-order execution against a book, with nothing recurring underneath it. On the numbers, roughly 159 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, capital allocation scores slightly negative and return on capital scores flat. Crucially, management themselves attribute the 2023-25 margin surge substantially to a favourable demand-supply environment rather than to their own skill, and attribute the FY26 collapse to outside shocks — so the honest reading is a well-run, conservatively financed, cyclical manufacturer in a commoditised industry, whose resilience is sound but whose earnings are set by steel and coal prices, government fund release, and order timing rather than by anything it controls.
