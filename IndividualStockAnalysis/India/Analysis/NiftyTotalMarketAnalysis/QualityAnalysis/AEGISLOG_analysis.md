# Aegis Logistics (AEGISLOG) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aegis Logistics is an Indian company that owns and operates port-side storage terminals for bulk liquids (chemicals, petroleum products) and gases (mainly LPG, and now ammonia), and also buys and resells LPG in India. It makes money three ways: charging fees to store and move other people's cargo through its tanks and jetties (terminalling/throughput), importing and reselling LPG in bulk and in cylinders to industrial, commercial, household and auto-fuel customers, and trading/sourcing LPG cargoes. Its customers include state oil companies (IOC, HPCL, BPCL), large private conglomerates, industrial users, hotels/restaurants/small industry, and households. It operates only in India, from terminals at Mumbai, JNPT, Kandla, Pipavav, Kochi, Mangalore and Haldia, with FY26 revenue of INR8,333 crores, normalized EBITDA of INR1,599 crores and profit after tax of INR1,107 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Liquid Terminal Division (Liquids)** — Rents out tank storage at ports for chemicals and petroleum products — including specialized heated storage up to 230°C — earning steady fee income; it is the smaller but far higher-margin half, with FY26 revenue of INR644 crores and EBITDA of INR472 crores (roughly 73% margins), on close to 2 million kilolitres of capacity being built out across the coastline.
- **Gas / LPG Division — Logistics (terminal throughput)** — Imports and handles LPG through cryogenic and static tanks, jetties, rail gantries, truck bays and pipeline links for oil marketing companies and other customers, charging a throughput fee; volumes hit a record 5.15 million tons in FY26, up 14%.
- **Gas / LPG Division — Distribution** — Buys LPG and sells it on its own account to bulk industrial users, to hotels/restaurants/small industry under the 'Aegis Pure Gas' brand, to households under the 'Aegis Chhota Sikander' brand, and as auto gas at fuel stations — the fastest-growing piece, with volumes up 45% to 7.54 lakh tons in FY26 (Q4 alone up 71%).
- **Gas / LPG Division — Sourcing** — Buys and resells LPG cargoes, which drives most of the group's headline revenue but carries thin margins; FY26 sourcing sales were 6.07 lakh metric tons, up 2%, and the LPG segment as a whole booked INR7,689 crores of revenue and INR1,131 crores of EBITDA.
- **Aegis Vopak Terminals Limited (AVTL) — joint venture / listed subsidiary** — A tie-up with global tank-storage operator Vopak that holds much of the liquid and LPG terminal infrastructure, was IPO'd (proceeds used to pay down debt), acquired 75% of Hindustan Aegis LPG at Haldia to enter the East Coast, and holds the 15-year take-or-pay petroleum contract at Pipavav for over 0.5 million tons a year.
- **Ammonia terminals (new energy)** — A newly launched line building India's first independent ammonia terminal at Pipavav (36,000 tons, backed by a 15-year take-or-pay deal with Hindustan Zinc), plus an MoU with L&T for a green ammonia terminal at Kandla — small today but positioned as the next growth leg, with management aiming to be vertically integrated in ammonia as it is in LPG.
- **Expansion pipeline / capex program** — Not a segment but central to the story: roughly USD1.2 billion of cumulative capex by FY27 and a USD5 billion roadmap to 2030, including a non-binding MoU for about INR20,000 crores at the proposed Vadhavan port, funded from about INR6,000 crores of cash and prudent debt.

### The story in depth

Aegis Logistics earns its keep by owning physical bottlenecks. Its terminals sit on allotted port land at Mumbai, Kandla, Pipavav, Haldia, Mangalore, Kochi and JNPA, with VLGC-compliant jetties, cryogenic tanks, rail gantries and pipeline hook-ups built over decades — assets the review judges a newcomer could not copy, which is why the Cost to Replicate pattern is a strong fit and why the framework scores mini-monopolies and switching costs at the top of its scale. Customers behave accordingly: they buy certainty rather than the cheapest price, signing 15-year take-or-pay contracts with Hindustan Zinc and a large conglomerate and an exclusive HPCL agreement running to 2038. The economics that follow are unusually comfortable — around INR1,100 a ton charged on cargo worth many multiples of that (the Toll Roads pattern), and cash collected from customers twelve days before suppliers must be paid, so the customer base effectively funds the business. Volumes have grown faster than the market throughout, with LPG logistics up from 3.33mt in FY23 to 5.15mt in FY26 and distribution up 45% in FY26 against industry growth of roughly 8-10%, and the long-tenured Chandaria/Moledina team has delivered what it promised, turning guidance of a 25% EPS growth rate into 32% over five years and commissioning the Pipavav and Mangalore terminals on the quarter they had named.

The uncomfortable part is that the very traits producing those patterns also produce the biggest risks. Because the moat is location and permits, every asset sits on government-controlled port land and the next leg of volume growth is gated on state pipeline projects — which is exactly why Government Dependency is rated the single high risk here: the thing that keeps competitors out also puts the landlord and the permit-issuer inside the business. The toll-road economics rest on a handful of PSU oil companies being the ones paying the toll, so the same contract structure that makes revenue predictable makes it concentrated, with Haldia shown to be structurally short of volume simply because 'BPCL is still not there' — Stakeholder Concentration is rated elevated on both ends of the chain. The Forward Integrators move into distribution, the clearest strong-fit pattern and the main profit driver in FY26, is also where cyclicality enters: margins per ton roughly rose from INR4,000 to INR7,000 while volumes grew 45%, but management attributes that to energy prices and procurement rather than brand or pricing strength, which is why Pricing Power is only a partial fit and Cyclicality is rated elevated. And the bulk industrial franchise wins business because propane currently costs less than natural gas — the Low Price Plus pattern — which is precisely the exposure flagged under Technological Innovation, a position defended on price rather than on anything a customer cannot walk away from. The distribution build-out is a genuine offset to government dependency, since it sells to private industrial, commercial and auto customers rather than the state, but it is an offset that trades one sensitivity for another.

Set against that, the scorecard is more sober than the narrative suggests. Growth persistence is scored at the bottom of the scale — sales have averaged about 7% a year and arrive lumpily — and the aggregate area scores for competitive advantage and return on capital are both low, so the strong pattern fits are not yet showing through as smooth compounding or high returns on the capital employed. There is no innovation-led advantage at all, brand is only partial (the Aegis Pure Gas and Chhota Sikander names exist but carry little weight across fourteen calls), and the Friendly Middleman read is mixed. Overall resilience is judged strained, and the combined rating lands at 64 out of 100 — decent rather than exceptional — from 70 on business quality, 71 on multibagger fit and only 44 on risk safety. The swing factor is the capex programme: almost all spending is expansionary, scaling from an INR4,500 crore plan in FY23 to roughly INR10,000 crore by FY27 with a $5bn pipeline to 2030 at a guided 25% EBITDA return, funded partly from INR5,939 crores of cash that also lets the company build through a downturn. The optimistic case is that this widens the same permit-gated bottleneck the company already owns, extends it into ammonia — India's first independent ammonia terminal, with a 2 million ton gas distribution target by FY28 — and hedges the decarbonisation question the risk review flags. The realistic case is that the company is spending heavily on land it does not own, to serve customers it cannot easily replace, in a business whose current margin uplift comes from a fuel price gap nobody controls.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Cyclicality, Stakeholder Concentration. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.7× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 93% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ⚠️ Returns on capital slid from about 22% to 14% — the business is earning less and less on the money invested in it.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Every asset sits on government-controlled port land (Kandla/Deendayal, JNPA, Haldia, Mangalore, Pipavav, and the proposed Vadhavan) and depends on port authorities, land allotments and permits, while volume step-ups are explicitly gated on state pipeline projects (KGPL, Jamnagar-Loni) whose timelines have repeatedly slipped — from 'end of FY25' in the May 2024 call to 'June 2026' and then 'H1 FY27', with analysts citing land-compensation problems. Core throughput customers are the PSU oil marketing companies, port jetty upgrades directly cut volumes (Haldia -10% in Q1 FY23), and a ₹20,000 crore Vadhavan MoU awaits 'land development and formal approvals'.

> "We have entered into a nonbinding memorandum of understanding for potential investment of around INR20,000 crores in the proposed Vadhavan port. We await the land development and formal approvals." — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Growing distribution business sells to private industrial/commercial/auto customers rather than the state, diluting the PSU-throughput share of profit*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Terminalling and distribution are operations-linked flow products with 8–10% structural LPG import growth and take-or-pay/1-year storage contracts, but distribution margin — the main FY26 profit driver — is openly a function of energy prices, jumping from ~₹4,000 to ~₹7,000/ton 'as the energy prices have risen ... because of the uncertainty involved', which is a cycle-peak margin management nonetheless calls sustainable, alongside a $1.2bn-to-$5bn capex build-out into that peak.

> "the margin that we have earned during the year has been around INR7,000 against INR4,000-odd in the previous year ... we feel the INR7,000-odd margins should be sustainable" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow-product demand tied to customers' operations, not their capex; take-or-pay and long-tenor contracts; ₹5,939 crores of cash funding countercyclical expansion at 0.6x gearing*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 4 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 4% and 17% (±4.5 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 6% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 14%).

### Stakeholder Concentration — ELEVATED
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**What the calls show:** Both ends of the chain are concentrated: throughput depends on a handful of PSU oil companies (the 2022 call shows Haldia volumes structurally impaired because 'BPCL is still not there'), Haldia is 'anchored by an exclusive terminaling agreement with Hindustan Petroleum through 2038', Pipavav liquids rest on one 15-year take-or-pay with a single conglomerate (>0.5 mt/yr) and the ammonia terminal on one take-or-pay with Hindustan Zinc; on supply, the West Asia disruption cut national LPG availability 50% in April 2026 and 30% in May.

## What to watch

- Distribution margin per ton holding near the FY26 level of about INR7,000 — the Cyclicality risk says this is a function of energy prices, not pricing power, and it is the main profit driver.
- The propane-versus-natural-gas price gap staying favourable; the Technological Innovation risk and the Low Price Plus pattern both rest on it, and management defends the position on price alone.
- State pipeline and port decisions — new land allotments, permits and the proposed Vadhavan terminal — which the Government Dependency high risk identifies as the gate on the next step-up in volumes.
- Whether the PSU customer base broadens; the Stakeholder Concentration risk cites Haldia running short because one oil major has not arrived, and the same handful of buyers pay the toll.
- Whether the INR10,000 crore capex programme converts into the guided ~25% EBITDA return and smooths the lumpy 7% average sales growth flagged by the persistence-of-growth check, given the low aggregate scores on return on capital and competitive advantage.
