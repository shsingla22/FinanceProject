# Aegis Logistics (AEGISLOG) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aegis Logistics is an Indian company that owns and runs storage terminals and related handling infrastructure at seaports for bulk liquids (petroleum products, chemicals) and gases (mainly LPG), describing its vision as being the leading provider of logistics and supply chain services to India's oil, gas and chemical industry. It makes money two ways: renting out tank capacity and charging fees to move cargo through its jetties, pipelines and rail/truck loading facilities for customers such as state oil companies (IOC, HPCL, BPCL) and large private conglomerates, and separately buying LPG and reselling it to industrial users, hotels/restaurants, households and auto-gas outlets under its own brands. Its facilities sit along both Indian coasts — Mumbai, JNPT, Kandla, Pipavav, Mangalore, Kochi and Haldia — with a large ongoing construction programme and a partnership with global tank-storage firm Vopak through subsidiary Aegis Vopak Terminals Limited. In FY26 the group reported revenue of INR8,333 crores, normalized EBITDA of INR1,599 crores and profit after tax of INR1,107 crores, with cash and investments of about INR5,939 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Liquid Terminal Division (Liquid business)** — Rents out tank storage for petroleum products and chemicals at port terminals and charges for handling cargo in and out; small in revenue (INR644 crores in FY26) but very profitable, contributing INR472 crores of EBITDA — roughly a third of group EBITDA on under a tenth of revenue — with total capacity across India approaching 2 million kilolitres and long-term take-or-pay contracts such as a 15-year deal at Pipavav for over 0.5 million tonnes a year.
- **Gas Division / LPG business** — The much larger segment by revenue (INR7,689 crores and INR1,131 crores EBITDA in FY26, up 26% and 68%), covering everything the company does with LPG and, increasingly, ammonia; management splits it into logistics (terminal throughput), distribution, and sourcing.
- **LPG logistics / terminalling (throughput)** — Imports and unloads LPG at its port terminals for oil marketing companies and other customers and moves it out by pipeline, rail gantry and truck, earning a handling fee per tonne; throughput reached 5.15 million tonnes in FY26, up 14%, and is expected to rise further as the Jamnagar–Loni and Kandla–Gorakhpur pipelines connect and more berths become able to take very large gas carriers (VLGCs).
- **LPG distribution** — Buys LPG and sells it directly to end users — bulk industrial customers (e.g. the ceramics cluster at Morbi), commercial and domestic cylinders under the 'Aegis Pure Gas' and 'Aegis Chhota Sikander' brands, and auto-gas — supported by its own bottling plants and a growing distributor network; management calls this the fastest-growing part, with volumes up 45% to 7.54 lakh tonnes in FY26.
- **LPG sourcing** — Procures and on-sells LPG cargoes in bulk, which adds a lot of revenue but relatively thin margin; sales were 6.07 lakh tonnes in FY26, up only 2%, making it the slowest-growing of the gas activities.
- **Aegis Vopak Terminals Limited (AVTL)** — The joint-venture subsidiary formed with global storage partner Vopak that holds much of the terminal infrastructure and has been the vehicle for acquisitions and expansion (Mangalore, Kochi, and a 75% stake in Hindustan Aegis LPG at Haldia); it was listed via an IPO whose proceeds the group used to pay down debt.
- **Ammonia terminals / new energy** — A newer line applying the same storage-and-distribution model to ammonia, including what management calls India's first independent ammonia terminal at Pipavav (36,000 tonnes, backed by a 15-year take-or-pay contract with Hindustan Zinc) plus a non-binding MoU with Larsen & Toubro for a green ammonia terminal at Kandla; still small today but positioned as a future growth leg.
- **Capex and expansion programme** — A continuous build-out of new tanks, cryogenic LPG storage, bottling plants and jetties across its ports, with cumulative capital spending expected to reach about USD1.2 billion by next year, a stated roadmap of USD5 billion by 2030, and a non-binding MoU for roughly INR20,000 crores of potential investment at the proposed Vadhavan port.

### The story in depth

Aegis earns its keep by owning physical bottlenecks. Its terminals sit on allotted land at India's major ports, hard-wired to jetties, pipelines and rail gantries, and cost between INR525 and INR1,675 crores a site to build — which is why the quality framework scores it full marks on partial monopoly and switching costs, on mini-monopolies as the default independent counterparty for the oil majors, and on assurance and convenience benefits. The same assets explain three of the strongest pattern reads: Cost to Replicate (a well-funded copycat cannot rebuild permitted port land quickly), Toll Roads (a fee of roughly INR1,100 a ton on cargo the refiners cannot land any other way, with a 77% liquid-segment margin behind it), and Corporate Culture (the Pipavav and Mangalore cryogenic terminals and the ammonia terminal all arrived on the schedule management promised, and a 25% EPS growth guidance was beaten at 32% over five years). Customers pay before suppliers need to be paid — cash comes back 12 days early — so the business funds its own working capital while it grows.

Those same traits create the biggest risks, and this is the central tension in the file. Because every asset is fixed infrastructure on government port land — Kandla, JNPA, Haldia, Mangalore, Pipavav, plus a proposed INR20,000 crore Vadhavan project still waiting on land approvals — the thing that makes the position hard to copy also makes it impossible to move, and Government Dependency is flagged as the one HIGH risk. Because the tolls come from long contracts with a short list of counterparties, the recurring revenue that supports the Toll Roads read is also Stakeholder Concentration at ELEVATED: Haldia volumes stayed weak because 'BPCL is still not there', and Kandla went quarters after commissioning without a throughput agreement. And the forward integration into own-account distribution — bottling plants, distributors, autogas, volumes up 45% in FY26 — which drives both the Forward Integrators and Market Share Gainers patterns, is where the Cyclicality risk lives: margin per ton nearly doubled from about INR4,000 to INR7,000 explicitly because energy prices rose, and management calling that sustainable is described in the record as the classic cycle-peak claim.

The numbers underneath are more mixed than the narrative. Competitive advantage and return on capital both score only 0.33 in the quality areas, growth 0.5, and the framework marks persistence of growth at minus two — sales have averaged about 7% a year and are lumpy — which sits awkwardly against the delivered 32% EPS growth and tells you the earnings, not the revenue line, have done the compounding. Several patterns land only as PARTIAL for want of evidence rather than for contrary evidence: pricing power is attributed by management to energy-price swings and buying scale rather than deliberate price increases, the consumer brands appear once in 2022 and then vanish from the calls, and the middleman channel is simply never discussed. Innovation is a clean NO FIT — growth here is bought with capex, not invented, and the capex bill is escalating from INR4,500 crore to INR10,000 crore by FY27 with a $5 billion roadmap to 2030 behind it. The energy-transition question is live but not yet answered: the installed base is hydrocarbon-specific, and the hedge is that the same asset format is being repurposed for ammonia, via India's first independent ammonia terminal, a 15-year Hindustan Zinc contract and a green-ammonia arrangement with L&T. The combined rating of 64 out of 100 — Decent, three stars — with resilience marked STRAINED is a fair reflection: a genuinely hard-to-copy toll position whose owner cannot choose its landlord, its handful of anchor customers, or the energy price that is currently flattering its margins.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Cyclicality, Stakeholder Concentration. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.7× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 93% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ⚠️ Returns on capital slid from about 22% to 14% — the business is earning less and less on the money invested in it.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** The entire asset base is fixed infrastructure sited on government port land (Kandla/Deendayal, JNPA, Haldia, Mangalore, Pipavav, and a proposed INR20,000 cr Vadhavan investment awaiting 'land development and formal approvals'), throughput customers are the PSU oil marketing companies IOC/HPCL/BPCL, and the single largest volume driver management has cited call after call is the state-owned KGPL/Jamnagar-Loni pipeline, whose commissioning slipped from FY25 to March 2026 to June 2026 to H1 FY27 over land-compensation issues. Even the LPG import mix is shaped by a government-brokered India-US purchase arrangement.

> "We await the land development and formal approvals." — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Deliberate diversification away from PSU-linked terminalling into own-account distribution (now the profit engine), private long-term take-or-pay counterparties (a large conglomerate at Pipavav, Hindustan Zinc for ammonia, L&T MoU) and Itochu as an equity partner.*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Distribution margins — the main earnings driver — nearly doubled from ~INR4,000 to ~INR7,000/ton explicitly because 'energy prices have risen', and management insists this is sustainable, the classic cycle-peak claim; Q4 FY26 throughput was disrupted by the West Asia situation with country-wide LPG supply down 30-50%. This sits alongside a $1.2bn-by-FY27 and $5bn-by-2030 capex programme, i.e. capacity being committed at elevated margins.

> "the margin that we have earned during the year has been around INR7,000 against INR4,000-odd in the previous year" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Demand is a flow product — cooking, autogas and industrial fuel consumed in customers' operations, not their capex; liquid storage runs on take-or-pay contracts; expansion is funded from ~INR6,000 cr cash with gearing capped at 0.6x, so investment continues through disruptions rather than being cycle-forced.*

- ⚠️ Sales actually FELL in 4 of the last 11 years — revenue moves in cycles, it does not compound steadily.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 4% and 17% (±4.5 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 6% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 14%).

## What to watch

- Whether the roughly INR7,000/ton distribution margin holds when energy prices fall — the Cyclicality risk says this doubling came from price, not from durable pricing power.
- Progress on land and formal approvals for the INR20,000 crore Vadhavan project, the clearest live test of the HIGH Government Dependency risk.
