# Ahluwalia Contracts (AHLUCONT) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Ahluwalia Contracts (India) Limited is an Indian construction contractor that builds structures for other people — as management puts it, the company is "only in buildings and factories," not roads or pipelines. It takes construction contracts from government bodies and private clients (hospitals, railway station redevelopment like CSMT, government complexes like Central Vista, plus private developers such as DLF, Signature Global, SmartWorld and Emaar), builds the project over roughly 2 to 3 years, and bills the client as work progresses — so revenue comes out of a stock of signed contracts management calls the "order book" (Rs 7,588 crores in Sept 2022, Rs 21,096 crores by March 2026). It bids for work competitively, wins on being lowest bidder ("L1") or on quality/timeliness in the private market, receives mobilisation advances up front and leaves retention money with the client until completion, and does a large part of the physical work through subcontractors on labour or labour-plus-material rates. FY26 turnover was Rs 4,565 crores with a PAT of Rs 264 crores and an EBITDA margin around 9.5%; work is spread across north, east, west and south India (Delhi/NCR alone is about 44% of the order book) with a small amount outside India in Nepal.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Hospital / healthcare buildings** — Builds hospitals and medical facilities (e.g. a Tata Memorial Hospital project) — the single largest slice of the order book at 32% in the Nov 2022 breakdown.
- **Institutional and infrastructure buildings** — Railway station redevelopment (CSMT/CSTM, Rs 300-350 crores of work in FY26 and about Rs 700 crores planned for FY27), the Central Vista government project (~Rs 2,600 crores, design-build, started Jan 2026), airports and education campuses — management says this is now the deliberate focus area.
- **Residential buildings** — Constructs housing towers for private developers like DLF, SmartWorld and Emaar (13% of the order book in 2022); management has since slowed bidding here, calling residential "not a focus area," especially in NCR.
- **Commercial and industrial buildings** — Offices, hotels and factory-type projects, including the India Gem & Jewellery Park (work starting Q1 FY27, expected to bill 20-25% of project value in FY27); commercial was about 8% of the order book in 2022.
- **Government sector work** — Contracts for public clients, which were 81.5% of the order book in 2022 — high competition and thinner rates, and exposed to state cash crunches (Assam) and pollution shutdowns (NGT closures in Delhi).
- **Private sector work** — Contracts for private developers and institutions, 18.5% of the order book in 2022 but deliberately grown since, because "there are only a handful of contractors" there, competition is less and rates are better than government jobs.
- **Mechanisation / owned equipment** — A rising capex programme in machinery, decided a couple of years ago to cope with year-round labour shortages and bigger, more complex buildings on squeezed timelines; it has raised depreciation so far, with management arguing the margin benefit shows up over the coming couple of years.
- **Real estate inventory** — A small holding of own real estate (Rs 59.73 crores in the Nov 2022 balance-sheet run-through) — mentioned only in passing and immaterial next to the contracting business.

### The story in depth

Ahluwalia's strongest points all sit on the balance sheet and in the size of the work it has won. Cash comes back from customers about 20 days before suppliers have to be paid, so clients effectively fund the business, helped by INR802 crores of project mobilisation advances. The company carries INR22 crores of gross borrowing against roughly INR840 crores of cash and bank balances, has grown purely organically with no acquisitions, bids deliberately conservatively, and spent ahead of the need on mechanisation. That earned the top score on stewardship. Alongside it, the order book has gone from INR7,588 crores in November 2022 to INR21,096 crores in March 2026, with INR10,257 crores of new orders in FY26 including the largest-ever Central Vista win, and the company now works across 17 states after moving into South India and Nepal. Sales have compounded at about 14% a year, and operating profit is about INR10 on every INR100 of sales with only mild year-to-year movement. This is what makes the Market Share Gainers and Corporate Culture patterns come out as partial fits rather than misses.

The problem is that almost none of this converts into pricing. Growth is entirely volume and order-book led, and price has gone the other way: the 12% margin promised in FY23 never arrived, management now describes low-double-digits as the ceiling, and margins have de-rated from the 13-14% of the 'glory days' to 9.5%, which management calls an industry-wide phenomenon it cannot reverse. That single fact is why four separate patterns come back as no fit at all — Pricing Power, Cost to Replicate, Innovation Dominance and Toll Roads — and why the Competitive Advantage area of the quality framework scores zero. It is also the same fact underneath the Good Enough Goods risk: work is awarded on lowest-bid tendering, the service is largely commoditised, and the advantages that do exist (prequalification for bigger tickets, in-house design, machinery) are things a funded rival could buy. The reputation with repeat marquee clients such as DLF, Adani, Google, Tata Memorial, CSMT and Central Vista is real, but it buys more work rather than a better price, which is exactly why Brand Strength is only a partial fit. The cost culture — clustering sites to spread overheads, INR274 crores of mechanisation capex in FY26 — is genuine, but scale has not produced high returns, so Low Price Plus is partial too; management itself says mechanisation raises capital spending and depreciation without lifting margins yet. Order book growth also outruns what gets billed, with revenue converting at only about 11% growth. And the INR840 crores of cash that supports the stewardship score also drives the one clear negative on shareholder returns: there is no dividend, no buyback and no distribution policy ever set out, and the single analyst question about it goes unanswered.

The risk review points at the same traits from the other side. The order book that drives the market-share story was 81.51% government in FY23 and still leans heavily on central and state tenders — Central Vista at INR2,600 crores, CSMT, the Varanasi, Darbhanga and Kota airports, Chandigarh station — and receipts repeatedly stall on state finances and election cycles in places like Bihar and Himachal, which is the high-risk Government Dependency call. The very same large contracts also create the concentration watch item, since one client's redesign or a delayed land handover resets guidance on its own. Cyclicality is elevated because all revenue depends on clients' own building programmes and swings with monsoons, elections, pollution bans and design delays; the Q1FY25 operating margin fell to 6.58% from 10.83%. Yet the strengths do defend against this: escalation clauses and cement and steel pass-throughs cushion input costs, the INR21,096 crore order book gives 24 to 30 months of visibility, the net cash position absorbs delayed government payments, and the deliberate shift to private clients — from 81% public in FY23 to over 60% private by FY26 — attacks the dependency directly, even as concentration in the NCR has actually risen back to 44%. Technology and new-entrant risks are both low, and two risk channels show no signal at all, which is why resilience reads as sound. The combined rating of 49 out of 100 — 58 on business quality, only 25 on multibagger fit, 62 on risk safety — sums it up: a well-run, well-financed, growing contractor with an unusually good cash cycle, but no durable pricing advantage, and management's case for margin recovery from 9.5% rests on an industry inflection and steadier state governments rather than on anything the company controls.

### Low Price Plus — PARTIAL
*"Cheapest — and still the best"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (0 of 1 checks supporting).

**What the calls show:** There is a real cost culture — geographic clustering of sites to spread overheads (Nov 2022 through Aug 2024 calls), mechanisation capex of INR274 crores in FY26 to cut labour dependency — but scale has not produced high returns: EBITDA fell from ~11% (FY23) to 8.3% (FY25) and capex intensity is rising without commensurate margin, so the low-cost edge is not demonstrably hard to copy.

> "within a radius of about 15 to 20 kilometers, we would be doing nearly 20 million square feet. So, obviously the overheads can be spread, the cost can be reduced." — *management, on an earnings call*

- ❌ Keeps ₹52 of every ₹100 of sales after input costs and turns its assets 1.1× a year — not the high-turnover, low-margin machine this pattern needs.

### Brand Strength — PARTIAL
*"The name itself sells"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits, with mixed numbers (1 of 2 checks supporting).

**What the calls show:** A genuine reputational franchise in complex buildings — repeat marquee clients (DLF, Adani, Google, Tata Memorial, CSMT, Central Vista) and a stated scarcity of credible private-sector contractors — but it converts into work volume, not premium pricing or A&P-backed brand economics, and margins fell while the order book tripled from INR7,588 crores (Nov 2022) to INR21,096 crores (Mar 2026).

> "There is greater demand for timely delivery , quality delivery. There are only a handful of contractors in the private sector side. The competition is less." — *management, on an earnings call*

*"Winning customers from rivals"*
