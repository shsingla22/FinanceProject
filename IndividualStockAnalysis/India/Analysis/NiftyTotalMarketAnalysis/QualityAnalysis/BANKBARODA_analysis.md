# Bank of Baroda (BANKBARODA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

Bank of Baroda is a large Indian bank that takes deposits from the public and lends the money out at a higher interest rate — the gap between what it pays savers and what it earns from borrowers (its net interest margin, around 2.8–3.2%) is its main source of income. It lends to individuals (home, car, education, gold, mortgage and personal loans), to farmers, to small and mid-sized businesses, and to large companies, and it also runs a treasury that invests in bonds and trades. It operates mainly across India but has a meaningful overseas business too, taking international deposits and making international loans that were growing over 20% a year by 2026. By March 2026 its total business (deposits plus loans) had crossed Rs 30 lakh crore, with full-year net profit of about Rs 20,021 crore and a return on assets consistently above 1%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Retail** — Loans to individuals — home loans (historically about 70% of the retail book), auto, education, mortgage and personal loans — the fastest-growing lending segment, crossing Rs 3 lakh crore by March 2026 and growing 18% a year.
- **Agriculture** — Farm lending, including a growing share of gold loans, which management highlights as improving the quality of this book; growing roughly 9–21% a year across the calls.
- **MSME** — Loans to micro, small and medium businesses, run as its own vertical; growth picked up from around 10% in 2024 to over 20% by mid-2026.
- **RAM (Retail, Agriculture, MSME combined)** — How management groups its three higher-yielding lending books and reports them as a bloc — described as the bank's core business model focus, at 61% of the loan book by March 2026.
- **Corporate credit** — Big-ticket lending to large companies — historically the largest part of the balance sheet and the swing factor in overall growth, though the bank has at times deliberately slowed it to protect margins; grew 11–15% in the most recent quarters.
- **International banking** — Overseas deposit-taking and lending, mostly corporate in nature, which management says has often carried better margins than domestic corporate lending; grew 23–24% a year in 2026 and is funded largely by overseas deposits rather than wholesale borrowing.
- **Treasury** — Manages the bank's own investment portfolio and trading; its gains and mark-to-market losses swing operating profit from quarter to quarter, and RBI valuation rule changes have twice been cited for dips.
- **Deposits / retail liability franchise** — The funding side of the bank — current and savings accounts (CASA), term deposits and bulk deposits; CASA is treated as the strategic prize because it is cheap money, running around 38–44% of deposits, while bulk deposits have been deliberately cut back to protect margins.
- **Digital banking (bob World)** — The bank's digital platform, credited with driving very fast personal loan growth (nearly 150% in one early quarter) and run under an IT/platform-functions vertical.
- **Stressed assets / recovery (SAMV)** — A dedicated vertical that collects on and recovers bad loans; the bank targeted Rs 10,000 crore of recoveries in FY25 and reports gross NPAs down from 3.5% to about 2% and credit costs under 0.5%.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Bank of Baroda comes out of these three reviews as a solidly run but structurally ordinary lender — an overall score of 53 out of 100, or "Decent," built from a business-quality score of 47 and a risk-safety score of 64. What it does well is visible in the checks and the patterns at the same time. It is taking share from a non-dominant position, with advances up 16.2% in FY26 and 17.4% in Q1FY27, retail/agri/MSME lending now 61% of the book and total business past Rs 30 lakh crore, and it has done so without loosening standards — slippage of 0.91% and SMA of 0.07% are what turn the "Market Share Gainers" pattern from a growth story into a credible one. The same discipline shows up as "Corporate Culture": guidance on slippage, credit cost and bad loans repeatedly met or beaten, ROA above 1% for 15 to 18 straight quarters, and an income-tax refund used to build a Rs 1,500 crore floating provision instead of being booked as profit. Management scores well on both stewardship and growth-directed spending, with the bob World digital platform driving roughly 150% personal-loan growth to 22 million users and new subsidiaries funded internally.

The catch is that almost none of this converts into pricing power, and that single fact links most of the negative checks, the weakest patterns and one of the risks. Commercial banking products here are described as undifferentiated commodities — the bank competes on price for both corporate loans and deposits — which is why "Mini-monopolies" scores worst, "Intangible benefits" is negative, and the "Pricing Power" pattern is not merely absent but contradicted: the yield on advances fell from 8.55% in Q1FY25 to 7.37% in Q1FY27 and net interest margin compressed from 3.18% to 2.77%, with management conceding that repricing depends on the market. The "Good Enough Goods" risk is the same trait viewed from the risk side — management openly says it cannot hold both price and volume in corporate lending and has repeatedly let fine-priced, low-yielding corporate assets run off. So the growth that earns the Market Share Gainers pattern is volume-led rather than price-led, and it is being bought with thinner spreads. The genuinely hard-to-replicate assets — a nationwide low-cost deposit base with cost of deposits down to 4.66% by July 2026, and one of the largest international networks of any Indian bank — are what keep "Low Price Plus," "Cost to Replicate" and "Brand Strength" at partial rather than absent, but none of them reaches full strength. Return on capital scores worst of all seven quality areas, because a leveraged bank balance sheet with roughly 1.06–1.15% return on assets and no moat protecting spreads has nowhere structurally better to go.

On risk, the review flags one high item and four to watch, and each connects back to something already noted as a strength. Government dependency is rated high risk: as a public sector bank, the regulator sets the economics, and the RBI's investment-valuation norms alone cut operating profit 8.5% in Q1FY25, while the very floating provisions that demonstrate prudence cannot be released without regulatory permission. Cyclicality is the mirror image of today's clean numbers — credit cost at 0.29%, gross bad loans at 1.89% and SMA at 0.07% are cycle-best rather than normal levels, which is exactly why the "Cyclical market growth" check scores negative even though the numbers look good; treasury income also swung from roughly Rs 2,000–2,200 crore in June 2025 to Rs 800 crore a year later. Concentration risk is not theoretical: one legacy corporate exposure, NMC Health, forced a USD 600 million out-of-court settlement that cut Q1FY27 net profit from a would-be Rs 5,528 crore to Rs 1,278 crore and return on assets from 1.10% to 0.25% — a reminder that a single name can still dominate a quarter despite the shift to granular lending. Deposit migration out of the banking system is the standing threat to the cheap funding base that underpins the whole story, softened for now by CASA growth of 9.8–10% described as top-quartile. Technology is the one low risk, since the disruption discussed is the bank's own build rather than an outside threat. The bank is also thoroughly covered by analysts and media, so there is no hidden-gem discount here; overall resilience was not established.

Actually, one clarification on the balance of evidence: seven of the eight risks and patterns above are partial or watch-level rather than clear-cut, which is what produces a middling score rather than a strong or weak one.

> "the trading profit... has gone down to 800 crores because of the market scenario" — *management, on an earnings call*
