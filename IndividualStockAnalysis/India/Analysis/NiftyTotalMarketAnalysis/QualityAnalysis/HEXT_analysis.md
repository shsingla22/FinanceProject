# Hexaware Tech. (HEXT) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Hexaware Technologies (HEXT) is an India-headquartered IT services and outsourcing company, listed in Mumbai since February 2025, that runs and rebuilds the technology of large corporations for a fee. Clients hand it work such as day-to-day IT operations, application maintenance, software engineering, data engineering, modernizing decades-old legacy code, cybersecurity and back-office business process services, and Hexaware delivers it with roughly 34,000 employees, so revenue is essentially billings for people and platform-based services under multi-year outsourcing, consolidation and transformation contracts (quarterly revenue was $389 million in Q1 CY26). Customers are big global firms in financial services, banking, insurance, manufacturing and consumer, high tech and professional services, plus named examples in airlines, pharma/clinical research, casual dining, data centres and asset management; the top 10 clients are about 36% of revenue and it now has two clients above $100 million a year. Its largest market by far is the U.S., followed by Europe/UK and Asia Pacific (including India and a build-out in the Middle East), and it reports on a calendar-year fiscal.

**The parts of the business:**

- **IT Services** — The core business — application, infrastructure, engineering and data work for clients — and the larger, faster-growing part of the company: management said IT grew close to 9% year-on-year versus 4.7% for BPS, and IT headcount has risen for 11 straight quarters.
- **BPS (Business Process Services)** — Outsourced back-office/process work, reported separately from IT; it is the smaller, slower-growing piece and was where headcount actually shrank in Q1 CY26.
- **Financial Services (FS)** — Serving asset managers, insurers and similar firms; called out as one of the strongest growth verticals after a 'smart recovery', at ~19% growth in CY24, though one large client's spending cuts made a quarter or two lumpy.
- **Banking** — Work for banks, including large consolidation deals where a bank cuts its vendor list and Hexaware wins a bigger share; it was the weak spot in CY24 at 7.6% growth but rebounded 13.5% sequentially in Q2 CY25 and is described as a growth driver into CY26.
- **High Tech and Professional Services (HTPS)** — Technology companies and professional-services firms; the fastest-growing vertical at about 22% in CY24, and management hired a dedicated head to attack what it calls a large untapped segment.
- **Technology, Products and Platforms (TPP)** — A new vertical being carved out of HTPS and reported separately from Q1 CY26 — management's bet on selling to software and technology product companies as a distinct growth engine.
- **Manufacturing and Consumer (M&C)** — Factory and consumer-goods clients; the problem area — the only vertical with negative growth, hurt by tariffs, trade uncertainty and two clients that went through Chapter 11 bankruptcy.
- **RapidX (legacy modernization / software engineering)** — An in-house AI platform that reverse-engineers very old code and the business logic buried in it (one U.S. airline's code was over 50 years old) and rebuilds it; billed as a 'growth accelerator', with two or three large modernizations due to complete in CY26 and a 'zero-license' offering built on it to move clients off paid SaaS software.
- **Tensai (IT operations platform)** — The platform behind its IT outsourcing work, being rebuilt around AI agents — roughly 80 supervisory and 400 atomic agents — which management says let it renew one large outsourcing contract at 50% more volume for about 10% more revenue.
- **AI for business services** — Building AI agents into clients' own operations (clinical research steps for a global CRO, aircraft-repair guidance for an airline, factory yield models for a chip maker); management says this pool of opportunity is bigger in total than all its AI-for-IT work.
- **GCC setup (SMC acquisition)** — Helping multinationals set up and then run their own captive offshore centres in India and elsewhere; acquired in mid-2025 with about $22 million of prior-year revenue, EPS accretive from day one, aimed at a market growing from roughly 1,700 to 2,700 GCCs in India.
- **Cybersolve (cybersecurity)** — An acquired identity-led cybersecurity capability, credited with winning a deal at a data centre company in Q1 CY26.
- **Private equity / private markets practice** — A new sales push at PE firms and the companies they own, launched in 2025 with a dedicated Chief Private Markets Officer hired in early 2026; pipeline is building, with revenue expected to follow.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Hexaware is, first and foremost, a well-run and genuinely cash-generative business, and that is where its scores are strongest. It earns ₹24 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital employed, turns every ₹1 of assets into ₹1.16 of sales, and does it with no debt, $220-235m of cash, collections in 65-75 days, and operating cash flow running at about 76% of EBITDA and 125% of profit after tax. Sales have compounded at roughly 16% a year with unusually little year-to-year variation, and the operating margin sits near 14% and moves only about a point and a bit either way. The framework scores Return on Capital a full 1.0 and Capital Allocation, Growth and Management all at about 0.83. Management's conduct is scored as a standout: it pre-announced that it would stop reporting an adjusted EBITDA number, itemised every one-off (the client provision, restructuring, impairment, the labour code charge) and flagged its own misses, including lowering growth guidance mid-year. That same behaviour is what makes Corporate Culture the only STRONG FIT in the multibagger screen — the March 2025 promise of 17.3% reported margin in CY25 landed at 17.1%, the promised banking recovery arrived, and the manufacturing-and-consumer bottoming call played out. Cash return, candour and delivery are one trait seen from three angles. The main gap on the shareholder-return side is that the ~45% dividend policy is consistent but there is no buyback, so there is no evidence of the company buying its own shares when they are cheap.

The growth story and the competitive weakness turn out to be the same fact viewed from two ends. Hexaware is demonstrably taking share: it is a sub-$1.6bn player, its client pyramid keeps deepening ($100m clients going from one to two, more $50m clients, $10m-plus clients up four year-on-year to 34, pipeline past $4bn), CY24 growth of 13.5% beat almost all peers, and it keeps winning vendor-consolidation mandates including a professional-services firm going sole-source. Platform R&D scores a 2 because it converts into paid work rather than sitting in a lab — RapidX moved from proofs of concept to two large legacy-modernisation deals, and Amaze was adopted as a top-five bank's only refactoring standard. But the wins are being bought, not commanded. The screen rates Pricing Power, Toll Roads and Cost to Replicate all NO FIT, and the quality framework scores Competitive Advantage at zero and Industry Structure at 0.17. The clearest single illustration is the flagship renewal that delivered 50% more volume for only about 10% more revenue: that is a market-share win and a unit-price cut in the same contract. Management openly budgets for AI-driven price deflation. What makes those wins affordable is a real, itemised cost culture — industry-low attrition around 11%, Tier-2 delivery locations, offshore mix up about 440 basis points, utilisation pushed to 83-84%, a European restructuring, best-in-industry collections and a low tax rate — but the screen rates even that only a PARTIAL Low Price Plus, and notes nothing described would stop a well-funded entrant. Brand Strength is likewise PARTIAL: third-party validation is real and repeated (Brand Finance top-25 IT brand, ranked #1 by Whitelane in the UK and Ireland), but no advertising spend is quantified and there is no heritage story behind it.

The risk review lands on SOUND resilience with one ELEVATED item and six on watch, and the elevated one attacks the growth engine directly. Technological Innovation is rated elevated because AI is rewriting how IT services are delivered, not just what is sold — management opened the February 2026 call noting an Anthropic release that 'shook our stock markets globally for our industry' and conceded the software development lifecycle is being rewritten. The offsetting evidence is the same platform cadence that earns the Innovation Dominance partial fit, plus the zero-license anti-SaaS offering and a first vibe-coding offering. Several other risks are simply the flip side of listed strengths: the vendor consolidation that wins Hexaware share is also Shifting Consumer Preferences, alongside clients insourcing into GCCs (1,700 rising to 2,700 in India) — which is why the company bought SMC to sell GCC set-up and now competes with specialists like ANSR, the New Entrants risk. The self-serve AI substitution behind Good Enough Goods is the same force behind the no-fit on Pricing Power, with 90-95% of clients already buying AI coding tools themselves. Concentration remains real despite the widening pyramid: one government-sponsored enterprise client is down 40-45% over four quarters at a cost of about 70 basis points a year, one European client triggered a $9m provision, one client's furlough dented a quarter, and two clients went through Chapter 11. Policy noise (DOGE, tariffs, a $12.5m Indian labour code charge) and ordinary cyclicality sit alongside it, while Fashion Risk is low because the revenue is contractual and operations-embedded. The combined rating of 58 out of 100 — Decent, three stars — reflects exactly this shape: 65 on business quality, 66 on risk safety, and only 40 on multibagger fit. This is a high-quality operator and an honest one, gaining ground in an industry whose economics are being repriced around it.
