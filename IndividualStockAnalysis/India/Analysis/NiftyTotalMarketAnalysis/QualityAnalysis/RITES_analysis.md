# RITES Ltd. (RITES) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: CONSTRUCTION*

RITES Ltd. is an Indian government-owned company (a Schedule 'A' enterprise of the Government of India, listed on the NSE and BSE) that mainly sells engineering and project-management consultancy for infrastructure — management explicitly says "we are not a construction company, we are a project management consultancy company." Its customers are Indian Railways and other government bodies, public-sector and institutional clients such as educational institutions, and foreign governments and railways in Africa, South and Southeast Asia and the Middle East. It earns money in four main ways: a percentage fee on the cost of infrastructure projects (booked either as pure consultancy or, when a client wants a single-window contract, as much larger "turnkey" revenue for the same scope of work), fees for inspecting and certifying goods (quality assurance), exporting locomotives and coaches to overseas railways, and leasing; it also owns 51% of REMC, a power/renewable-energy joint venture. It runs 700+ live orders across India and abroad, ended FY26 with a record order book of about INR 9,416 crore, and says its business needs hardly any working capital.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Consultancy (project/infrastructure consultancy)** — Design, project management and supervision of infrastructure projects for a fee that is a percentage of the project cost — the highest-margin stream (about 35% margin in Q3 FY26) and the core of the company, with consolidated consultancy revenue reaching an all-time high of roughly INR 1,300 crore in FY24.
- **Turnkey (turnkey construction projects)** — The same consultancy role delivered as a single-window contract where RITES books the whole project cost as revenue and pays out the executing contractors, so it is very large in the order book (about 49% of it) but carries very thin margins (around 1.3% at PBT level), and the actual fee earned is no different from consultancy mode.
- **Quality Assurance (QA)** — Third-party inspection and certification of equipment and supplies, historically about two-thirds dependent on Indian Railways and a big profit contributor; after Railways cut back, management deliberately shifted the mix so over half now comes from non-railway clients such as the PM Vishwakarma Scheme (an INR 67 crore order) and the GeM procurement portal.
- **Exports of rolling stock ("Expotech" / part of RITES Videsh)** — Sourcing and shipping locomotives and coaches to overseas railways — orders from Mozambique, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka — with revenue booked only on shipment, an all-time-high export order book of about INR 1,700 crore at end-FY26 and execution timelines of two to three years; margins have fallen from historic ~25% to roughly 12–13.5% now that most orders are won through global competitive tenders rather than Indian line-of-credit deals.
- **International consultancy (RITES Videsh)** — Overseas advisory and supervision work such as project management for Ghana Railways (~INR 97 crore) and highway construction supervision in Guyana, run from a stronghold in Africa plus newer offices in the UAE and pursuit of Southeast Asian markets; together with exports it made up an international order book of about INR 2,150 crore at end-Q3 FY26.
- **Leasing** — Leasing out rolling stock/assets — a smaller stream that management describes as sitting midway between the high consultancy margins and the very low turnkey margins.
- **REMC (REMCL) – power subsidiary** — A 51%-owned company in renewable and round-the-clock power supply that signed a 900 MW power purchase agreement and a 700 MW letter of award and commissioned a solar plant at Bhilai; in FY24 it posted record revenue (up 30%+) and profit (up 37%+) and paid about INR 80 crore of dividend, roughly half of which came to RITES.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

RITES enters this review as a five-decade-old government engineering consultancy whose single greatest strength and single greatest vulnerability turn out to be the same thing: its official standing. As a Schedule 'A' Government of India enterprise it did all of Indian Railways' quality-assurance inspection for more than forty years on nomination, and that credential still opens doors — it wins nomination work at home and has carried the firm onto global tenders in Ghana, Guyana, Bangladesh, Mozambique and a new UAE office. That is why the screen marks Brand Strength and the certification-style 'Toll Roads' business as likely fits. But the records also show the toll booth being dismantled: from FY24 Indian Railways opened inspection to four players, consultancy moved from nomination to competitive bidding, and exports moved from Indian-only funded tenders to open global ones. The 'Cost to Replicate' pattern fails for exactly this reason — the advantage was granted administratively and removed by decision, after which rivals immediately took share. The same fact drives the two HIGH RISK verdicts, Government Dependency and New Entrants, and it is why Industry Structure is the weakest of the seven quality areas at a clearly negative score.

The company's response to that shock is the most encouraging thread in the records, and management deserves credit for describing it honestly. Candor scores at the top of the range: leadership quantified the two structural challenges up front, admitted export margins have permanently reset from around 25% to 12-13%, and warned that newer, lower-margin orders will drag reported profitability down. It then did what it said it would. Quality assurance was rebuilt over two years back to FY24 revenue levels with the client mix flipped from roughly two-thirds Indian Railways to about 60% non-Railways — defence, power distribution companies, GeM and PM Vishwakarma. The first export orders in four years arrived. Spare broad-gauge diesel locomotives were converted to Cape gauge for around eleven African countries, with 30 units ordered. The order book compounded from about ₹5,100 crore in Q1 FY23 to an all-time-high ₹9,416 crore, international business reached a record ₹2,150 crore, and inflow runs at roughly 1.5 orders a day. Growth is purely organic, working capital is negligible, dividends are generous, and management held the line rather than retrenching through the downturn — hence the STRONG FIT on Corporate Culture and the SOUND resilience read.

The catch is that this recovery is being bought with volume rather than value, and the records are blunt about it. Pricing, Mix and Volume is the worst-scoring check in the whole framework at -2: roughly 70% of fresh inflow is competitively won, low-margin turnkey work is rising toward about 49% of the order book at margins near 1.3%, and even remaining nomination clients are renegotiating fees downward. So the very order-book growth that supports the Market Share Gainers case is the mechanism through which blended margins fall — share was won in new segments precisely while it was lost in the profitable core franchise, which is why that pattern lands only as PARTIAL, and why Pricing Power and Low Price Plus are outright NO FITs. Management's answer is a self-imposed discipline of not bidding below 20% operating margin and 15% net margin, and operating margin today still sits near 23% of sales with unusually steady year-to-year movement. The counterweights to cyclicality are real too — a fee-based model, 13 verticals, 700-plus concurrent orders, price-variation clauses passing input costs to executors, and management framing FY24 as the trough ahead of an FY26 growth year and an FY27 'disruptive growth' year. The combined rating of 49 out of 100, a Mixed two-star grade, reflects exactly this split: decent business quality and a fair multibagger fit dragged down hard by a risk-safety score of only 22, because the moat that made the profits was never the company's to keep.
