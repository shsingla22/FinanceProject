# IDFC First Bank Ltd. (IDFCFIRSTB) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

IDFC FIRST Bank is an Indian bank, formed by the merger of IDFC Bank and Capital First, that takes deposits from the public and lends the money out. It sells savings and current accounts, fixed deposits, and a wide range of loans — home/mortgage, vehicle, consumer, small-business (MSME), rural/agri, microfinance and credit cards to individual and small-business borrowers, plus corporate and commercial loans to companies. It earns most of its money from the spread between the interest it charges on loans (net interest margin around 5.9-6.2% on assets) and its cost of deposits (about 6%), topped up by fee income from lending, credit-card spends, wealth management, trade and foreign exchange, and occasional treasury/trading gains. It operates only in India, is listed on the NSE and BSE, and by the June 2026 quarter had a loan book of about INR3.05 lakh crore, customer deposits of just under INR3 lakh crore, and quarterly profit of INR1,075 crore.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Deposits (CASA and retail deposits)** — The bank's funding engine — savings, current and fixed-deposit accounts gathered mainly from retail customers; customer deposits were close to INR3 lakh crore with CASA (low-cost current and savings money) at INR1.58 lakh crore, a CASA ratio above 50%, and management repeatedly calls this the foundation that gives it 'license to grow the loans'.
- **Retail, Rural and MSME loans** — The core lending business — mortgages, vehicle loans, consumer loans, rural/agri and small-business loans — at about INR2.4 lakh crore (roughly four-fifths of the loan book), growing ~18% a year with gross NPAs of 1.40% and collection efficiency of 99.5%.
- **Wholesale / corporate loans** — Loans to companies (non-infrastructure corporate and commercial banking, including trade and FX services that generate fees); about INR64,000 crore and the fastest-growing part at ~30% year-on-year, after the bank deliberately went slow here for years following its legacy problems.
- **Microfinance (MFI)** — Small loans to low-income borrowers, a INR6,700 crore book that caused a stretch of heavy provisions and losses from about 2024 through 2025 but has since 'largely got restored' on asset quality, with 93% of it now covered by the government CGFMU guarantee scheme.
- **Credit cards** — A consumer card business with 4.8 million cards in force and a INR9,600 crore outstanding book growing ~19%, with card spends up 22% year-on-year — a source of both interest and fee income.
- **Wealth management** — Managing and distributing investments for customers, with assets under management of about INR64,000 crore growing 24% a year; a fee-income business rather than a lending one.
- **Legacy infrastructure book** — Old infrastructure project loans (roughly INR12,000-13,000 crore of accounts at merger, including toll roads) inherited from the pre-merger IDFC Bank, which management has been running down and provisioning away — a shrinking drag rather than a business they are growing.
- **Treasury** — The bank's own investment/trading book in government securities, which produced a INR181 crore gain in the June 2026 quarter; management treats it as volatile and reports 'core operating profit' excluding it.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

IDFC First Bank comes out of the three reviews as a genuine, well-run growth story that has not yet turned into a genuinely profitable one. The combined rating of 54 out of 100 ("Decent", three stars) is built from three very different legs: business quality scores 52, fit against the multibagger patterns scores only 28, and risk safety scores 88. In other words, most of the score is carried by what could go wrong being reasonably contained, not by what is going right being exceptional. The quality framework says the same thing in its area scores: Growth 0.8 and Customer Benefits 0.75 sit at the top, while Return on Capital is at the floor (-1.0), Competitive Advantage is a flat zero and Industry Structure is negative. The pattern screen found no confirmed match at all — six of eleven patterns came back only partially met and one was a numbers-only hint with no support from the calls. Resilience is recorded as unknown.

The engine of the story is deposits, and it is the same engine that explains most of the strengths, most of the pattern near-misses and one of the flagged risks. The bank has built a cheap, granular funding base — roughly half of deposits in low-cost current and savings accounts for four straight years, cost of funds down to 5.96% by the first quarter of FY27 — and has used it to take share far above the system, with deposits growing 32-37% and loans compounding 20-25%, credit cards going from 1 million issued in 2022 to 4.8 million by mid-2026, and branches rising from 651 to 961. That earns the top mark on market share gain and drives the partial fits on Market Share Gainers, Low Price Plus and Cost to Replicate. The pricing power it demonstrates, though, is one-sided: the bank cut its savings rate to 3% and still grew retail deposits 37%, which is real strength on the funding side and is exactly why the review rates the Good Enough Goods risk as merely a watch item rather than worse. On the lending side there is no evidence of raising prices, the net interest margin has drifted from 6.18% to 5.96%, and the mix has moved toward lower-yielding secured, mortgage and corporate loans. So growth is volume-led rather than price-led — which is why the same franchise scores -1 on pricing, mix and volume and why Pricing Power and Brand Strength stop at partial.

The costs of that build are where the weaknesses cluster, and management says so itself. Seven years in, the cost-to-income ratio is still 70.7% and return on assets only touches about 1% (roughly 0.9% once one-off gains are stripped out), which produces the -1 on asset turn and the bottom score on return on capital; the heavy branch and in-house technology spending is credited as growth investment rather than maintenance, but the payoff has not yet shown up in returns, which is also why Innovation Dominance stays partial. Book value, by management's own admission, has grown substantially from repeatedly raising equity at a premium rather than from retained profit, and there is no dividend or buyback — hence the -1 on distribution to shareholders and the capped Capital Allocation score. Notably, the willingness to admit all of this is itself scored as a strength: candor rates a 2 and underpins the partial fit on Corporate Culture, with the same chief executive throughout, an apology for the toll-road surprise and a plain admission that the cost-income target was missed. On the risk side, only three of eight channels are flagged. Earnings have proved to swing with the credit cycle — the microfinance downturn plus a legacy toll account cut second-quarter FY25 profit to Rs.201 crore against an adjusted Rs.626 crore — and two separate policy actions bit inside the window: a state toll waiver forcing about Rs.250 crore of provisions, and the Reserve Bank raising microfinance risk weights from 75% to 125% at a cost of 21 basis points of core capital. Against that, the bank has deliberately de-risked, cutting fixed-infrastructure lending from 22% of assets to 1.2%, working out Rs.14,000 crore of legacy stressed loans, taking top-20 borrower exposure from 16% to 9% and the telecom exposure to zero, with collection efficiency steady at 99.5% throughout. That de-risking is why four of the eight channels are rated low and why bad loans fell from 3.36% to 1.51%; it is also part of why yields are lower. The bank is now coming off the bottom of the microfinance cycle — slippages down 30% year-on-year and profit up 132% — so the open question is simply whether the recovering revenue finally outruns the cost base.

## What to watch
