# Orkla India (ORKLAINDIA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Orkla India is a packaged-food company that sells everyday Indian cooking staples under two old, well-known brands: MTR (about 100 years old) and Eastern (about 40 years old). It makes money selling two kinds of products to ordinary household shoppers — spices (single spices and blended masalas), which are about 67% of revenue, and convenience foods (ready mixes, ready-to-eat sweets, meal products), about 33% — through roughly 679,000 retail touchpoints, plus modern trade and fast-growing online/quick-commerce channels. Its strategy is deliberately regional rather than pan-Indian, on the view that 'food is local': about 70% of revenue comes from South India, where MTR leads in Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh and Eastern leads in Kerala. Another 21% of revenue comes from exports to Indians living abroad in 45 countries, mainly the Gulf. FY26 revenue was INR 2,493 crores (up 5.7%) with EBITDA of INR 424 crores, a 16.9% margin, produced from eight in-house factories and 22 outsourced units.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Spices** — Sells pure/single spices (chili, coriander powders) and blended masalas such as Sambar, Puliogare, Rasam, Garam Masala and Eastern Chicken Masala; it is the bigger half of the company at 67% of revenue, grew volumes 10.1% in Q3 FY26 and revenue 6.1% in Q4 FY26, with reported revenue held back for two years by a 20–30% collapse in raw spice prices that forced MRP cuts (prices began rising again by Q4 FY26).
- **Convenience Foods** — Ready-mix and ready-to-eat products across three platforms — Sweets (e.g. Gulab Jamun, ready-to-eat Mysore Pak, now in 50,000 outlets), Breakfast (Rava Idli, Dosa Mix, Upma Mix, plus a fresh idli-dosa batter business) and Meals (the Minute range and cooking ingredients) — contributing 33% of revenue, growing 19.2% in Q2 FY26 and 6.4% in Q4 FY26, and described by management as the long-term growth driver.
- **International business** — Exports the same MTR and Eastern products to Indian migrants in 45 countries, worth about 21% of consolidated revenue, with the Gulf (GCC) making up 70% of it and growing 11.8–16.4%; Eastern is the No. 1 Indian spice brand in the UAE by household reach and has been India's largest branded spice exporter for 24 straight years.
- **Digital commerce (Project BOLT)** — Online and quick-commerce selling, described as one of the fastest-growing channels — up about 49% in Q2 FY26 and 43.4% in Q3 FY26 — with a dedicated investment programme called Project BOLT that, along with freight costs, cost roughly 200 basis points of Q4 FY26 margin.
- **Distribution network (incl. Eastern route-to-market restructuring)** — The reach engine behind sales — about 679,000 retail touchpoints (~70% in core southern markets) plus 765 rural distributors across Karnataka, AP and Telangana; management is currently rebuilding Eastern's legacy 'one system for all' distribution into separate spices, convenience-food and modern-trade structures, which caused near-term disruption in Kerala in Q4 FY26.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Orkla India is a branded packaged-foods business built on two old, well-known names — MTR at 100 years and Eastern at 40 — that hold the number one position in Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Kerala, and rank as the top Indian spice brand by household reach in the UAE. The quality framework scores it best on the things a food company can control: customer benefits and management both come out at the top of the range, and growth close behind. Reach is the practical expression of that — 679,000 selling points, 765 rural distributors opening 5,000 new villages, a direct-to-retail system for Eastern, and digital commerce growing 38% to 8.7% of domestic sales. Advertising is doing real work rather than decorating the P&L, with regional campaigns converting into measurable penetration gains. Out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹17 is operating profit, and that margin has been steady. The demand backdrop supports the effort: 60% of the spice market is still unorganized and formalising, per-capita consumption is very low (12 of 260 sambar occasions even in its strongest state), and after seven quarters of falling spice prices the cycle has turned upward.

Those same strengths explain both which multibagger patterns fit and why the overall pattern score is low at 26 out of 100. Only two of eleven patterns are likely fits — Brand Strength and Market Share Gainers — and both rest on the identical engine of heritage brands plus distribution plus advertising: penetration for MTR pure spices went from 20.3% to 30.6% in Karnataka and 4.3% to 13% in Andhra Pradesh, 22,000 outlets were added, and volume growth accelerated from 1.8% in FY23 to 5.9% in FY26. But that engine is one the company has to keep pushing. There is no self-reinforcing effect — the network effects check is scored at the bottom of the range because each new customer adds nothing for the next one — and competitive advantage as a whole scores negative. Growth has averaged about 20% a year but is lumpy, so persistence scores negative too. Six patterns land as partial and two as no fit: pricing power is capped because pure-spice prices are mechanically linked to mandi prices, innovation is steady but small in scale, and forward integration into direct-to-consumer selling is real but early. In short, the company is winning share the hard way, by outspending and out-distributing a weak unorganized field, rather than sitting on a structural lock.

The risk review is the most reassuring of the three (safety 78, resilience judged sound), and its findings mostly mirror the pattern conclusions rather than contradicting them. Seven of eight risk channels are low or merely worth watching; the one elevated risk, Good Enough Goods, comes from exactly the trait that caps the pricing-power pattern — in pure spices, the larger part of a segment that is 67% of revenue, management says the brand premium cannot exceed 10-15% before customers simply buy and grind chilli themselves, which forced price cuts through the deflation stretch. The offset is that blended spices are recipes consumers cannot copy at home and hold a firmer premium. Two more links run the same way: the commodity cycle that earns a full mark as a growth tailwind now that prices have turned is the same cycle flagged under cyclicality, and GST formalisation that transfers share from the unorganized trade also showed up as a cost, with 7-10 days of billing lost during the GST 2.0 transition and PLI income falling to zero. Concentration is geographic rather than customer-specific — 70% of revenue from four southern states and 70% of the international business from the Gulf — and Kerala alone cut fourth-quarter group growth from 7.8% to 6.2%, showing how one region's disruption moves the whole number. Combining 62 for business quality, 26 for pattern fit and 78 for risk safety gives 55 out of 100, a Decent three-star verdict: a well-run, well-liked brand business in a growing market, without the self-reinforcing economics that would justify more.

### Cyclicality — WATCH
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Revenue and reported margins swing with agri-commodity cycles: two years of unprecedented spice deflation (>30%, chili -50%) forced MRP cuts that held spices value growth to ~0-3% despite 6-10% volume growth, and Q4 FY26 marks the turn into an inflationary cycle with chili up 20-30%. The demand base itself is a flow/consumable staple with volume growth accelerating from 1.8% (FY23) to 5.9% (FY26), so the cycle hits realisation and PLI, not consumption.

> "we've had two straight years of deflation. This unprecedented price movement of over 30% was led by chili, which reduced by 50%" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/consumable food demand tied to daily cooking, not customer capex; volume growth stayed positive and accelerated through the deflation cycle, and EBITDA margin still expanded from 14.4% (FY23) to 16.9% (FY26)*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 0 of the last 6 years with swings of ±33 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 13% and 20% (±2.4 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 2% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 4%).
