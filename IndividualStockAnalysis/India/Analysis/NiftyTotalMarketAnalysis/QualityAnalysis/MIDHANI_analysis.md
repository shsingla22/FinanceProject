# Mishra Dhatu Nigam (MIDHANI) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Mishra Dhatu Nigam (MIDHANI) is a government-owned "Miniratna" defence public sector company, set up in 1973 in Hyderabad to make inside India the special metals the country would otherwise have to import. It melts and processes very high-performance alloys — titanium alloys, nickel-, cobalt- and iron-based superalloys, maraging and armour-grade steels — and sells them mostly as raw material (bars, rods, plates, rings, sheets) to defence and space customers such as ISRO, DRDO, HAL and the armed forces, plus nuclear and power-sector programmes; a growing share also goes to overseas buyers and foreign engine makers it says it is in advanced talks with. It earns money by converting bought-in metals (nickel, cobalt, moly, tungsten, vanadium, chromium — none of which India has much of, so they are imported and price-volatile) into certified, quality-critical alloy products sold largely against a long order book, and increasingly by selling finished items such as engine parts, rings, fasteners and bulletproof jackets rather than plain material. Operations are centred on its Hyderabad (Kanchanbagh) plant with its melting, forging and heat-treatment facilities; FY26 turnover was a record INR 1,208.63 crores with PAT of INR 130.79 crores and an order book of INR 2,290 crores as of 1 April 2026.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Titanium and titanium alloys** — Melting (vacuum-arc remelting furnaces) and supply of titanium alloys for aero engines, ISRO gas bottles and propellant tanks, and other critical uses — management says it is about 27%-29% of the order book (roughly INR 741 crores), the plant is running near full established capacity, and FY26 titanium production hit a record ~700 tons, nearly double the prior year.
- **Superalloys (nickel-, cobalt- and iron-based)** — The company's flagship, hardest-to-make products, used in fighter-aircraft and aero-engine parts and rocket stages; in FY26 it got CEMILAC airworthiness certification for 10 cast and rod superalloys and NADCAP heat-treatment certification, and developed a single-crystal blade material for the first time — management calls superalloys and titanium the two things MIDHANI is known for.
- **Special steels, maraging steel and armour-grade steel** — Other special-steel grades sold alongside titanium and superalloys, including the 90 tons of armour-grade steel supplied for the Republic Day Presidential Dais; the calls treat this as a regular part of the revenue mix but give no separate size.
- **Armour products / Garuda bulletproof jackets** — Finished ballistic protection products for the armed forces — Garuda jackets contributed "INR 30-plus crores" in FY24 and, after design modifications, MIDHANI began bidding for lower-specification jacket requirements too; small relative to the metals business.
- **Aero-engine components and value-added items** — Machined and formed parts rather than raw metal — Adour engine parts for HAL's Jaguar aircraft (a repeat order of about INR 66 crores was mentioned in 2022), over 700 ring-rolled superalloy and titanium rings for aero engines in FY26, and a new INR 40 crore aerospace fastener (bolts) facility inaugurated in FY26.
- **Exports** — Overseas sales of the same alloys, helped by disruption in European supply chains; management flagged roughly INR 17-20 crores in one quarter of FY25, about 10% of that quarter's revenue, and describes exports as an area they want to keep growing.
- **NALCO joint venture (titanium project)** — A joint venture with NALCO that was still at the land-acquisition, clearance and consultant/equipment-tendering stage in 2022, with no revenue contribution described in the calls.
- **Metal bank (raw material security)** — Not a revenue line but a new initiative: an MOU signed with major stakeholders in FY26 to stockpile six critical imported raw materials so defence projects and MIDHANI's own melting are not hit by supply-chain shocks.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

MIDHANI's core strength is that it makes materials almost nobody else in India can make, and that customers cannot easily switch away from. It is the sole domestic melter of the most critical alloys — the stock for single-crystal turbine blades is, in management's words, "melted only in MIDHANI" — and its material sits in every ISRO launch-vehicle stage, in Chandrayaan-3, and in flight-tested Kaveri engines. Buyers there are paying for certified reliability rather than the lowest price. On top of that, every material has to be individually qualified and certified (CEMILAC, NADCAP, RDSO, OEM audits that run for years), so once a grade is approved it tends to stay approved. The quality framework scores competitive advantage, customer benefits and industry structure positively, and the end markets are genuinely expanding — defence indigenisation, aero engines, space launches, advanced thermal and small modular nuclear — with the order book rising from about ₹1,300cr in 2022 to roughly ₹2,290cr by April 2026. This is exactly why the 'Cost to Replicate' pattern is the one clear STRONG FIT in the screen: a 1973 national mandate, 40-plus years of qualified grades written into ISRO/DRDO/HAL platform specifications, and organisation-level certifications that a newcomer would need years to accumulate.

the same qualities that build the moat also weigh on the economics. Being the certified specialist means being asset-heavy and slow-moving: every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.38 of sales, roughly 1,022 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and every ₹100 of capital in the business generated just ₹8 of operating cash this year. Inventory, work-in-progress and scrap bloat are flagged repeatedly. Return on capital and capital allocation are the two negative areas in the quality framework, and they are the direct financial cost of the qualification-heavy, order-book-driven model that the moat depends on. The screen reflects this too — because revenue is lumpy order execution with no licence, service or subscription stream, and because working capital is funded by the company rather than by customers, 'Recurring Revenue' is a clear NO FIT. Contracts are fixed-price with no escalation clause, so when nickel, molybdenum, cobalt, LPG or power costs rise, MIDHANI absorbs them. That single fact explains both the NO FIT on 'Pricing Power' and the ELEVATED cyclicality risk: margins fell from about 30% in FY22-23 to 18% in FY24 and about 14% in Q1FY25 before recovering in FY26. It is also why several patterns land only as PARTIAL — genuine institutional reputation, a steady drip of named innovations, real moves downstream into armour, plates, springs and fasteners — but none of them convert into a pricing or recurring-revenue advantage.

The risks cluster around who the customer is and where the metallurgy is easy. Demand is set by government budgets, DAC approvals and PSU tenders; FY26 turnover splits ₹603cr from PSUs and ₹301cr from defence, and results are repeatedly explained by a short list of named counterparties — HAL, ISRO, DRDO/GTRE, BrahMos, BEML — making government dependency HIGH RISK and stakeholder concentration ELEVATED. Exports are the diversification story but remain small at about ₹85cr in FY26 against a ₹100cr target, and are themselves government-linked. Indigenisation, the same policy that created the company's mandate, now works against it: technology transfers are putting private firms into the same tenders (a Delhi private firm holds the ABHED jacket transfer of technology, and management concedes 'multiple people' make single-crystal blades and that large armour orders get split), which is the New Entrants risk. And at the undemanding end of the product range, cheaper suppliers simply win — management says openly on Pinaka low-alloy steel that MIDHANI 'may not be able to compete', while specialty steel is the largest part of the business. Technological obsolescence is the one low risk: nothing in the record suggests a platform shift that would strand what MIDHANI has built. Taken together — strong moat, weak returns, no pricing power, concentrated government demand — the combined rating lands at 42 out of 100, a Mixed (2-star) verdict, with resilience assessed as STRAINED.

### Cost to Replicate — STRONG FIT
*"Could a newcomer copy this?"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** The replication barrier is concrete and repeatedly evidenced: a 1973-vintage national indigenisation mandate, 40+ years of qualified grades embedded in ISRO/DRDO/HAL platform specifications, multi-year organisation-level certifications (CEMILAC for 10 alloys, NADCAP, RDSO, ongoing GE/Safran audits requiring repeat visits over years), and sole domestic capability in single-crystal blade material melting — none of which a funded newcomer could rebuild quickly.

> "The raw material that goes into the manufacturing of single crystal blades is melted only in MIDHANI" — *management, on an earnings call*

*"Small cost, can't skip it"*

**What the calls show:** Strong certification/standards anchoring — CEMILAC airworthiness for 10 super alloys, NADCAP heat-treatment, RDSO for railway springs, GE/Safran/Pratt qualification running for years — and the alloy is a small but performance-critical input into engines and missiles; but toll-road economics are undercut by fixed-price L1 tendering and pass-through failure on nickel/cobalt/LPG that crushed margins from ~30% (FY22-23) to 14-18% (FY24-25).

> "nowadays whatever orders we are getting it will be on the fixed price only" — *management, on an earnings call*

### Corporate Culture — PARTIAL
*"A culture that compounds"*

## What to watch

- Working capital: whether the roughly 1,022-day cash cycle and the repeatedly flagged inventory, WIP and scrap bloat start shrinking — this is the main drag behind the negative Return on Capital and Asset Turn checks.
- Fixed-price contracts: any sign of escalation clauses or input-cost pass-through would directly address the 'Pricing Power' NO FIT and the ELEVATED cyclicality risk that together caused the FY22-FY25 margin slide.
- Customer mix: exports moving meaningfully beyond the ~₹85cr FY26 level toward and past the ₹100cr target, and progress on qualifying with GE Aerospace, Pratt & Whitney and Safran — the only visible relief for HIGH RISK government dependency and ELEVATED stakeholder concentration.
- Competition from transferred technology: whether private players holding DRDO/IIT transfers (for example the ABHED jacket) begin taking share in armour and other split orders, which would test the New Entrants risk against the 'Cost to Replicate' moat.
- Product mix drift: how much revenue comes from the commodity end where management concedes it cannot compete on price (Pinaka low-alloy steel) versus the certified premium end protected by CEMILAC, NADCAP and sole domestic melting — the balance that decides the 'Good Enough Goods' risk.
