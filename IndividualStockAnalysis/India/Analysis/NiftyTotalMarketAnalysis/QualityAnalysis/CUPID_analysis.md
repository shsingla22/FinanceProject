# Cupid (CUPID) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Cupid Limited is an Indian manufacturer, based at a factory in Sinnar, Nashik (Maharashtra), that makes male condoms, female condoms, water-based lubricant jelly and In Vitro Diagnostic (IVD) test kits. It earns almost all of its money from exports — 89% to 94% of sales in the quarters discussed — mainly by winning bulk tenders from government health programmes and international agencies such as the United Nations Population Fund (its largest customer at about INR57 crore in FY23), plus buyers in South Africa, Tanzania and Brazil; it says it has shipped to over 104 countries. Revenue was INR164.1 crore in FY23 with an order book of about INR177 crore, and the business runs at roughly 94-95% of a 560 million pieces-per-year condom capacity. Under new management (the Halwasiya group, which took control in 2023) it is also trying to build a consumer-brand (B2C) business in India and abroad, and raised about INR385 crore via warrants to FIIs in early 2024 largely for an acquisition.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Male condoms** — Latex male condoms sold largely into export tenders and institutional buyers; management guided to about 75% of revenue for FY24, making it the biggest single line.
- **Female condoms** — The company says it was the first in India to make female condoms; this is the high-margin line at roughly 23% of revenue, but it is lumpy — sales swing quarter to quarter on South African lab-testing delays and tender timing (only INR1 crore in Q3 FY24 versus an expected INR21 crore in Q4), and a US FDA filing was pending that could open the US market.
- **Water-based lubricant jelly** — A smaller product sold alongside condoms (INR10-15 crore expected in FY23 at 40-45% margins, about INR25 crore supplied to UNFPA in FY23), but UNFPA cancelled outstanding jelly orders in 2023 after a quality parameter deteriorated in transit and storage.
- **In Vitro Diagnostics (IVD) kits** — A newer diagnostics line of about 16-17 test kits (including HIV and malaria) licensed by the Drug Controller of India, still tiny — roughly INR90 lakh of sales in one quarter versus INR20 lakh the quarter before — with management saying the big export tender opportunity (a INR300-400 crore market) only opens once it has three years of manufacturing history plus WHO and CE certifications.
- **Domestic / B2C business** — A small home-market segment, about 6-11% of sales, that the new management wants to grow by adding a sales force and selling branded condoms and lubricant jellies through Indian retail channels.

### The story in depth

Cupid rates Mixed, 37 out of 100, built from 38 for business quality, 41 for multibagger fit and 32 for risk safety — the lowest of the three is risk, and that is the crux of the story. The business itself is not weak on the surface: Rs.33 of every Rs.100 of sales is operating profit, the only quality area scoring positive is return on capital, the end market is genuinely under-penetrated and growing (Indian family planning and sexual wellness described as at an infancy stage, with post-COVID acceleration and rising female condom demand), and resilience is sound because demand is functional, repeat-purchase and consumable rather than cyclical or fashion-driven. Three of the eight risk channels — cyclicality, shifting consumer preferences and fashion risk — come back low for exactly that reason. The problems are not with what Cupid sells; they are with who it sells to, and what management has done with the money.

The single trait that shapes almost everything is that 89–94% of revenue comes from business-to-business export tenders to governments and multilateral agencies. That one fact simultaneously produces both of the high-risk verdicts and most of the pattern failures. It makes government dependency high risk, with UNFPA alone at roughly Rs.57cr of Rs.164cr of FY23 sales, and it makes stakeholder concentration high risk, since that same buyer unilaterally cancelled already-placed lubricant-jelly orders and one South African counterparty's lab repeatedly collapsed female condom revenue. It is also why pricing power is a clear no fit — across seven calls there is not one discussion of raising price, with earnings instead swinging on latex costs, export incentives, currency and tender timing — and why recurring revenue is a no fit, with the order book falling from Rs.187cr in July 2022 to Rs.177cr in April 2023 to Rs.80cr by January 2024. The certification regime cuts the same way twice: WHO prequalification, CE marking, USFDA approval and the three-year manufacturing-experience rule are the one real barrier behind the partial scores on cost to replicate and toll roads, and they are the stated silver lining against good-enough-goods and new-entrant risk — but Cupid is the party gated by those standards rather than the one collecting the toll, and the same lab-and-approval machinery is what stalls its revenue. Brand strength is a likely fit on heritage and own-brand selling, yet it remains unmonetised precisely because the tender channel leaves no room to charge for a brand.

The second theme is management follow-through, and it explains why the strengths have not converted into growth. Revenue has been flat near Rs.160–164cr with quarters stuck around Rs.40cr, and even the FY24 guide was essentially a repeat of FY23 — so market share gainers is a no fit despite the runway, and the gain-in-market-share check scores negative. Capacity ran at 94–95% for years while expansion was deferred and a modest Rs.12–15cr debottlenecking was left undecided, which is the growth check on capex scoring negative and is also the operational reason share could not be captured. The forward-integration move into own-brand retail is a likely fit in direction but embryonic in size: roughly 1,000 outlets, advertising and promotion under 10% of spend, no brand ambassador, and management explicitly declining to spend until traction appears — the same reluctance that keeps the government-dependency silver lining at about 10% of sales. Innovation dominance and corporate culture both fail on the same evidence: the IVD programme guided at Rs.30–40cr, then Rs.10cr, and delivered Rs.40 lakh with a Rs.1.4cr loss, while USFDA female-condom approval slipped from six-to-nine months to one-to-one-and-a-half years. Capital allocation is the weakest quality area at -1.5: a record Rs.5 dividend, but a buyback promised call after call while Rs.84–125cr sat idle and the stock was cheap, and then a reversal into heavy dilution — Rs.385cr of warrants, about 15% dilution and roughly the entire market capitalisation of six to eight months earlier, for an undisclosed overseas acquisition, plus ESOPs struck at Rs.140. Working capital compounds it, with about 200 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers.
