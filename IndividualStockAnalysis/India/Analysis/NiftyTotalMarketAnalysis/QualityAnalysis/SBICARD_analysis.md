# SBI Cards (SBICARD) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

SBI Card (SBI Cards and Payment Services Limited) is an Indian company whose only business is issuing and running credit cards — it is described on the calls as "India's largest pure-play credit card player" and the country's second-largest credit card issuer, with about 18.6% share of cards in force and roughly 19.5% share of card spending as of Q1 FY27. It gives cards to retail consumers and to companies (corporate cards), sourcing customers through State Bank of India's banking channel ("Banca", ~46-47% of new accounts), the open market (~53-54%), and co-branded tie-ups with brands like BPCL, Tata Neu, Flipkart, IndiGo, PhonePe and Aditya Birla. It earns money two ways: fee and spend-based income from the money customers charge to cards (about ₹4.3 trillion of spends in FY26) plus annual/other fees, and interest on the balances customers carry — the loan book, called receivables, was about ₹58,269 crore in Q1 FY27, of which roughly 55% earns interest through revolving balances (~22%) and EMI plans. It operates only in India (headquartered in Gurugram, listed in Mumbai), funding itself with borrowings at a cost of funds around 6.6%, and its profitability swings mainly on credit costs — bad-debt losses — which ran as high as 8.5% in Q1 FY25 and improved to 6.5% by Q1 FY27, with FY26 profit after tax of ₹2,167 crore.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Cards-in-force / new account acquisition** — Signing up and maintaining credit card customers — the base grew from 14 million cards in Q1 FY23 to about 2.26 crore (22.6 million) in Q1 FY27, with over 1 million new accounts added in Q1 FY27 alone; this is the foundation everything else is built on.
- **Banca channel** — New cards sold through State Bank of India's branches and digital platforms (YONO, internet banking, and the newer 'SBI Card Sprint' digital onboarding), contributing roughly 42-47% of new accounts and described as a channel management wants to keep growing.
- **Open market and co-brand sourcing** — The other ~53-54% of new accounts, won directly in the market or through partner-branded cards; the BPCL SBI Card alone crossed 5 million cards, called one of India's largest fuel co-branded partnerships.
- **Retail spends (spend-based / fee income)** — The everyday purchases customers put on their cards — ₹3.54 trillion in FY26 and ₹94,033 crore in Q1 FY27 — on which the company earns fee and spend-based income; online spends are about 63% of retail spends and this is the largest and fastest-growing revenue driver.
- **Corporate spends (B2B)** — Cards used by companies for business expenses — a sizeable but volatile and low-margin line that management deliberately shrank (₹14,183 crore in Q1 FY23 fell to ₹5,249 crore in Q1 FY25 after they cut low-margin corporate accounts).
- **Receivables — revolver book** — Balances customers choose to carry over and pay interest on, at roughly 22% of the book in FY26 versus 26% back in Q1 FY23; it is the highest-yielding piece and management expects it to drift slightly lower.
- **Receivables — EMI book** — Purchases converted into fixed monthly instalments, about 35% of the mix in Q1 FY23 and growing 37% YoY in Q1 FY25; management explicitly says it will keep building this book as the revolver share softens.
- **RuPay credit card on UPI** — Letting cardholders pay via UPI QR codes at small merchants, launched after an RBI enabling change; it crossed ₹1,000 crore a month by FY25 and keeps growing ~10-13% a quarter, and is the main way the company reaches Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities.
- **Risk, underwriting and collections** — Not a revenue line but the function management spends the most airtime on — tightening who gets a card, cutting credit limits (5 lakh accounts reduced in one quarter of FY25), and expanding collections; it drove credit cost from 8.5% down to 6.5% and gross NPA to 2.04%, and is the single biggest swing factor in profits.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

SBI Cards earns its "Decent" rating (50 out of 100, built as 64% x 56 for business quality plus 36% x 40 for risk safety) on the back of reach rather than pricing. The quality framework's two strongest marks are distribution and convenience: the Banca channel plugs the company into the parent bank's branch network and depositor base, consistently sourcing 42-46% of new accounts and uniquely allowing it to lend to new-to-credit customers (20-30% of Banca sourcing) that open-market rivals cannot even see, while instant issuance on YONO and net banking, the Sprint digital onboarding platform and a top-rated app make signing up frictionless. The end market itself is expanding fast, with cards in force going from roughly 75 million to 121 million, industry spends reaching INR 23.62 trillion and digital transactions up eleven-fold between 2021 and 2025. These same traits are what let three of the multibagger patterns register at all, each as a partial fit: Friendly Middleman (branch staff recommending the card to existing depositors), Cost to Replicate (privileged access to branch and depositor data is the one genuinely hard-to-copy asset) and Forward Integrators (owning the digital acquisition channel). A fourth, Recurring Revenue, is partial because the installed base of 1.92 crore cards in 2024 rising to 2.26 crore by July 2026 does behave like an annuity through annual fees, interchange and interest on a 54-55% interest-earning book, even though management never describes it in renewal terms.

The awkward part of the story is that the assets producing the patterns are the same ones producing the risks. Reliance on the parent's branches is simultaneously the hardest-to-replicate advantage and the Stakeholder Concentration risk (rated Watch): a delay in SBI's own branch and Shikhar cycle by itself pushed Q1 FY23 sourcing below one million accounts, and new-to-credit customers come almost only through that door. The recent good news has the same double character. Market Share Gainers scores only a partial fit because cards-in-force share has sat flat at roughly 18.4-18.6% for four years, and the headline recovery in spends share from 15.9% in Q1 FY25 to 19.5% in Q1 FY27 was achieved by deliberately slowing acquisition during the credit stress -- the very same action that shows up as the Cyclicality risk playing out (gross credit cost running 5.6% in Q1 FY23, up to 8.5% in Q1 FY25, back to 6.5% by Q1 FY27, GNPA at 2.04% with six straight quarters of falling delinquencies). The framework reads that as a cycle just past its trough rather than a structural gain. Credit for handling it belongs on the culture side: management flagged the deterioration promptly and in detail in August 2024, cut limits on 5 lakh customers, and then delivered the improvement it promised -- though the tightening was reactive rather than ahead of the cycle, and the Corporate Culture pattern stays partial. Governance pulls the other way: leadership is deputed from the parent and rotates fast, with three managing directors and four chairmen in five years, which the framework marks negatively for independence and long-horizon ownership.

What is missing is any ability to set price, and this is where the strengths stop helping. Three patterns are outright no-fits. Pricing Power fails because management declined to lift the 3.5% monthly revolver rate in August 2024 and again resisted a move to 3.75-4% or a cut in rewards in May 2026, interchange is set by regulation, and spend-based fee yield has already fallen from about 1.6% pre-COVID. Low Price Plus fails from the other end: cost-to-income worsened from about 49% in Q1 FY25 to 55-58% guided for FY26/27, and as a non-bank issuer the company has no deposits and funds itself at 6.4-7.5% wholesale rates. Toll Roads fails because customers carry several competing cards and can simply switch to a cheaper personal loan. The risk review names the same forces: Government Dependency is High Risk because RBI circulars have already reset activation, over-limit and inactive-card rules and the November 2023 risk-weight increase landed directly on the business; Technological Innovation is High Risk because the payments rail is shifting under the card format itself, with UPI at roughly 80% of transaction volume and card relevance now depending on riding RuPay-on-UPI (growing 10-13% quarter on quarter) rather than plastic. Shifting Consumer Preferences and Good Enough Goods sit on Watch for the same reason growth is described as volume-led with an adverse mix: the profitable revolver share slid from 26% to 22% with a stated downward bias, interest-earning assets fell from 62% to about 55%, and the response has been to build the EMI and instalment book to about a third rather than to reprice. Only Fashion Risk is Low, since roughly half of customers transact every month across everyday categories. The quality scores line up with that reading -- customer benefits at 0.75 and growth at 0.6, but return on capital at 0.0, industry structure negative, and management barely positive -- and overall resilience is recorded as unknown.

## What to watch

- Whether spends share holds above 19.5% once acquisition is loosened again -- the Market Share Gainers pattern is partial precisely because share recovered while growth was throttled, not while it was running.
- Whether gross credit cost keeps falling from 6.5% or turns back up toward the 8.5% peak -- the Cyclicality risk and the just-past-the-trough reading in the Cyclical market growth check both rest on the improvement continuing.
- Any change to the revolver mix trend below 22% or to interest-earning assets below roughly 55% -- Shifting Consumer Preferences plus the Pricing Power no-fit means the company cannot offset a further mix slide by raising the 3.5% rate.
- Cost-to-income landing outside the 55-58% guided range, given the Low Price Plus no-fit and wholesale funding at 6.4-7.5% with no deposit base to lean on.
- Any interruption to Banca sourcing away from the 42-46% band, or a further RBI rule change on card economics -- the Stakeholder Concentration and Government Dependency risks hit the two things the company does not control.
- Continued rapid turnover of managing directors and chairmen deputed from the parent, which is what drives the negative mark on independent, long-term stewardship.
