# Jindal Stainless Ltd. (JSL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: METALS*

Jindal Stainless Limited (JSL) makes stainless steel — it melts it from scratch and then rolls and finishes it into hot- and cold-rolled sheet, coil and other flat products, which it sells mostly to Indian industrial buyers and fabricators. Its customers are sectors like automobiles, ornamental and industrial pipe & tube, railways (coaches, wagons, Vande Bharat, metro cars), lifts and elevators, white goods and infrastructure projects such as foot-over-bridges and railway cable trays, plus exports to Europe, Japan, Korea, Taiwan and other markets (exports were roughly a fifth to a quarter of sales in FY23 and have since been deliberately dialled back in favour of the domestic market). It earns money on the spread between raw-material cost (nickel prices swing the margin a lot) and selling price, measured by management as EBITDA per tonne — guided at roughly Rs 18,000–20,000 a tonne — multiplied by volume; FY26 deliveries were 2.57 million tonnes, consolidated EBITDA Rs 5,560 crore and profit Rs 3,185 crore, with net debt down to Rs 3,040 crore. Its main plants are at Jajpur (Odisha) and Hisar (Haryana), with cold rolling also at Mundra (Gujarat) and Kharagpur, total melting capacity of 4.2 million tonnes a year including 3 million in India and a newly commissioned 1.2 million-tonne melt shop in Indonesia, and a stated target of 3.5 million tonnes of sales by FY29.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Core stainless steel manufacturing (Jajpur and Hisar, India)** — The main business: melting stainless steel and rolling it into hot-rolled and cold-rolled products for domestic and export customers — this is essentially all of the roughly 2.57 million tonnes sold in FY26 and the bulk of group profit.
- **Downstream / value-added capacity (HRAP, CRAP, cold rolling)** — The finishing lines that turn crude steel into higher-priced processed sheet and coil, being expanded with a 1.1 million-tonne HRAP and 0.17 million-tonne CRAP line at Jajpur plus an extra Rs 900 crore for cold rolling at Hisar and Kharagpur, taking CRAP capacity to 2.67 million tonnes by FY28.
- **Chromeni Steels Private Limited** — A 0.6 million-tonne-a-year cold rolling mill at Mundra, Gujarat, bought outright for over Rs 1,600 crore in FY25 and described in FY26 as ramping up with rising capacity utilisation.
- **Indonesia melt shop (SMS) and NPI** — A 1.2 million-tonne-a-year stainless steel melt shop in Indonesia commissioned ahead of schedule, plus a nickel pig iron (NPI) operation that is ramping up — this is the overseas raw-material and melting leg of the business.
- **Jindal United Steel Limited (JUSL)** — A manufacturing facility taken in as a wholly-owned subsidiary in FY23 so that, as management put it, all critical stainless steel facilities sit under one umbrella with better synergies.
- **Jindal Stainless Steelways** — The service-centre subsidiary that processes and sells steel, including direct sales to vehicle and equipment makers (OEMs); one of the smaller earnings contributors within total subsidiary EBITDA of Rs 208 crore in Q1 FY25.
- **Iberjindal ("Iber"), Spain** — The overseas trading/distribution arm serving European markets, whose results swing with inventory levels and European stainless prices.
- **Rathi and Rabirun** — Two smaller acquired operations being ramped up from FY25 — Rathi was guided at a thinner margin of about Rs 4,000–6,000 EBITDA per tonne, well below the core business, and Rabirun's plan was still being firmed up.
- **Brand and channel programmes (Jindal Saathi, Jindal Infinity)** — Not a separate profit centre but a demand-creation effort — co-branding, QR-code authentication and loyalty schemes for pipe-and-tube fabricators and retailers, now extended into consumer advertising with a brand ambassador and an IPL team tie-up, aimed at defending market share against cheap imports and counterfeits.
- **Captive and renewable power** — Supporting infrastructure rather than a business line: renewable sources had reached 56% of imported power at Jajpur and Hisar by Q3 FY26, with a 315 MW solar-wind hybrid project partially commissioned in Q4 FY26.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Jindal Stainless is best understood as a company that has done nearly everything well inside a business that structurally resists doing well. The quality work scores it strongest exactly where management controls its own actions: competitive advantage, capital allocation and the ability to keep building. Growth capex has been continuous and self-funded — the Jajpur 1mt expansion, JUSL rolling capacity to 3.6mt, the Chromeni 0.6mt cold-rolling mill, a 1.2mt Indonesian melting shop commissioned ahead of schedule and a further INR900cr of cold-rolling — all while net debt to EBITDA fell from 0.8x in FY23 to 0.55x in FY26 and debt-to-equity sits at 0.15. Acquisitions were bolt-ons that deepened the same business rather than diversifying away from it (JUSL, Chromeni, Rathi, Rabirun), and the promised numbers arrived: EBITDA per ton guided at INR18,000-20,000 back in August 2022 was held and beaten at an average of 21,300 in the first nine months of FY26. That record of saying and then doing is why the Corporate Culture pattern is the single strongest fit in the screen, and it is the same evidence behind the high marks for long-term independent thinking and for expansion continued straight through the FY23 export-duty downturn. The two areas that score worst — return on capital and persistence of growth, where sales have averaged about 18% a year but arrive lumpily — are the fingerprints of the industry, not of the management team.

The patterns and the risks are, in several places, the same fact seen from two sides. The company sells through fabricators, pipe and tube makers and retailers, and invests in them through the Jindal Saathi co-branding scheme, now in its fifth year with roughly 35 partners, plus loyalty programmes and 155-hour fabrication courses run through government institutes. That earns a partial fit on the Friendly Middleman pattern and a strong score on distribution reach that neither domestic rivals nor importers can match. But those same intermediaries are the channel through which the biggest threat arrives: the Good Enough Goods risk is rated elevated precisely because fabricators and small intermediaries buy cheap imported stainless on price and the end customer cannot tell the difference — which is why the company built QR-code authentication and the Saathi Seal in the first place, and why FY26 brought a first-ever brand ambassador, the Jindal Infinity campaign and a cricket tie-up. The brand push is a defence dressed as marketing. The same doubling holds for scale: management points to integration and cost competitiveness as the reason it holds share, and the mix has genuinely moved up, with hot-rolled to cold-rolled shifting from 40:60 to 30:70 over three years and cold-rolled capacity heading to 2.67mt by FY28. Yet the screen rates Cost to Replicate a clear no-fit, because Chinese and Indonesian mills have already replicated that scale and undercut it, holding 30-50% of the Indian market throughout the period. The advantage is real inside India and absent at the border.

What the company cannot manufacture is pricing power, and this is where the screen and the risk review agree most bluntly. Realisations are an explicit pass-through of nickel and ferrochrome prices — blended selling prices fell about 4-4.5% in the third quarter of FY26, and in May 2026 management openly conceded it could not pass on a sharp fuel and ammonia cost increase because importers' costs had not risen. Pricing Power, Toll Roads and Recurring Revenue are all no-fits: stainless is the dominant input cost for the customer, not a small critical ingredient, and revenue is tonnes shipped at resetting prices with nothing contracted or renewing behind it. That feeds directly into the two high risks. Cyclicality is high because volumes and margins swing with the commodity cycle, as the August 2022 destocking episode and the deliberate 5-10% volume cut showed. Government dependency is high because the swing factor is often policy rather than operations: a 15% export duty in FY23 hit 70-80% of the export book, and by FY26 management is publicly asking for the quality control order to be reinstated along with anti-dumping and safeguard measures. The offsets are genuine rather than cosmetic — demand is spread across autos, railways and metro, lifts, white goods and pipe and tube with no single customer of consequence, Indian stainless consumption is expanding off a low base, buyers are shifting from lowest-price decisions toward whole-life cost, and the low debt means a bad year is survivable rather than existential, which is why the overall resilience reads as sound. The 52-out-of-100 rating is the honest arithmetic of that split: a well-run, well-financed, share-gaining operator scoring 62 on business quality, 48 on multibagger characteristics and only 40 on risk safety.

### Corporate Culture — STRONG FIT

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Stainless steel is a commodity business where management repeatedly states realizations are governed by nickel/ferrochrome movements, and volume/margin guidance swings with the cycle; the Aug 2022 call describes destocking and a 5-10% volume cut, while capacity is being expanded aggressively (Jajpur, JUSL 1.6→3.6mt, Chromeni, Indonesia 1.2mt SMS, 3.5mt sales target by FY29) — classic expansionary over-investment exposure. > "normally in the stainless steel, the prices are governed by the movement in the raw material prices" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Demand is largely flow/operations-linked across autos, railways, metro, pipe & tube, white goods rather than a single customer's capex; net debt/EBITDA of 0.55x and countercyclical capacity building through the 2022 downturn show balance-sheet strength.*

## What to watch

- EBITDA per ton against the 21,300 nine-month FY26 average — the Corporate Culture fit rests on delivering guided per-ton profit, and slippage there is the first sign the mix shift is being competed away.
- Import share, currently described as steadily above 30% and once near 50% — this is the direct read on both the Cost to Replicate no-fit and the elevated New Entrants risk.
- Whether the quality control order is reinstated and anti-dumping or safeguard duties are granted — the high Government Dependency rating turns on outcomes the company can only lobby for, not control.
- Whether the FY26 consumer brand spend (Ranveer Singh, the Infinity campaign, the cricket tie-up) and the QR authentication programme actually change what fabricators buy — that is the only live defence against the elevated Good Enough Goods risk, and the Brand Strength pattern stays partial until it shows up in realisations.
- Net debt to EBITDA against the 0.55x level as capex continues toward the 3.5mt FY29 target — the low leverage is what makes the high Cyclicality risk survivable, and it is the single assumption doing the most work in the sound resilience call.
