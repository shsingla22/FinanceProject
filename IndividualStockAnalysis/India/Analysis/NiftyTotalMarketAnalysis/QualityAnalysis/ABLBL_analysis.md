# A B Lifestyle (ABLBL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aditya Birla Lifestyle Brands Limited (ABLBL) is an India-based western-wear fashion company that designs and sells branded clothing and footwear — men's and women's formal and casual apparel, innerwear, and sportswear — under owned and licensed labels such as Louis Philippe, Van Heusen, Allen Solly, Peter England, Reebok and American Eagle. It became a separately listed company on 1 May 2025 after being demerged, and trades on the BSE and NSE as ABLBL. It makes money by selling to Indian consumers through its own branded stores (about 3,348 stores covering 4.9 million square feet across nearly 800 cities and towns), through department stores and multi-brand trade partners, and through e-commerce; a small additional slice comes from contract manufacturing of garments exported to brands abroad. In FY26 it reported revenue of INR 8,396 crores, EBITDA of INR 1,429 crores (17% margin) and reported profit after tax of INR 171 crores, with net debt of INR 726 crores at year-end.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Lifestyle Brands (also called the Power brands)** — The core, much larger part of the company — the four established apparel brands Louis Philippe, Van Heusen, Allen Solly and Peter England (including the small-town Peter England Red format) — with FY26 revenue of INR 7,154 crores, an EBITDA margin of 19.6%, and 8% full-year like-for-like sales growth, making it roughly 85% of total revenue.
- **Emerging Businesses** — The newer, smaller growth portfolio of Reebok, American Eagle and Van Heusen Innerwear, running over 390 stores and growing 18% in the March 2026 quarter; it is still less profitable but losses are shrinking fast (Van Heusen Innerwear targeting break-even by Q4 of the current year) and management expects this portfolio to be about one-fourth of the whole company in 4-5 years.
- **Retail (own stores)** — The company's own exclusive-brand store network is described as the main engine of growth — management adds stores every year (300-plus added in FY26, a pipeline of about 120 more planned for the next year) and tracks 'like-to-like' growth of existing stores, which ran 5-15% across the four calls.
- **Trade / departmental stores (wholesale)** — Selling through multi-brand outlets and department-store chains, which management calls a 'fairly robust' business split roughly 60-40 between department stores and multi-brand outlets, and which returned to double-digit growth by the December 2025 and March 2026 quarters.
- **E-commerce** — Online selling, which went through a deliberate correction of heavy discounting to fix profitability and shrank for several quarters, then recovered to double-digit growth by the December 2025 quarter.
- **Contract manufacturing (exports)** — A non-branded side business that makes garments for other brands around the world; it sits in the 'others' line of Lifestyle Brands, was about INR 127 crores in the March 2026 quarter, and grew strongly after a new large factory added capacity.
- **Forever 21 (discontinued)** — A brand the company closed during FY25; it no longer contributes revenue and its removal from the base depressed reported growth in the Emerging Businesses segment during FY26.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

A B Lifestyle earns its "Decent" 57-out-of-100 rating on the strength of what it already owns rather than on what it might become. The returns are the best part of the record: for every ₹100 of capital in the business it produced ₹28 of operating cash this year, operating profit runs at ₹16 on every ₹100 of sales with margins barely moving year to year, and after direct input costs the company keeps ₹60 of every ₹100 customers pay. Behind those numbers sits one asset that shows up in almost every part of the analysis — a 3,348-store, 4.9 million square foot network across nearly 800 towns, backed by decades-old brands like Louis Philippe, Van Heusen, Allen Solly and Peter England. That network scores 2 out of 2 on distribution advantage, and it is what makes the Forward Integrators pattern a strong fit: this is a brand owner that has taken ownership all the way to the shop floor, holding its own inventory on consignment instead of handing the customer relationship to someone else. Capital allocation looks deliberate too — capex stepped up from ₹150-225cr historically to ₹320-330cr in FY26 specifically to open 300-plus new stores, with about ₹300cr guided again for FY27, alongside a stated 15-25% profit payout policy, a first ₹0.50 dividend and ₹726cr of net cash still on hand.

The same traits that produce the strengths also produce most of the risks, and this is where the story gets less comfortable. Brand strength is a strong-fit pattern, but it rests on advertising that has to be bought again every year — spend rose from 3.3% of sales in FY25 to 5.5% in the June 2026 quarter, including IPL sponsorship, and it cost real money in the short run, with profit at ₹24cr against ₹54cr on a comparable basis. That is exactly the mechanism behind the fashion risk flagged in the review: demand rests on taste, and salience is rented, not owned. Similarly, the store network that creates the barrier to entry is also what makes the company more exposed to a weak discretionary spending cycle, and the review notes the company is spending into that weakness on purpose while wedding and festive calendars swing results around. Most telling, the brands do not translate into the ability to raise prices — across four quarters of calls there was no example of a price increase that stuck without losing volume, margin gains came from spreading fixed costs and product mix instead, gross margin was flat to slightly worse, and the GST change was described as a small net negative. So Pricing Power is scored as no fit despite the brand story, and the competitive advantage area of the quality framework comes out at zero.

The clearest gap is between what management claims about winning share and what the numbers show. The Market Share Gainers pattern is only a partial fit: management points to six straight quarters of positive same-store sales and calls its growth industry-leading, but when analysts put its roughly 8% growth next to a competitor's roughly 13% over nine months, no clear answer came back, and retail growth was said to lag by 500-600 basis points. In innerwear the pressure is more concrete — cheaper mass-market rivals grew 12% and 19% while the company managed only marginal growth, which is precisely the "good enough goods" risk of a premium player losing ground to adequate substitutes, made slightly worse by GST raising prices on items above ₹2,500. The industry structure score is negative for this reason. Against that, the response is visible and partly working: the emerging portfolio of Reebok and American Eagle is growing 13-18% with losses halving, Reebok itself has gone from ₹200cr to over ₹500cr in sales and from 90 to 210-plus stores. But that growth engine is licensed rather than owned, which is the stakeholder concentration risk — about 85% of revenue is safely in owned brands, while the fastest-growing part depends on licences, and one licence, Forever 21, has already closed. Overall the risk picture is sound: seven of eight risks sit at watch rather than serious, technology disruption is low, and demand is repeat-purchase consumer spending rather than someone else's capital budget. This is a steady, cash-generating retailer with a real physical moat and honest self-assessment from management, but without the pricing power or demonstrated share gains that would lift it above decent.
