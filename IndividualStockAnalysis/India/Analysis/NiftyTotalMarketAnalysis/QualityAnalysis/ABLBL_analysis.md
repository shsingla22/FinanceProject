# A B Lifestyle (ABLBL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

ABLBL (Aditya Birla Lifestyle Brands Limited) is an Indian branded-apparel company that sells western-style clothing, innerwear and sportswear to everyday Indian shoppers under names like Louis Philippe, Van Heusen, Allen Solly, Peter England, Reebok and American Eagle. It makes money by selling this merchandise through its own network of 3,348 stores covering 4.9 million square feet across nearly 800 Indian cities and towns, plus wholesale trade channels (multi-brand outlets and department stores, roughly a 60-40 split), e-commerce, and a small contract-manufacturing business that exports garments to other brands worldwide. Growth comes from two levers management repeats constantly: opening more stores (300+ added in FY26) and getting more sales out of existing ones (like-to-like growth). It became a separately listed company on 1 May 2025 after being demerged, and in FY26 posted revenue of INR 8,396 crore, EBITDA of INR 1,429 crore (17% margin) and reported profit of INR 171 crore, with net debt of INR 726 crore and a maiden dividend of 50 paise per share.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Lifestyle Brands** — The core and by far the largest part — the four established menswear/apparel brands Louis Philippe, Van Heusen, Allen Solly and Peter England (management calls them the 'Power brands'), which brought in INR 7,154 crore of FY26 revenue (roughly 85% of the company) at a ~19.6% EBITDA margin and grew high-single-digit for the year.
- **Emerging Businesses** — The smaller, faster-growing portfolio of Reebok, American Eagle and Van Heusen Innerwear, spanning 390+ stores and growing 18% in Q4 FY26; it is currently loss-light but improving fast, and management expects it to be about one-fourth of the whole company in 4-5 years.
- **Reebok** — The sportswear brand inside Emerging Businesses, acquired at some point before these calls with 100 stores and since expanded to 210+ stores with sales more than doubled; it grew 20%+ in Q3 and 30% in Q4 FY26 and management plans another 40-50 stores a year.
- **American Eagle** — A casual/denim brand in the Emerging portfolio that management describes as steadily and profitably growing, with double-digit like-to-like growth in recent quarters; no separate revenue figure is given in the calls.
- **Van Heusen Innerwear** — The innerwear line inside Emerging Businesses, which had been loss-making but cut losses sharply and returned to double-digit growth, with management targeting a break-even quarter by Q4 of the current fiscal year.
- **Peter England Red** — A small-town store format under the Peter England brand that management says returned to significant double-digit growth on the back of strong small-town demand.
- **Contract manufacturing / exports** — A side business that manufactures garments for other brands around the world and shows up in the 'others' line of the Lifestyle Brands channel mix (about INR 127 crore in a quarter); it had a good year after a new large factory added capacity.
- **Forever 21** — A brand that was closed during FY25 and is repeatedly cited only as a drag on the prior-year comparison base for Emerging Businesses — excluding it, Q3 FY26 segment growth was 19% rather than 13%.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

A B Lifestyle earns a middling overall grade of 59 out of 100 — "Decent," three stars — built from a business-quality score of 60, a multibagger fit of 46, and a comparatively high risk-safety score of 72, with resilience judged sound. The best of the company sits in what it earns and what it offers customers: out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹16 is operating profit, and every ₹100 of capital in the business threw off ₹28 of operating cash this year. After direct input costs the company keeps about ₹60 of every ₹100 of sales, a roughly 59% gross margin that reflects brands customers are willing to pay up for. Those brands — Louis Philippe, Van Heusen, Allen Solly, Peter England — are decades old and are the reason the Brand Strength pattern is a strong fit. The other strong-fit pattern, Forward Integration, is really the same asset seen from the other side: 3,348 stores and 4.9 million square feet across roughly 800 towns, all channels joined up, and privileged access to new mall space through developer partners such as Phoenix. That network is the single highest-scoring quality check in the whole framework, and it is also what management is spending against — capex deliberately stepped up from a ₹150–225 crore maintenance run-rate to ₹325 crore in FY26 for 300-plus new stores, with a first-year dividend and a stated 15–25% payout policy alongside.

The trouble is that the same traits that generate the patterns also generate the risks, almost one for one. Brand strength has to be continuously rented rather than owned: advertising and promotion jumped from 3.3% of sales in FY25 to 5.5% in the first quarter of FY26 on an IPL sponsorship, which coincided with 15% like-for-like retail growth and better brand recall — but the spend was lumpy and management has guided it back down, which is exactly the Fashion Risk finding that relevance in this business must be bought again each season. The premium positioning is why the Low Price Plus pattern is explicitly no fit and why Pricing Power looks likely — discount correction and wedding and premium upgrades pushed the Lifestyle margin to a four-year high of 20.6% — yet gross margin was essentially flat (input costs moved only from 41.1% to 41.4% of sales), so the gain came from selling less cheaply rather than from a widening structural advantage. And the heavier tilt toward wedding and formal wear is named as the direct reason growth lags peers. That shows up as the one clearly negative quality check: full-year revenue grew 7% and Lifestyle 8%, which analysts twice flagged as 500–600 basis points below mid-premium rivals such as Arvind Fashions. Share is being donated, not taken — which is why Market Share Gainers only lands as a partial fit despite a very large store base.

This pattern repeats in the weakest scores. Industry structure and competitive advantage are the only negative areas in the framework, and they line up with the risk review: every risk channel examined came back at "watch" rather than clear. Cost to Replicate is only partial because the barrier is a physical network and landlord relationships rather than anything that stops a competitor from entering apparel. The New Entrants concern is that e-commerce is a channel the company had to discipline rather than one it controls, leaving full-year online sales roughly flat after the rationalisation. The Good Enough Goods concern is sharper: mass innerwear brands Dollar and Lux grew 12–19% while the company's premium innerwear grew only marginally, and management was asked outright whether shoppers were trading down. Meanwhile, the growth engine meant to become a quarter of the company in four to five years rests on two licensed foreign brands, Reebok and American Eagle, whose economics the company does not ultimately own — the Reebok bolt-on has been scaled well, from 90–100 stores to over 210 and roughly ₹200 crore to about ₹500 crore-plus of sales, but the entry was weak and BIS licensing acted as a "speed breaker," which is also the government-dependency finding that policy moves costs and mix without setting revenue. So the honest read is a genuinely high-return, well-branded retail machine that is currently growing slower than its peers, spending more to defend relevance, leaning on borrowed brands for its next leg, and carrying ₹726 crore of net debt while it does so.
