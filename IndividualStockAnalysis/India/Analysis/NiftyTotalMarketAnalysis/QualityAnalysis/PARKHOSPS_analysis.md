# Park Medi World (PARKHOSPS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Park Medi World Limited (brand name "Park Hospital") runs a chain of private multi-super-specialty hospitals in northern India, treating both admitted (in-patient) and walk-in (out-patient) patients across roughly 30 medical specialties. It earns money by charging for hospital stays, surgeries, procedures, diagnostics and consultations — with about 80% of billings coming through government health schemes such as CGHS, ECHS, railways and state schemes, plus TPA/insurance and cash-paying patients — so its revenue is essentially beds occupied multiplied by average revenue per occupied bed per day (about INR 28,000 in FY26 at roughly 64% occupancy). It positions itself as affordable and high-volume rather than premium, keeping build cost near INR 35 lakh per bed and using 250-350 bed hospitals clustered densely within a few states. As of the May 2026 call it operated 16 hospitals with 3,960 beds across Haryana, Punjab, Delhi-NCR, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh, reporting FY26 revenue of INR 1,679 crores, EBITDA of INR 444 crores (26% margin) and profit of INR 274 crores, with almost no debt (INR 28 crores of term loans).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Hospital operations / network of multi-super-specialty hospitals** — The core and effectively only business — 16 owned hospitals (3,960 beds, ~870 ICU beds, 1,200+ doctors and 2,400+ nurses) that served about 8.7 lakh patients in FY26 (95,525 in-patients and 7.78 lakh out-patients) and generate all of the company's INR 1,679 crore revenue.
- **High-end tertiary and quaternary care (case mix)** — The higher-acuity specialties — cardiology, oncology, neurology, orthopedics/joint replacement, urology and gastroenterology, including robotic surgery on three da Vinci 5th-generation robots — which management says produced about 56.9% of FY26 revenue, up 316 basis points year-on-year, and is the main lever pushing ARPOB from INR 26,200 to INR 28,000.
- **Diagnostics and laboratories** — In-house labs supporting the hospitals, of which eight carry NABL accreditation with five more in the accreditation pipeline; the calls treat this as a quality/support function rather than a separately sized revenue stream.
- **Acquisitions (Brownfield) — buying and turning around existing hospitals** — A major growth engine rather than a separate segment: about INR 1,000 crores deployed on acquisitions to date (KPS Institute of Medical Sciences in Agra, Krishna Super Speciality in Bathinda, a 200-bed Narela unit bought through insolvency proceedings), and management says acquired assets contributed roughly 61% of FY26 revenue.
- **Greenfield builds — new hospitals from scratch** — The other growth engine, such as the 350-bed Panchkula hospital built for INR 125 crores and commissioned in April 2026; these cost money for the first 12-15 months before breaking even, versus acquired units that turn profitable much faster.
- **Expansion pipeline** — About 1,500 beds under execution — including Kanpur, Delhi, Gorakhpur, an oncology-dedicated Ambala extension, Rohtak and a 150-bed Mohali expansion — meant to take capacity from 3,960 to 5,460 beds by March 2028, fully funded from internal cash flow of INR 329 crores a year.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Park Medi World runs 16 hospitals and 3,610 beds aimed at the affordable end of the north Indian market, and almost everything good about it traces back to one decision: build and run beds more cheaply than anyone else. Capacity was added at INR34-35 lakh per bed, which the record notes is less than half what a competitor spends, and the FY26 outlay of INR430cr for 610 beds (a 20% increase in capacity) is clearly money spent to grow rather than to keep the lights on, earning 18% return on capital and 20% on equity. Growth has been paid for out of the business itself — INR329cr of operating cash flow — while term debt was cut from INR450cr to INR28cr, and ten of the hospitals were bought out of distress after screening 50-60 loss-making propositions. The company hires salaried, full-time doctors at five to eight years' experience rather than celebrity consultants, holds 63 government-awarded DNB teaching seats, reports industry-low consultant attrition, and only started talking to investors after two decades. The end market is expanding on its own: northern states are short of beds, and government health spending is targeted to rise from 3.8% to 5.5% of GDP by 2030. On the framework's own scoring, this shows up as strength in capital allocation (1.67) and management (1.33), and weakness in return on capital (0.33) and competitive advantage (0.50).

The multibagger screen reads the same cost obsession in two ways at once. It is the clearest reason two patterns fit — Low Price Plus, evidenced by the per-bed capex, the 30% ICU / 40% general ward layout that fits more beds into less space, and salaried doctors; and, alongside it, Innovation Dominance, because the savings are recycled into three fifth-generation da Vinci robots, 150 robotic kidney transplants, 2,600 robot-assisted joint replacements and a 316bps rise in high-end case mix. Volume growth is real and not bought: IPD up 18%, OPD up 22%, footfall up 24%, occupancy up 244bps even while adding a fifth more beds, with Q4 EBITDA at 28% (up 268bps) and debtor days falling from 161 to 129 — but the screen only rates Market Share Gainers a partial fit because no explicit share percentage is given. The same low-cost trait also caps what the screen will credit. Cost to Replicate is only partial, because the barrier management describes — sub-INR35 lakh capex per bed, tolerance of a four-to-five-month government receivable cycle, and cluster density — is an operating-cost advantage rather than a structural one. Brand Strength is partial: the company is the largest private chain in Haryana and the Tri-City and second-largest in North India and has served 8.7 lakh patients, yet by design it spends nothing on advertising and cultivates no premium image. And Pricing Power is a clear non-fit, because management refuses to raise prices at all: realisation improves only when the CGHS regulator revises its tariff, and an analyst noted ARPOB growing 5-7% against roughly 8% cost inflation. That is also why the profit margin check scores only 1 — 26 paise of operating profit per rupee of sales, but with swings of about plus or minus 5.6 points a year, a sign that key costs and prices are set outside the company.

The risk review lands on the same point from the other side, and it is worth being explicit that the top strengths and the top risks are the same facts viewed differently. Roughly 80-83% of revenue comes from government insurance schemes, about 93% of that central government, so the regulator sets both the price and the payment cycle — this is rated HIGH RISK for government dependency and ELEVATED for stakeholder concentration, since a single payer relationship drives the majority of revenue and analysts pressed on receivables and disallowed claims on both calls. Being deliberately the cheap option (ARPOB around INR28,000, with management declining to push it up) is what makes the Low Price Plus pattern fit, and it is also why Good Enough Goods is ELEVATED: there is no premium to defend and the price is not set by the brand. The offsets are the same items that appear as strengths — payer mix broadening from 89-11 to 80-20 with a stated path to 70-30, debtor days down to 129, accreditation, 30% ICU beds, robotics and transplants, and 56.9% of revenue from high-acuity work a small local hospital cannot do. Five of the eight risk channels come back LOW: demand is medical necessity rather than taste or cycle, footfall and occupancy grew through the period, expansion is funded from cash with only INR28cr of term debt, technology here is incremental and self-funded, entry is blocked by the same cost-and-receivables tolerance, and the one consumer shift discussed — insurance penetration rising and affordable-segment patients moving toward Park — runs in the company's favour. Overall resilience is judged SOUND, and the combined rating is 59 out of 100, or Decent: 71 for business quality, 41 for multibagger fit and 60 for risk safety. In short, a well-run, self-funded, genuinely cheap operator with a long runway, whose main limitation is that its economics are set by a payer it does not control.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Roughly 80-83% of revenue comes from government insurance schemes, of which ~93% is central government (CGHS/ECHS/railways/paramilitary), so tariffs, rate revisions and claim-processing rules set by the regulator directly determine both pricing and the working-capital cycle; management explicitly models FY27 revenue uplift off a CGHS rate hike, and cancer-drug pricing was administratively pushed to below 30% of MRP, compressing that line to 'minimal profits'.
