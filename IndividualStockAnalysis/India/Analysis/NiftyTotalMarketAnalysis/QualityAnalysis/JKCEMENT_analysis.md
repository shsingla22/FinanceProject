# J.K. Cement Ltd. (JKCEMENT) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: CEMENT & CEMENT PRODUCTS*

JK Cement Limited is an Indian cement maker that manufactures and sells grey cement (its main product), white cement, and wall putty, branded and sold mainly through dealers to construction buyers, with a smaller "non-trade" (bulk/institutional) portion. It earns money by mining limestone, burning it into clinker at integrated plants, grinding it into cement at plants and split grinding units, and selling it by the bag — so profits swing with cement prices per bag, fuel/petcoke and diesel costs, and freight (lead distance, rail vs road mix). Its plants and markets are spread across North India (Rajasthan, Haryana, UP, Punjab), Central India (MP, UP), the South (Karnataka), the West (Gujarat) and now the East (Bihar), with some white cement also supplied from plants in the UAE. It is in a long capacity build-out — roughly 24 million tonnes in 2024 heading to 30, then a stated target of 40 million tonnes by FY28 and 50 million by FY30 — funded partly by debt (gross debt about INR5,551 crores as of June 2026); full-year FY26 net sales were about INR12,568 crores with EBITDA of INR2,318 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Grey cement** — The core business — ordinary construction cement made at integrated clinker plants and grinding units and sold by the bag across North, Central, South, West and East India; it drives almost all volume and revenue (volumes grew 19% year-on-year in the June 2026 quarter, mostly from the new Central India and Bihar capacity), and management talks about it in per-tonne terms (EBITDA of INR982 per tonne in Q1 FY27).
- **White cement** — A smaller, separate and evidently higher-value product line supplied from domestic plants plus plants in the UAE, with volumes growing 11% year-on-year in Q1 FY27; management said domestic production can now meet the whole domestic demand after UAE supply into South India was disrupted.
- **Wall putty** — A finishing/coating product ("JK Cement WallMaxx"-type white cement wall putty) treated as part of the white segment; the company is adding a 6 lakh tonne (0.6 million tonne) putty plant at Nathdwara, Rajasthan, expected to commission around September 2026.
- **Capacity expansion projects** — Not a product but a constant management theme — greenfield and brownfield clinker and grinding units (Panna, Prayagraj, Hamirpur, Ujjain, Buxar in Bihar, Muddapur in Karnataka, and a new 7 million tonne Jaisalmer project with grinding at Bikaner and Punjab costing about INR3,630 crores for the integrated unit) that determine future volumes, capex and debt levels.
- **Government incentive income** — A recurring earnings item from state-level fiscal incentives on certain plants (e.g. Aligarh), which management guides on separately and which has been falling as older incentive periods expire and GST rules change.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

J.K. Cement's clearest strength is that it builds things and finishes them. The framework gives its top marks to management and to the way the company expands: capacity has gone from 15 to 24 to 30 million tonnes and is now heading to 40, plants have arrived on the dates promised (Prayagraj built in ten months, 30MT hit by FY26), and the footprint has moved from a Northern base into Central, Eastern and Southern India, with Jaisalmer, Bikaner and Punjab next. Spending is aimed at growth rather than upkeep — roughly ₹3,500-4,000 crore of growth capex planned for FY27 against about ₹300 crore of maintenance. Management also talks straight, conceding uncomfortable facts such as losing putty leadership to Asian Paints and the early losses in paints. That record is what earns the 'Market Share Gainers' pattern a likely fit, on 19% volume growth and share taken in Central India. But the screen is careful about why: the share comes from new grinding capacity arriving in new places, not from winning customers away from rivals in the core markets, where management says it only holds its position.

The same trait that drives the growth also explains the biggest weakness. Because volume is the lever, price is not. The framework marks pricing down outright — growth is capacity-led while realisations have been under pressure in the North since FY23, and profit per tonne has fallen even as volumes rose 19%. The screen agrees from the other direction: 'Pricing Power' and 'Toll Roads' are both a clear no, with the record showing grey cement realisations falling, putty prices pushed down by a cheaper competitor, and prices set by regional supply and rivals' new plants rather than by the company. 'Brand Strength' only partly fits for the same reason — J.K. is a real consumer name stretched across cement, white cement, putty and paints, and advertising spend is deliberate, but it has not bought a premium price. This is why competitive advantage scores a zero and returns on capital score poorly: each new tonne is sold into whatever price the region allows. The company's answer is cost, not price — an itemised ₹150-200 per tonne savings programme covering logistics, split grinding to cut a 477 km lead distance, waste-heat recovery, green power, 12% alternative fuels, 50% pet-coke and captive coal at Mahan. That is a genuine, sustained cost culture, and it is also the reason technology is rated a low risk: the only innovation in play is this steady efficiency work, with no R&D pipeline anywhere in the record. But cost discipline does not remove the cyclicality risk, which stays elevated — operating margins have swung from about 20% to 14.5% and from 22.5% to 16.9% across the period, moving with regional prices and fuel costs.

The consumer-facing side is both a hedge and an exposure, and it is the same dealer counter in each case. Putty and paints sit alongside cement on a shared trade channel with dealer tours and market seeding, and ready-mix concrete is being rolled out from about 5 plants toward 50 by FY27 and 100 by FY28 — enough for partial fits on 'Friendly Middleman' and 'Forward Integrators'. That same counter is where Asian Paints attacked, using its paint outlets to become the largest putty distributor and forcing J.K.'s putty prices down; the company's ₹600 crore paints venture, now at breakeven, is explicitly a defence of that counter. So the channel that supports the brand extension is also the route through which a new entrant reached the customer, and it is why both 'New Entrants' and 'Good Enough Goods' sit on watch. The one durable barrier the screen finds is physical rather than commercial: permitted limestone at Jaisalmer, Panna, Telangana and captive coal, with management noting few sites remain available in the North — and white cement is described as a long-standing duopoly. But that barrier depends on the state, which is also the single highest-rated risk: roughly ₹230-260 crore a year of state incentives, more than a tenth of FY26 operating profit, exposed to GST changes and expiry, and mining leases that stall on elections and changes of government, with Odisha unresolved for years. Put together — strong execution, weak pricing, a cost programme doing the work a brand premium cannot, and a government-linked profit and permit base — the combined verdict is 50 out of 100, a 'Decent' three-star rating, with resilience judged sound.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Cyclicality. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.9× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Government touches nearly every profit lever: state incentive income of ~INR230-260 cr a year (>10% of FY26 EBITDA) that gets clipped by GST changes and expiry, mining leases that stall on elections and changes of government (Odisha/Toshali unresolved for years), limestone block auctions, coal block clearances, Jaisalmer contingent on state-funded evacuation infrastructure, and regulatory clearance issues flagged in Punjab.

> "because of reduction in GST and also in case of Rajasthan, not able to avail the incentive... there has not been much progress because of the elections" — *management, on an earnings call*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but none of the 3 computed fingerprints confirms it in the numbers yet — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Cement is a price-taker commodity: across the 20 calls margins swing violently with regional pricing and pet-coke costs (EBITDA margin 19.95%→14.49% in Q2FY23, 22.5%→16.9% by Q1FY27), and management repeatedly frames outlook in terms of price hikes 'per bag', North price pressure and $10/t pet-coke moves, while running a very heavy multi-year capex cycle (24→30→40→50 MT, capex INR3,500-4,000 cr in FY27, net debt/EBITDA rising to 1.69) into markets where peers are simultaneously adding capacity.

> "the prices are under pressure and I would see that the second quarter, the pricing would be lower than the 1st Quarter" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Demand is construction-flow/repeat rather than a single customer's capex programme; trade mix maintained at 65-70%, and the company keeps investing counter-cyclically ('we have no plans... we are on schedule') while peers 'have been talking about taking a step back with respect to capacity expansion'*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 1 of the last 11 years with swings of ±10 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 13% and 24% (±3.2 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 14% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 22%).

## What to watch

- Profit per tonne: the 'Pricing, Mix and Volume' check is the single worst score — volume-led growth only becomes real earnings growth if North realisations firm up and per-tonne profit stops falling.
- State incentives and mining approvals: the 'Government Dependency' high risk turns on the ₹230-260 crore annual incentive income surviving GST changes and expiry, and on stalled leases such as Odisha being resolved.
- The putty and paint counter: watch whether putty realisations stabilise against Asian Paints and paints hold breakeven within the ₹600 crore cap — this is where 'New Entrants', 'Good Enough Goods' and the partial 'Brand Strength' all meet.
- Delivery on the next leg: the top-scoring 'Independent, long-term and tenacious' check rests on 40MT by FY28 and the RMC roll-out to 50 plants by FY27 arriving on time, as past phases did.
- The cost programme: with pricing power absent, the ₹150-200 per tonne savings from logistics, waste-heat recovery, alternative fuels and captive coal ('Low Price Plus') is the main defence against the elevated cyclicality risk — it needs to actually land in margins.
