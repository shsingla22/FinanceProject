# RattanIndia Power (RTNPOWER) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

RattanIndia Power runs a single large coal-fired electricity plant of 1,350 megawatts at Amravati in Maharashtra, India's largest power-consuming state, built as five 270 MW BHEL units and fully commissioned by March 2015. It sells nearly all of that electricity to state distribution companies (DISCOMs) under a long-term power purchase agreement running to 2040, at tariffs set by that contract, and buys its coal under a fuel supply agreement with Coal India. Revenue is therefore fairly fixed and predictable: management says the plant runs at roughly 82-83% PLF and generates EBITDA of about Rs.1,000-1,100 crore a year, with realisation around Rs.3.8 per unit. On top of the regular tariff, it is chasing roughly Rs.2,500 crore of 'regulatory receivables' — disputed tariff claims against the DISCOM that courts have largely decided in its favour and that it expects to collect in lumps over the next three to four years.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Amravati thermal power plant (PPA sales)** — The core and effectively the whole business — a 1,350 MW coal plant about 90% contracted under a PPA to 2040, delivering the ~Rs.1,000-1,100 crore of annual EBITDA management describes as stable since 2015.
- **Merchant power sales** — A small new activity started in January 2024 — buying coal at Coal India auctions outside the linkage system and selling roughly 28 MW (potentially up to 100 MW, expandable to 300 MW) on the power exchange at around Rs.7 a unit when prices beat generating cost.
- **Regulatory / litigation receivables** — Not a product but a major cash item: about Rs.2,500 crore of past tariff claims against the Maharashtra DISCOM, arising from coal-shortfall and other escalation disputes, being realised in irregular chunks as court cases conclude.
- **Land holdings (Amravati site and Poena Power, Punjab)** — Over 1,000 acres at Amravati (with power, water, rail and highway access, eyed for data centres or industry) plus about 800 acres in Punjab held in subsidiary Poena Power; neither earns revenue today and Poena took a one-off Rs.23.8 crore write-off, but management calls both valuable for future commercial use.
- **Sinnar plant (Sinnar Thermal Power / STPL)** — A separate 1,350 MW plant near Nashik that defaulted and went into NCLT insolvency around January-February 2024, taking roughly Rs.7,000 crore of debt with it; it is now deconsolidated from the accounts, though RattanIndia may bid to get it back as sponsor.
- **Expansion plans (thermal phase 2 and renewables)** — Not yet operating — once the remaining debt (Rs.522 crore to Kotak as of March 2024, plus junior debt at ~20%) is cleared, management intends to add either a 660-800 MW supercritical unit at Amravati (roughly Rs.6,000 crore, ~Rs.1,500 crore equity) or renewable capacity, or both.

### The story in depth

RattanIndia Power is a single 1,350 MW coal plant that sells about 90% of its output to the Maharashtra distribution company under a power purchase agreement running to 2040, with coal supplied by Coal India under one fuel supply agreement. The one genuine strength the three reviews agree on is how hard that asset would be to rebuild: the multibagger screen gives a partial fit on 'Cost to Replicate' because the plant is commissioned and comes with a full PPA, a coal supply agreement, water allocation, rail and highway connectivity, and land parcels management says could not be assembled today, against five-to-seven-year build times for anything new. The risk review reaches the same conclusion from the other direction and scores 'New Entrants' as LOW, citing those same build times, near-impossible inland water allocation and no private capacity being added. Everything else in the quality framework points the other way — competitive advantage scores the worst of any area at -2.0, with no distribution reach of its own, no proprietary technology (the plant uses bought-in BHEL 270 MW subcritical units), no network effect from a single plant selling into one contract and an exchange, and no brand or image premium because the tariff is set by a formula.

The trait that creates the replication barrier is also the source of the two highest risks, and this is the central tension in the file. Owning one large, irreplaceable plant means owning one customer and one fuel supplier, which is why 'Stakeholder Concentration' is rated HIGH RISK, and why the screen rejects both 'Toll Roads' and 'Pricing Power'. Power is the distributor's single largest cost, not a small ingredient it would pay up for without argument, and the record shows years of litigation in which the customer's default position was to deny every claim, with roughly ₹2,500 crore of regulatory receivables still outstanding. Management itself caps the upside, saying realisation cannot move to ₹5 or ₹6 and that earnings before interest, tax and depreciation will simply stay in the ₹1,000–1,100 crore band. Because tariff, fuel and dispute resolution all run through government bodies, 'Government Dependency' is the other HIGH RISK, and the file traces the original financial stress to a coal linkage shortfall and a June-2013 circular the distributors ignored. The same structure shows up in the numbers: every ₹1 of assets produces only ₹0.31 of sales, about 336 days of cash is stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and the business returned only ₹4 of operating cash for every ₹100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings. Margins are 14% of sales but swing by roughly ±12.6 points a year, which the framework reads as key costs sitting outside management's control.

The history behind those numbers is why the screen records no fit at all on 'Corporate Culture': three chief financial officers and two managing directors left over five years, the chairman changed mid-period, a debt default forced a ₹3,000 crore distressed refinancing at 16–20% interest, and the sister 1,350 MW Sinnar plant was lost to insolvency proceedings after six years of failed negotiation. That past also explains the current capital allocation stance — no growth spending is underway, Phase 2 was halted mid-spend, a subsidiary's construction-in-progress was written off, and any expansion is an undecided option contingent on repaying debt, which leaves roughly 1,800 acres of land idle beside the plant. Looking forward, the risk review flags 'Technological Innovation' and 'Good Enough Goods' as elevated: electricity is an undifferentiated commodity, cheaper solar tariffs are the obvious substitute against a realisation of about ₹3.8 per unit, and a single coal asset contracted to 2040 sits on the wrong side of that cost curve while management answers renewable questions defensively. The offsets are real but small — the group has run and profitably exited solar assets, renewables is one of two declared expansion options, base-load demand is continuous and non-discretionary, and a merchant book started in January 2024 at 28 MW aims to reach roughly 100–300 MW as a first step away from one customer. Resilience is judged sound, but the combined score is 22 out of 100, a Weak one-star rating: a durable, hard-to-copy asset whose earnings are capped by the very contract that makes it durable.
