# JSW Steel Ltd. (JSWSTEEL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: METALS*

JSW Steel makes and sells steel — both flat products (hot-rolled and cold-rolled coil, galvanised, electrical steel, tinplate) and long products (beams, rails, structural sections, special engineering steel) — mostly in India, where it sold about 30 million tonnes in FY26 and booked consolidated revenue of over Rs.51,100 crores in the March 2026 quarter alone. Its customers are Indian industrial and construction buyers: automotive makers (priced on quarterly contracts), packaging, alloy engineering, infrastructure and general manufacturing, plus retail buyers, with roughly half of retail sales sold under its own brands. It earns money on the spread between steel selling prices and input costs — iron ore and coking coal together are about 60% of cost of goods sold — so it is pushing hard on 'captive' mines it owns itself to secure raw materials and cut logistics costs, and on higher-priced value-added products (management targets keeping these above 50% of the mix). Operations are overwhelmingly India-centric across multiple plant sites (Vijayanagar, Dolvi, Kadapa, Raigarh, and a new Odisha site at JSW Utkal), with small overseas businesses in the United States and Italy, and management is spending heavily — Rs.1,26,000 crores of approved capex over 4–5 years — to grow capacity toward 62 million tonnes by FY32.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Indian steel operations (Vijayanagar, Dolvi, Kadapa, Raigarh, JSW Utkal)** — The core business: large integrated steel plants across India that ran at 96% capacity utilisation in Q4 FY26 and produced the bulk of the ~30 million tonnes sold in FY26, with Vijayanagar being expanded by a further 5 million tonnes to 25 million tonnes, which management says would make it the world's largest single-location steel plant.
- **Value-added and special products (VASP) / downstream** — Higher-priced finished steel — galvanised, electrical steel, tinplate, cold-rolled, structural products — where downstream capacity ran at 95% utilisation in Q4 and management is adding about 3 million tonnes more capacity between FY28 and FY29 to keep VASP above 50% of the mix.
- **Long products** — Beams, rails, structural sections and special engineering steel aimed at India's infrastructure build-out, expanded via a 1 million tonne structural mill at Kadapa (targeted FY29), a beam-and-rail expansion at Raigarh, and the acquisition of BMM Ispat, a 0.9 million tonne long-products plant in Karnataka bought for about Rs.6,500 crores that can be doubled to 1.8 million tonnes cheaply.
- **Raw material / mining (iron ore and coking coal)** — Self-owned mine supply to feed the steel plants — 25 iron ore mines (13 operating, ~1.7 billion tonnes of resources) that covered about one-third of iron ore use in Q4, plus coking coal from Mozambique (MdR), a 30% stake in Australia's Illawarra mine and Indian mines/linkages; the goal is 50% captive supply of both iron ore and coking coal by FY31.
- **JSW JFE joint venture (BPSL business)** — A 50-50 joint venture with Japan's JFE Steel, formed by end-March 2026, into which JSW put its BPSL steel business — it removes roughly Rs.37,000 crores of debt from JSW's balance sheet (about Rs.30,000 crores done by March) and is targeted to grow to around 10 million tonnes of capacity; its volumes are tracked separately and are excluded from FY27 guidance.
- **POSCO joint venture** — A newly signed joint venture with South Korea's POSCO to build a brand-new 6 million tonne integrated steel plant in Odisha, partly to supply POSCO's existing 2 million tonne cold-rolling plant in Maharashtra with locally made steel instead of imports from Korea.
- **Overseas operations (USA and Italy)** — Small foreign plants — a 1.5 million tonne Ohio operation and a Plate & Pipe Mill in Texas, which together turned to $36 million of EBITDA in FY26 from a $35 million loss the prior year, plus Italian operations that made EUR 16.4 million of EBITDA — clearly minor next to the India business.
- **JSW One (online platform)** — An online steel sales and financing platform in which JSW holds about 61%; its steel volumes grew 50% year-on-year, gross merchandise value reached Rs.6,200 crores (up 57%, with over Rs.2,000 crores driven by its credit offerings), and it turned profitable for the first time in Q4.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Government Dependency, Cyclicality, Technological Innovation, New Entrants, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 1.0× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 72% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital held near 11% (was about 10%) — no slide in what the business earns on its capital.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** Domestic realisations are visibly propped up by the 11.5% safeguard duty — management concedes prices only recovered 'post the imposition of safeguard duty' and an analyst directly asks about the risk of withdrawal of protection given domestic prices ~20% above parity. Raw material security is likewise a function of government auctions and clearances: 25 iron ore mines, EC capacity limits, Goa mine wins, coal linkages, plus a POSCO JV signed at an India-South Korea state summit.

> "do we see any risk of some withdrawal of protection given our domestic prices are enjoying 20% kind of higher prices due to the protection" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Demand is broad-based private/infrastructure-led rather than a single subsidy scheme, and management argues India's 11.5% duty is milder than the 25-50% protection elsewhere.*

### Cyclicality — ELEVATED
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear exposure, but none of the 3 computed fingerprints confirms it in the numbers yet — held one step below the top severity.

**What the calls show:** This is a pure commodity steel cycle business: management frames the quarter through coking coal ($16/t up, $12-15/t guided higher), iron ore, NSR moves and international HRC benchmarks, and admits raw materials that make up ~60% of COGS are cyclical. On top of that it is committing roughly Rs.2.26 lakh crore of capex to more than double capacity to 62-78mt by FY32 — classic expansionary over-investment into a demand forecast, with margins already swinging (Q4 EBITDA margin 19%, FY26 normalised PAT only ~Rs.8,700cr on ~Rs.1.9 lakh cr of revenue).

> "it keeps changing depending on -- because both are cyclical in nature, but I would say only about 60% put together is the cost share of iron ore and coking coal" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Deleveraging via the JFE JV (leverage down to 1.81x, cap cut from 3.75x to 3.0x) and rising captive iron ore/coking coal integration (50% of both targeted by FY31) cushion, but do not remove, the cycle.*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 3 of the last 11 years with swings of ±28 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 11% and 27% (±4.9 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 9% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 17%).

### Technological Innovation — ELEVATED
*"Out-invented by the industry"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** The expansion is overwhelmingly blast-furnace based just as the industry faces a decarbonisation transition; an analyst pressed exactly on this, asking how far India is investing in scrap-based EAF and whether BF will keep dictating expansion for a decade. Management defends BF on scrap scarcity and cost — a reasonable answer, but it means a very large new asset base is being built around the incumbent method.

> "For now, the best technology for India is blast furnace -based and using various technologies to reduce the emissions from the blast furnace -based steelmaking" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Incremental, value-adding innovation cadence rather than a bet-the-company shift: ~30%+ of the mix is already EAF, a 4mt gas-DRI/scrap 'green steel' plant at Salav, scrap processing hubs, 1 GW renewables plus 320 MWh storage, and hydrogen/emissions technology access through POSCO.*

- ✅ Growth is holding up: 7% a year earlier, 18% recently — no structural stall.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has collapsed from a peak of 27% to 16% — the Nobel-Biocare-style fingerprint of a premium being competed away.

### New Entrants — ELEVATED
*"A new player changes the rules"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure, and 1 numeric fingerprint reinforces it.

**What the calls show:** Structurally cheaper imported steel from China and Vietnam is the entrant that resets economics — it made India a net importer for two years until duty reversed it, and management tracks Vietnamese/Chinese price hikes as a driver of its own pricing. Domestically, capacity is being added rapidly by many players, including POSCO's own India ambitions, though JSW has converted that particular entrant into a partner.

> "Due to a large flow of imports in the past 2 years, we have been a net importer. With the imposition of safeguard duty last year, steel imports have declined" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: High capital entry barriers in flats ('The entry barriers for flat capacity setting up is more'), low specific investment cost, and co-opting global entrants JFE and POSCO into JVs rather than facing them as rivals.*

- ⚠️ Operating margin drifted down from about 18% to 15% — steady profit erosion, the mark of rising competitive pressure.
