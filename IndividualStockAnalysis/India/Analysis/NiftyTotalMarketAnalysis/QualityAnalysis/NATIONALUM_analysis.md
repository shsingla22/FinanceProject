# National Aluminium Co. Ltd. (NATIONALUM) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: METALS*

NALCO is an Indian government-controlled company (Government of India holds 51.28%, a "Navratna" CPSE under the Ministry of Mines) that digs bauxite ore out of the ground, refines it into alumina, and smelts some of that alumina into aluminium metal. It sells alumina mostly for export by ship — around four cargoes of 30,000 tons a month, typically three sold at spot prices and one on an LME-linked term contract, to buyers such as smelters in the Middle East and Indonesia — plus a growing amount to domestic customers, while its aluminium metal goes largely to Indian buyers. Money is made on the gap between its production cost, which management says is the lowest in the world for bauxite and alumina per Wood Mackenzie, and international prices (LME for metal, alumina spot/contract prices), so profits swing with those prices and with how many tons it produces. Nearly all operations sit in Odisha — mining and refining at Damanjodi, smelter and captive power at Angul — with a dedicated port facility at Vizag; FY26 revenue was about Rs.17,843 crores with PAT of Rs.5,816 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Bauxite mining (Damanjodi)** — Mines the raw ore that feeds its own refinery — about 7.5 million tons a year (roughly 76 lakh ton run rate), with roughly 3.5 tons of bauxite needed per ton of alumina, so it is the captive starting point of the whole chain rather than a separate sales business.
- **Alumina refinery (Damanjodi)** — Converts bauxite into alumina hydrate and calcined alumina at 2.1 million tons of capacity (running near 100%, about 23 lakh tons), and is the main swing factor in results — of the roughly Rs.2,000 crores of 9-month revenue growth in FY26, about Rs.1,600 crores came from alumina.
- **Aluminium smelter and captive power plant (Angul)** — Smelts alumina into cast aluminium metal at about 4.6 lakh tons of capacity (4.71 lakh tons produced in FY26), using roughly 2 tons of alumina and about 14,500 units of power per ton of metal, with the attached coal-fired captive power plant supplying the electricity that makes up around 37% of metal cost.
- **Utkal D&E coal mines** — Company-owned coal blocks that began operating in January 2025 with combined capacity of 4 million tons, feeding the captive power plant so NALCO buys less outside power — coal output grew about 41.84% in FY26.
- **Wind power plants** — Four wind farms totalling 198 MW across Rajasthan, Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh — a small renewable add-on to the power base, with generation up about 9.25% in FY26.
- **Vizag port facility** — A dedicated port berth used to export alumina and metal and import inputs; it is infrastructure supporting sales rather than a profit centre of its own.
- **5th stream alumina refinery expansion** — A 1-million-ton refinery addition costing about Rs.7,600 crores funded entirely from internal cash (no borrowing), with commissioning starting June 2026 and roughly 2–3 lakh tons of output expected in the first year before full capacity — management is lining up one- to three-year contracts for the extra alumina.
- **Caustic soda joint venture (with GACL/GSCS)** — A JV supplying caustic soda, a key refinery input, though the landed cost of Rs.36,700 per ton runs high because transport from the JV to the refinery alone adds Rs.6,000–6,500.
- **Critical minerals from red mud (early stage)** — An exploratory effort to extract rare earths from the red-mud waste left by alumina refining, via an MoU with NML Jamshedpur and another party in Chennai; the pilot technology is one and a half to two years away, so it contributes nothing today.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

National Aluminium is a government-controlled aluminium and alumina producer that scores well on the things a balance sheet can show and poorly on the things a franchise would show. The quality framework gives it credit for disciplined stewardship — a zero-debt balance sheet paying for a multi-thousand-crore expansion entirely out of money the business itself generates, with no acquisitions and investment continued even while alumina prices were falling — and for a headline return of about ₹30 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital employed. The end market is genuinely growing, with roughly 9% annual growth in Indian aluminium consumption and demand pulled along by electric vehicles, solar and electrical uses. The risk review calls the company sound, and the combined rating lands at 37 out of 100, or Mixed: business quality 42, multibagger fit 18, risk safety 52.

The reason the score stays middling is that a single trait runs through almost every weak reading: the product is an undifferentiated commodity sold at prices set outside the company. That one fact explains the cluster of worst scores in the quality work — no mini-monopoly, no network effects, no switching costs for buyers who re-tender alumina and metal constantly, and no pricing power, with management stating plainly that growth is volume- and efficiency-led while alumina realisation fell from about $580 to about $370. It equally explains why the multibagger screen finds no fit at all on Pricing Power, Brand Strength, Toll Roads, Recurring Revenue and Forward Integrators. What the screen does find are the patterns available to a commodity producer that runs itself well: verified lowest-cost bauxite and alumina production for seven to eight years, cost cuts such as caustic soda consumption falling from 121kg to 99kg for a ₹129 crore saving, hard-to-copy captive assets in bauxite, coal, power and a Vizag port facility, record volumes with domestic alumina sales rising from 40,000 tonnes to 140,000 tonnes, and steady small-scale process innovation including pilot work to pull critical minerals out of red mud. Technology, notably, is bought in rather than owned — outside consultants prepare the plans and a licensor is being appointed for the new smelter.

The risks are largely the same traits seen from the other side. Cyclicality is rated high risk for exactly the reason pricing power scores worst: alumina realisation moved from about $580 to $370 to $310-320 and metal prices swung with the market, so earnings follow prices the company cannot set. Government dependency is the second high risk and cuts both ways with the quality work — state ownership at 51.28% sits alongside the note that mine leases were once a real barrier to entry but are now awarded by open auction with, as management puts it, no more special allocations for state companies, while environmental conditions dictate how ore can be moved at Pottangi. New entrants is on watch because new low-cost Indonesian refining capacity is resetting the price floor, which is a direct challenge to the cost-leadership pattern that carries most of the investment case. Customer and supplier concentration is on watch too, with 40-50% of alumina exports having gone to the Middle East before conflict there hit January volumes, and caustic soda coming from a single joint-venture source that adds ₹6,000-6,500 a tonne in freight. The genuine cushions are the same ones the quality work praised — no debt, lowest-cost position, and a deliberate push into domestic sales — set against one clear operating drag: roughly 152 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Risks tied to fashion, changing tastes, cheaper substitutes and technology disruption are all rated low, because industrial demand is functional, repeat and moving toward aluminium rather than away from it.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** NALCO is a pure LME/alumina-price commodity producer: alumina realization collapsed from ~$580/t (FY25) to ~$370/t (FY26) and to $310-320 spot by Q1 FY27, while metal LME swung from $2,380 (April-25 tariff shock) to ~$2,867; management explicitly frames demand and earnings in terms of LME, tariffs, oil prices, the dollar index and new Indonesian/Indian capacity, and is simultaneously committing ~Rs.30,000 crores of smelter+power capex peaking at Rs.8,000-10,000 crores/yr in FY29-30 — classic expansionary capex at a price peak.

> "alumina prices fell by from around $562 to around $385 this year average and there was a negative impact of Rs. 1,652 crores" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Zero-debt balance sheet funding expansion from internal accruals, lowest-cost producer status per Wood Mackenzie, and volume/techno-economic efficiency gains (caustic 121kg to 99kg) that offset price falls*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 3 of the last 11 years with swings of ±23 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from 6% to 45% (swinging ±12.0 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 10% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 14%).

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** A 51.28%-owned Government of India Navratna CPSE anchored in fixed mining infrastructure, where fortunes turn on policy: mines now only via auction with 'no more dispensers' for PSUs, environmental clearance dictating conveyor-belt-only transport at Pottangi, DGMS clearance, an unresolved Supreme Court royalty ruling, the 7% import duty embedded in the pricing policy, coal linkage/FSA pricing and withdrawal of GST compensation cess, plus Ministry of Mines vision documents framing the demand outlook.

> "the EC (Environmental Clearance) specifically states that transportation must be done via conveyor belt only... we need to amend the EC, among other clearances, which will take some time" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Captive coal (Utkal D&E) and captive bauxite reduce input-policy exposure, and pricing is LME-linked rather than regulator-set*
