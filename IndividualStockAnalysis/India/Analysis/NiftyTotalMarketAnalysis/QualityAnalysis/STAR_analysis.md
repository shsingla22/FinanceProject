# Strides Pharma (STAR) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Strides Pharma Science is an India-based drug maker that manufactures and sells generic, off-patent medicines — mostly finished pills, capsules, nasal sprays and similar dosage forms — which it supplies to pharmacy chains, distributors, wholesalers, other pharma companies and government-funded health programmes rather than direct to patients. It earns money by making these products in its own plants and selling them at a margin, and management stresses that profitability comes from picking scarce, hard-to-make products and dropping low-margin ones (gross margins around 60%, and it exited 8-9 unprofitable products in FY26). Its largest single market is the United States, at roughly $284 million of revenue in FY26 (about $70 million a quarter), with a growing push into controlled substances, nasal sprays, transdermal patches, films and 505(b)(2) products to go 'beyond generics'; it also has a Canadian business it is building up. The rest — now nearly half of sales — comes from other regulated markets like Europe, the UK, Australia and the Nordics, plus emerging markets led by branded Africa and tender-based institutional supply; total FY26 revenue was about INR 48.6 billion (roughly INR 4,859 crore) with 19% EBITDA margins. The company has no domestic Indian sales business.

**The parts of the business:**

- **U.S. business (North America)** — Sells generic and increasingly complex/specialty medicines in the United States (and now Canada), the single biggest market at $284 million in FY26 and around 50%+ of group revenue, though growth has stalled recently on a weak flu season, new competition and slow controlled-substance quota allocations, with a stated aspiration of $375-400 million.
- **Other Regulated Markets (ORM)** — Supplies developed, tightly regulated markets outside the U.S. — Europe, the UK, Australia and the Nordics — growing steadily from about $31 million a quarter in early FY24 to a $52 million quarterly exit run rate in Q4 FY26, helped by a broad, fungible product range and reliable supply.
- **Growth Markets (formerly Emerging Markets, incl. branded Africa)** — Sells branded generics in emerging markets, with Africa the main contributor and the company gaining market share there; management calls it lumpy while it stabilises, but it is a key driver of the Ex-U.S. push.
- **Access / Institutional business** — Low-margin, tender-driven supply of medicines such as antiretrovirals to global donor-funded health programmes (e.g. the Global Drug Facility); revenues are lumpy and management has deliberately scaled this down to protect margins, with donor funding pressure hurting FY26.
- **Controlled substances portfolio (Chestnut Ridge)** — A U.S. controlled-substances manufacturing operation acquired around 2022, whose approved products (ANDAs) began launching in FY26; volumes depend on government quota allocations, which have been lower than hoped as a new entrant, but management sees it as a near-term growth engine.
- **Stelis Biopharma / OneSource (associate, biologics CDMO)** — An associated biotech contract development and manufacturing business making biological drugs for outside customers (14 customers signed by 2022, EU-GMP certified plants); it dragged on group losses in FY23 and was later separated out, after which Strides' own margins and profits visibly improved.
- **Vaccines (Sputnik and AmbiVax)** — A COVID-era side activity — 23 million doses of Sputnik vaccine stuck in inventory that the company struggled to sell, plus its own protein-based AmbiVax candidate submitted for Indian emergency-use approval; only discussed in the earlier calls and not part of the current business description.
- **R&D and IP investment ('beyond $400 million')** — Roughly INR 2,500 million (~$30 million) spent over 24 months buying intellectual property and funding partnered R&D in nasal sprays, transdermals, films and 505(b)(2) products, with benefits expected from the second half of FY27.

### The story in depth

Strides Pharma comes out of these three reviews as a genuinely repaired business rather than a great one. The strongest marks are for its people and its recent conduct: an internal talent pipeline scores at the top of the range (a CFO who joined straight from college, a CEO and Executive Director with 15-18 years each, elevated through a voted succession plan), and management is credited with delivering the 2022 reset largely as promised — net debt to EBITDA down from 2.3 times to 1.55 times, the INR1,000 crore debt-reduction plan met, operating costs held at 40.6% of sales, gross margin lifted from roughly 50% in FY23 to about 60%, and return on capital employed up from single digits to 15.8%. Growth spending is separately disclosed and rising (INR51.5 crore in one quarter of FY25 to INR418 crore in FY26, on nasal-spray capacity, capacity outside the US and product rights), and geographic diversification ran ahead of plan, with non-US revenue moving from $40 million to about $70 million a quarter over twelve quarters and now approaching half of sales. That is why Management scores highest of the seven quality areas at 0.67. But Capital Allocation and Return on Capital both score zero, held back by the earlier Sputnik and Stelis over-extension that made the rescue plan necessary in the first place, and working capital is the single worst check in the set at -2: about 157 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers.

The pattern screen and the risk review keep landing on the same facts from opposite directions, and that is the most important thing to understand about this company. Two of eleven patterns are LIKELY FIT, three are PARTIAL and six are NO FIT — a weak 26 out of 100 on multibagger fit. Market Share Gainers fits because non-US business compounds at 19% and the ORM business broke a six-quarter plateau of $40-43 million to reach $52 million, with claimed leadership in 37 of 68 US products. Yet the same review notes US revenue has been flat at $70 million a quarter for two years because share is being deliberately surrendered on competitive molecules. That surrender is exactly what drives the margin improvement praised above — exiting nine low-return products in FY26 and eight over nine months, scaling down low-margin institutional business — and it is simultaneously the evidence behind three ELEVATED risks. New Entrants is elevated because rivals took share in key products and the company chose to rationalise rather than defend price; Good Enough Goods is elevated because the buyer, not the patient, picks the product and cheaper challengers reset the price; and Pricing Power is a flat NO FIT because management never cites a price increase, only erosion and re-bidding. The industry structure check says the same thing a fourth time, scoring -2: US generics is fragmented with powerful buyers, the opposite of a stable few-player market. In short, disciplined product selection is a real strength and an honest admission of weakness at once — the margin gain is bought by walking away from revenue, not by charging more. The same doubling applies to the growth engine: the push outside the US earns credit for geographic expansion and share gains, while the tenders, donor allocations and Global Fund flows that come with it sit behind the Government Dependency WATCH, where donor funding shortfalls cut FY26 growth from around 10% to 6.4%, and behind the Cyclicality ELEVATED verdict, where a flu season that did not materialise swung the US book.

The remaining patterns and risks reinforce that this is a capable operator inside a structurally hard industry rather than a business with a moat. Toll Roads, Cost to Replicate, Friendly Middleman, Forward Integrators and Recurring Revenue are all NO FIT: the products are substitutable, the approvals and tender qualifications are open to any funded generic maker, revenue is one-off product sales that management itself warns is lumpy, and the front end belongs to partners — the biosimilar launch alone takes six to eight months and depends on a partner's timing. That partner and customer dependence is the Stakeholder Concentration WATCH, where results have repeatedly hinged on single relationships including the Endo portfolio, the Chestnut Ridge transition and the RDIF contract. The one forward-looking bright spot, Innovation Dominance, is rated LIKELY FIT on rising R&D and intellectual property spend of roughly INR2,500 million over 24 months and a named shift into nasal sprays, transdermals, films and 505(b)(2) products — but the screen is explicit that this is investment, not yet demonstrated innovation, with only six launches in FY26 and the benefits pushed out to the second half of FY27. Those same niche products are the stated silver lining protecting the roughly 60% gross margin from the good-enough-goods pressure, which means the margin defence and the growth story now rest on the same unproven pipeline. Overall resilience is STRAINED, and the combined score lands at 44 out of 100 — 55 for business quality, 26 for multibagger fit, 46 for risk safety — a Mixed, two-star verdict. The fair reading is a well-run turnaround that has fixed its balance sheet and its cost base, operating in a market that does not let good management translate into durable pricing or a defensible position, with the next leg of the story dependent on new-format products that have not yet earned their keep.
