# VA Tech Wabag (WABAG) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

VA Tech Wabag is an Indian-listed, pure-play water technology company (originally founded in Germany in 1924, now headquartered in Chennai) that designs, builds and then runs water treatment plants for city governments and large industrial customers. It sells drinking-water treatment, sewage/wastewater treatment, recycle-and-reuse, desalination (turning seawater into usable water) and industrial water systems — typically as engineering-procurement-construction (EPC) contracts where it earns revenue as it executes the project, followed by multi-year operation-and-maintenance (O&M) contracts that give steadier, higher-margin, more predictable cash flow. It deliberately stays 'asset-light', meaning it prefers to engineer, supply equipment and operate plants rather than own them, though it has taken selective HAM/BOOT/concession-style projects. Work spans four continents and 25+ countries — India plus the Middle East (a stated next growth engine), Africa, Southeast Asia, CIS and Europe — with international projects at roughly half of both revenue and the order book; FY26 nine-month consolidated revenue was about Rs. 2,530 crores with a ~13.7% EBITDA margin and an order book above Rs. 163 billion.

**The parts of the business:**

- **EPC (Engineering, Procurement & Construction) / EP** — Designing, engineering, procuring equipment for and building water and wastewater plants — the bulk of the business at roughly 64% of the order book (55% back in FY25), including pure engineering-and-procurement-only scopes where Wabag supplies technology and equipment rather than doing civil construction.
- **O&M (Operation & Maintenance)** — Running and maintaining plants for clients over long multi-year contracts, about 36% of the order book and around 18% of revenue; management repeatedly calls it their focus area because it brings margin stability, cash-flow visibility and long client relationships.
- **Desalination** — Seawater reverse-osmosis plants that make fresh water from seawater — a flagship line where the calls say Wabag ranks among the top global desalination players, with projects like the 400 MLD JICA-funded Chennai plant, 300 MLD Yanbu in Saudi Arabia, 50 MLD Senegal and 53 MLD for Reliance at Jamnagar.
- **Municipal water and sewage treatment** — Drinking-water and sewage treatment plants for city water boards and government bodies, often funded by multilateral agencies (World Bank, AIIB, JICA, EIB, KfW) — examples include Namami Gange river-cleaning projects, 200 MLD Pagla STP in Bangladesh, Bangalore's wastewater plants and 200 MLD Al Haer STP in Saudi Arabia.
- **Industrial water treatment** — Water, effluent and zero-liquid-discharge (ZLD) systems for refineries, petrochemical and manufacturing clients such as Reliance, Dangote, Petronas and Eurochem, plus newer ultra-pure water demand from semiconductor/solar-cell plants (e.g. the RenewSys solar cell order), data centres and green hydrogen.
- **Recycle and reuse** — Plants that treat used water so it can be used again by cities or industry — cited as a core high-technology line, with references including the 45 MLD Koyambedu plant in Chennai, a 40 MLD Ghaziabad plant and a direct potable reuse plant in Namibia.
- **Capital / asset-light PPP projects (HAM, BOOT, DBO, concessions)** — A small, deliberately limited number of projects where Wabag takes a build-and-operate or part-ownership role (HAM projects in Kolkata, Ghaziabad and Bihar; the Ghaziabad HAM plant reached final COD in January 2026), used selectively to win long O&M tails without heavy balance-sheet investment.
- **Geographic clusters (India, MEA, CIS/Southeast Asia, Europe)** — The company is run by regional clusters — India (its leadership market), Middle East & Africa (called the next key growth engine), CIS and Southeast Asia (expansion markets), and Europe (deliberately reduced, with the Romanian subsidiary divested, though bidding has recently improved).

### The story in depth

VA Tech Wabag builds and runs water treatment plants, and what customers are really buying is the certainty that a mission-critical plant will perform — over 1,500 plants delivered, performance-guaranteed trial runs, and repeat business from marquee clients such as Reliance moving from a 24 MLD to a 53 MLD desalination job. That track record sits on top of a genuinely expanding end market: freshwater is getting scarcer, demand is rising, government and multilateral money keeps flowing, and newer needs such as semiconductors, data centres and green hydrogen are being added on top. Management is the strongest single element in the file. Rajiv Mittal led the 2005 buyout and then the 2007 purchase of the group's own parent from Siemens; the team set targets three years ago (15-20% growth, 13-15% EBITDA, 20% ROCE) and then showed delivery against them; the balance sheet has been net cash for twelve straight quarters with debt coming down year after year, no big acquisitions, and a refusal to bid state and municipal work where payment security is weak. The order book has compounded from about Rs.10,000 crore in 2022 to Rs.11,000 crore in Aug 2024 to Rs.17,200 crore in FY26, and the deliberate shift toward international markets, operations-and-maintenance work and industrial customers has lifted core operating margin from 11.5% in Q1FY23 to 13.7% in 9MFY26.

Those same strengths explain exactly which of the eleven multibagger patterns fit and which do not — and the score of 33 out of 100 on pattern fit is the honest verdict. Only Corporate Culture is a strong fit, resting directly on the management and balance-sheet discipline above, and Market Share Gainers is a likely fit, resting on the same compounding order book and the claimed number-three-globally, largest-in-India position. Five more patterns land at partial: the long-dated O&M annuities that follow each plant sale (now 36% of a Rs.163 billion book, 18% of 9M FY26 revenue, on 10-20 year contracts) are real recurring revenue but sit behind lumpy project sales; the 100-year name and 125-plus patents are real barriers, but they are B2B credentials used to get past prequalification rather than a consumer brand or an unrepeatable cost base; and moving into owning and operating plants under HAM, BOOT and DBO concessions is genuine forward integration, but into operating contracts rather than into the end customer. Four patterns simply do not fit, and they all fail for one shared reason: the work is won through open competitive tendering as the lowest or preferred bidder on fixed-price contracts. That rules out Pricing Power (management talks about absorbing raw material costs and holding its 13-15% margin band, never about raising prices), Toll Roads (a 400 MLD plant is a headline capital item for the client, not a cheap critical consumable), Friendly Middleman (no channel or installer to cultivate) and Low Price Plus.

The risks are, for the most part, the strengths viewed from the other side. The secular end-market growth that scores so well is funded by governments and multilaterals — Namami Gange, AMRUT, Jal Jeevan Mission, JICA, World Bank, AIIB, KfW, EIB, Saudi Vision 2030 — which is precisely why Government Dependency is the one high risk in the file, softened only partly by roughly 30% industrial exposure and a spread across 25-plus countries. The tender-driven, lowest-bidder model that blocks Pricing Power is the same thing that creates the Good Enough Goods and New Entrants watch risks: a cheaper competent bidder can win regardless of technology depth, and management's answer is selectivity about which jobs to chase rather than defending price. The mega-projects that drove the order book — SIBUR, Senegal, Chennai 400 MLD, Yanbu, Al Haer — are also the source of the Stakeholder Concentration risk, since a handful of jobs visibly drive results in any given period. Cyclicality is a watch item because revenue tracks customers' capital spending, with the growing O&M annuity book as the main offset. Only technology change, consumer preferences and fashion are rated low, and in each case the trend actually runs in the company's favour. The financial cost of this model shows up plainly in the quality checks: about 234 days of cash is tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and every Rs.100 of capital in the business generated only Rs.7 of operating cash this year. That is why capital allocation and return on capital are the two negative areas against strong positives for management, competitive advantage and growth. The blended result — 61 on business quality, 33 on pattern fit, 64 on risk safety — gives 53 out of 100, a Decent three-star rating with a sound resilience profile: a well-run, honestly managed company in a growing market, held back by a contracting model that consumes cash and cannot set its own prices.

## What to watch

- Cash conversion: whether the 234-day working capital cycle shortens as international work (which management says carries better working capital than India) grows past half of revenue — this is the single biggest drag on the Return on Capital and Capital Allocation scores.
- Return on capital: whether the Rs.7 of operating cash per Rs.100 of capital improves toward the 20% ROCE target management set itself — the Corporate Culture pattern rests on delivery against those stated targets.
- O&M mix: whether annuity contracts keep rising from 36% of the order book and 18% of revenue — this is what would move Recurring Revenue from partial to a real fit and is also the main offset named against the Cyclicality risk.
- Government Dependency: whether the ~30% industrial share and the 25-plus country spread keep growing, since this is the only risk rated high and the strongest end-market check depends on the same government and multilateral funding.
- Bid discipline under competition: whether the 13-15% margin band and the stated refusal to take work on payment-security grounds survive rising competitive intensity — if selectivity gives way to price, the New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks harden and the Market Share Gainers pattern weakens.
