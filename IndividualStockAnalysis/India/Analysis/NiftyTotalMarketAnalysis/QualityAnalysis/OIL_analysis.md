# Oil India Ltd. (OIL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: ENERGY*

Oil India Limited (OIL) is a government-owned "Maharatna" central public sector company that finds, drills for and produces crude oil and natural gas in India, historically from its onshore fields in Assam (registered office at Duliajan, Dibrugarh). It earns most of its money by selling that crude oil at prices linked to international benchmarks (realizations quoted in dollars per barrel, e.g. $62.84/bbl in Q3 FY26 versus $112.73/bbl in Q1 FY23) and its gas at dollar-per-MMBtu prices to industrial and power buyers such as the Namrup power plant and fertiliser maker BVFCL, with royalty, excise and special additional excise duty (SAED) taken out along the way. Management describes the company today as an "integrated energy company" spanning upstream exploration and production, midstream pipelines, refining through its subsidiary Numaligarh Refinery Limited, and a growing renewables footprint. In FY25-26 the group reported consolidated income of about INR38,981 crore, EBITDA of INR13,498 crore and profit after tax of INR7,551 crore, and paid a dividend of Rs. 11.5 per share.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Upstream exploration and production (E&P)** — The core, revenue-generating business — drilling and operating oil and gas wells (74 wells drilled and 307 workovers in FY26, a record), producing 3.45 million tonnes of crude and 3.15 BCM of gas in FY26 (6.64 million tonnes of oil equivalent combined), with management repeatedly calling upstream its "focus and foundation" and targeting 4 million tonnes of oil and 5 BCM of gas.
- **Exploration acreage and new basins** — The company holds over 1 lakh sq km of exploration acreage and about 4,800 petroleum mining leases, and is starting what the analyst host called its "biggest exploration campaign" across the KG, Mahanadi and Andaman basins — this is spending today for production later, not current revenue.
- **Midstream / transportation (pipelines)** — Pipelines that move crude and finished products, described by management as "critical" to letting the upstream and refining growth actually flow: the Numaligarh–Siliguri product pipeline expanded from 1.72 to 5.5 million tonnes per year (commissioned and inaugurated by the Prime Minister), the Duliajan–Numaligarh crude line expanded from 1 to 2.5 million tonnes, and the Paradip–Numaligarh crude pipeline (about 90% complete) that will bring imported crude to the refinery.
- **Numaligarh Refinery Limited (NRL) — refining and downstream** — OIL's "material subsidiary" (OIL's share of the equity in the expansion is stated as 69%), a refinery that buys crude and sells fuels like petrol, and is big enough to swing group results — it earned INR1,406 crore PAT in Q1 FY23 on a $36.66/bbl refining margin but lost money in Q1 FY24 on a negative $15.59/bbl margin; it currently runs at 3 million tonnes a year and is being expanded to 9 million tonnes in a roughly INR28,000 crore project whose main units began commissioning in December 2025.
- **Gas offtake and evacuation infrastructure** — Not a separate profit centre but repeatedly flagged as a constraint on the business — gas production has at times been held back by weak or interrupted demand from customers (Namrup power plant, BVFCL shutdowns), with management pinning higher gas output on the North East Gas Grid / IGGL hookup and common-carrier licensing from the regulator PNGRB.
- **Renewable energy initiatives** — Mentioned by the CMD as a "growing footprint" within the integrated energy company, but the calls give no size or earnings figures for it.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Oil India is an oil and gas producer that has spent the last several years pushing further along its own value chain, and that push is the clearest positive in the record. Sales have grown roughly 12% a year on average, ₹31 of every ₹100 of sales is operating profit, and the company is putting money into growth rather than mere upkeep: the Numaligarh refinery is being taken from 3 to 9 MMTPA at a cost of about ₹28,000 crore (₹19,000 crore already spent by FY25), pipelines are being widened, and drilling has climbed from 36 wells in FY20 to 74-75 plus in FY26. That combination is why the screen calls Forward Integrators a strong fit — the refinery, the company's own product and gas pipelines, NRL's clearance to resell gas, and exclusive city gas areas all move it closer to the final customer. Two supporting strengths sit alongside it: a genuine technology cadence (deeper seismic imaging lifting well depths from about 4,000m to 5,500m and beyond, a Rajasthan well drilled in 23 days, an offshore technology-service agreement with Total Energies, a bamboo-based second-generation ethanol plant), which earns a likely fit on Innovation Dominance; and credible expansion out of the Assam home base into Rajasthan, KG, Mahanadi, Andaman, Kerala-Konkan offshore and city gas areas in Kolhapur and Ambala-Kurukshetra. Management is low-key and technical — the chairman appeared on an earnings call for the first time in early 2026, after nearly four years in the job — and it discloses bad news promptly, from the Baghjan liability to ₹3,300 crore of provisions on GST on royalty to production lost to an economic blockade.

The catch is that the very same traits producing those patterns also produce the biggest risks, and the two are not separable. Building refineries, pipelines and wells is what makes forward integration real, but it is also why every ₹1 of assets generates only ₹0.27 of sales — one of the weakest scores in the whole framework — and why about 165 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and getting paid by customers. The pipeline network looks like a toll road because it is regulated and hard to go around, but the regulator that grants that position is also the party that gates it: PNGRB authorisations decide what gets built and when. The same is true of the moat generally — legacy Assam mining leases, the North East pipeline grid, exclusive city gas areas and roughly 1 lakh square kilometres of acreage genuinely cannot be rebuilt by a newcomer, but they are a government grant rather than something the company won commercially. That is the single thread running through the highest-rated risk, government dependency: the windfall levy whose formula management admitted it does not know, administered gas at $6.5-6.65 per MMBtu with a notified $0.25 increase, and the ₹3,300 crore royalty tax provision. It also explains the three patterns that fail outright. Pricing power is absent because prices are set by global crude and administered gas rates and then taxed; low price plus is absent because a price-taker has no price-for-volume strategy to run; market share gaining cannot even be tested because management never compares itself to a named competitor. Growth is therefore purely a volume story while price works against it — crude realisation fell from $79.35 to $65.73 a barrel across nine months — and the cyclicality risk shows how violent that can be, with realisations swinging from $112.73 to $84.89 to $62.84 a barrel and refining margins from $36.66 to minus $15.59 and back to $16.27.

Put together, the picture is a company with real assets and a real forward strategy whose economics are largely decided by other people, and the combined score of 45 out of 100 — a mixed, two-star verdict built from 47 on business quality, 36 on multibagger fit and 54 on risk safety — reflects exactly that split. Financial resilience is judged sound, and the offsets are genuine rather than cosmetic: integration into refining, pipelines, city gas and biofuels is a natural hedge against crude swings, the dividend was maintained through the downturn, the Siliguri pipeline came in about ₹100 crore under estimate, and recent policy changes on royalty, post-wellhead costs, a 20% premium on new-well gas and government-funded deepwater seismic have run the company's way. The Indian energy market is still expanding, with the push from bottled LPG to piped gas targeting a rise from 80 to 500 CNG stations and from 73,300 to 1.4 million piped connections by 2030-35. But three softer risks all point in the same direction as the strengths: gas sales depend on a small group of northeast buyers and one evacuation route, so shutdowns at the Namrup power plant and BVFCL alone dented a quarter; the asset base is hydrocarbon infrastructure that cannot be repurposed if the energy transition accelerates, and the response so far is incremental; and the largest deal on the books is the opposite of a tidy bolt-on — a debt-funded Mozambique LNG stake that sat under force majeure until November 2025 with first gas now pushed to 2028-29. The commodity itself carries no brand, no recurring contracts and no specialist channel, so the screen scores those patterns as absent or unproven. What would move this from mixed to good is not a better story but evidence that the heavy spending finally converts into sales and returns, and that policy stops setting the ceiling.

## What to watch

- Asset turn and working capital: whether the ₹28,000 crore refinery expansion and the wider pipelines lift sales per rupee of assets above ₹0.27 and shorten the 165-day cash cycle, or simply add more idle capital.
- Government dependency (high risk): any change to the windfall levy formula, the administered gas price beyond the notified $0.25 increase, or the ₹3,300 crore royalty tax provision — these decide profitability more than operations do.
- Pricing, mix and volume: whether record drilling (74-75 wells) and higher output can outrun falling realisations, given crude has slid from $79.35 to $65.73 a barrel and the Pricing Power pattern is a clear no fit.
- Stakeholder concentration (watch): whether the Indradhanush grid, the Urja Ganga link to the mainland and new city gas areas actually broaden the gas customer base beyond a few northeast buyers and one evacuation route, after the Namrup and BVFCL shutdowns hit sales.
- Merger and acquisition (negative check): first gas from Mozambique LNG by 2028-29 and no further slippage now that force majeure has lifted — this is the largest single call on capital outside the core business.
