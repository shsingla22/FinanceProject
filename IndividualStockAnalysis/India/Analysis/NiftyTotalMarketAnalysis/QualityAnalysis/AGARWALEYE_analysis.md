# Dr. Agarwal's Health (AGARWALEYE) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Dr. Agarwal's Health Care runs a chain of eye-care hospitals and clinics — it treats eye problems, with cataract surgery as by far the biggest procedure, alongside refractive (SMILE/LASIK-type), retinal and corneal work, plus routine eye check-ups and consultations. Its customers are ordinary walk-in patients: management says nearly 10,000 people a day enter its facilities, and roughly 62% pay out of their own pocket, 27% via insurance/TPA and about 12% through government schemes. It earns money mainly by charging for surgeries (over 65% of revenue), plus consultations and diagnostics, and by selling spectacles/optical products and pharmacy items at its centres. It operates a 'hub-and-spoke', asset-light network — 269 facilities as of March 2026 across 14 Indian states and 5 union territories in 155 cities, plus 19 facilities in Africa — with the South of India its largest market at 61% of revenue; FY26 revenue crossed INR 2,080 crores with EBITDA of INR 614 crores and profit after tax of INR 168 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Surgical services** — Eye operations — cataract (about 75% of surgery volumes), refractive (~5%), retinal and corneal transplants — and this is the main pillar of the business at over 65% of group revenue, with 3.23 lakh surgeries done in FY26.
- **Diagnosis, consultations and non-surgical treatments** — Eye examinations, doctor consultations and treatments that don't involve surgery, contributing about 14% of total revenue and acting as the feeder that converts walk-ins into surgeries.
- **Sale of optical products and pharmacy** — Selling spectacles, lenses and eye-related medicines to patients at its centres, worth about 21% of total revenue.
- **Tertiary hospitals (hubs)** — The largest, most fully-equipped hospitals that handle complex and high-end procedures such as robotic 'Femto' cataract surgery — 28 of these as of December 2024, and the anchor of each regional cluster.
- **Secondary centres / day-care surgical centres (spokes)** — Mid-sized centres where routine day-care eye surgeries are performed close to where patients live — 132 as of December 2024 and the bulk of the network's new greenfield openings.
- **Primary centres / eye clinics (spokes)** — The smallest format — clinics for check-ups, consultations and optical sales that feed surgical cases up to the bigger centres — 61 as of December 2024, with more added every quarter.
- **India operations (South, West, North regions)** — The core of the company at roughly 90% of group revenue, led by the Southern region (61% of revenue, 182 facilities in FY26), then West (16%) and North (8.3%), including a recent entry into Delhi NCR.
- **International / Africa operations** — A smaller overseas eye-care network of 19 facilities in Africa, making up about 7% of total facilities and roughly a tenth of group revenue, with management's stated focus increasingly on India.

### The story in depth

Dr. Agarwal's Health comes out of all three reviews as the same kind of business seen from three angles: a low-cost, self-funding eye care network that grows by opening its own new sites rather than buying them. The quality framework gives full marks to the pieces that make that possible — capex that is overwhelmingly for new capacity rather than upkeep (Rs.275cr of Rs.310cr in FY26, with Rs.380-400cr guided for FY27 and 60 new facilities), a very cheap unit of expansion (a primary clinic at INR30-35 lakh, a secondary centre at Rs.4.5-7cr), and working capital that runs in the company's favour, with cash collected 76 days before suppliers have to be paid. Add operating profit of Rs.27 on every Rs.100 of sales and cash from operations at about 84% of EBITDA, and the expansion largely pays for itself; the company also used IPO money to repay Rs.195cr of debt. That is exactly the engine behind the 'Market Share Gainers' and 'Low Price Plus' patterns — 148 new sites since FY23, roughly one a week in FY26, 269 facilities across 14 states, and margins that held at 28.9% in FY26 through the heaviest building year.

The second thread is trust and the doctor's role in the transaction, and it is where the strengths, the patterns and the one live risk all meet. 'Brand Strength' is the only outright strong fit, resting on a multi-decade heritage, 360 published papers, footfall rising from 8,000 to 10,000 patients a day and 30 lakh patients in FY26. That brand sits alongside more than 1,000 doctors and about 140 in advanced training during FY26, which produces the 'Friendly Middleman' pattern: the in-house ophthalmologist effectively chooses the procedure and the lens tier while the patient pays out of pocket (62.4% cash payers). The result shows up as 'Pricing Power' of an unusual sort — headline prices rise only 2-3% a year, at or below inflation, but revenue per patient grows 13-14% because the mix shifts upward, with high-end cataract moving from 19.2% of cases in FY24 to 26.3% in FY26 and femto/robotic procedures up 87%. The company keeps Rs.91 of every Rs.100 of sales after direct costs, which is what that trust is worth in money. But note that the same 2-3% headline number is the single piece of evidence behind the only unresolved risk, 'Good Enough Goods': standard cataract surgery is a high-volume, largely commoditised procedure where charity, government-scheme and single-doctor providers are a reasonable substitute. The premium end is protected by scale and equipment; the base procedure is not.

The risk review is otherwise unusually clean, and for reasons that trace back to the same traits. Demand is medically necessary and age-driven rather than fashionable or tied to any customer's spending cycle, so cyclicality, shifting preferences and fashion risk are all low; revenue comes from an atomised base of 30 lakh patients across 155 cities, so no single relationship matters; government schemes are small and shrinking (12% to 9%, only 6% domestically), so pricing is set by the company rather than a regulator; and on technology the company is the buyer of new equipment rather than the incumbent being displaced. That is why risk safety scores 96 and resilience reads as sound. The offsetting weakness is structural and visible in the scores: industry structure rates 0.5 and competitive advantage 0.67, the two lowest areas. India's eye care market being dominated by standalone practitioners is simultaneously the runway for share gains and the reason barriers are thin — 'Cost to Replicate' only partially fits, because management's own economics show how cheap a clinic is to build, and a competitor could copy the format even if it cannot copy 30 years of brand and 1,000 doctors. The multibagger screen is the weakest of the three inputs at 65, held back by patterns that are simply unevidenced rather than contradicted: nothing in seven calls speaks to recurring revenue, and corporate culture is marked down mainly because merger questions went repeatedly unanswered on the August 2025 call. Blending 79 on business quality, 65 on pattern fit and 96 on risk gives an overall 79 — Strong.

## What to watch
