# Dr. Agarwal's Health (AGARWALEYE) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Dr. Agarwal's Health Care runs a chain of eye-care hospitals and clinics, treating patients for eye conditions — cataract, refractive (vision-correction), retinal and corneal problems — and also selling them spectacles/optical products and pharmacy items. Its customers are walk-in patients: about 62% pay cash out of their own pocket, 27% is covered by insurance/TPAs and roughly 12% comes through government schemes. Money is made mainly from surgeries (over 65% of revenue), with the rest from consultations/diagnostics and from optical and pharmacy sales; the calls describe an asset-light 'hub-and-spoke' network that keeps capex low while adding facilities. It operates a network that grew from 221 facilities (Feb 2025) to 269 in India plus 19 in Africa by March 2026, spread across 14 states and five union territories and 155 cities, serving over 30 lakh patients and performing over 3.23 lakh surgeries in FY26 on revenue of about INR 2,080 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Surgical services** — Eye operations — cataract (about 75% of surgery volumes), refractive, retinal and corneal transplants — and the biggest earner at over 65% of group revenue, with management pushing 'high-end' procedures such as robotic/Femto cataract (over 5,900 in FY26, up 87%) and SMILE lenticular refractive surgery.
- **Diagnosis, consultations and non-surgical treatments** — Eye tests, doctor consultations and treatments that don't involve surgery, which the CFO put at about 14% of total revenue and which also acts as the funnel feeding surgical cases.
- **Optical products and pharmacy** — Sale of spectacles/optical goods and eye medicines to the same patients, contributing about 21% of total revenues.
- **Tertiary hospitals (hubs)** — The largest full-service eye hospitals that anchor each cluster and handle the most complex cases — 28 of them as of December 2024, including a flagship being built at Cathedral Road, Chennai.
- **Secondary centres / day-care surgical centres (spokes)** — Mid-sized day-care surgical centres that do routine operations closer to where patients live — 132 as of December 2024 and the bulk of new 'greenfield' openings each quarter.
- **Primary centres / eye clinics (spokes)** — The smallest format, essentially consultation-and-diagnosis clinics that refer surgeries up to the hubs — 61 as of December 2024, with 12 more opened in the March 2026 quarter alone.
- **India business** — The core of the company at roughly 90% of group revenue, split by management into South (largest, ~61% of FY26 revenue, 182 facilities), West (~16%, 47 facilities) and North (~8%, including the newer Delhi NCR entry).
- **Africa / international operations** — A small overseas arm — 19 facilities, about 7% of the network — accounting for roughly a tenth of group revenue and shrinking in share as India grows faster.
- **Dr. Agarwal's Eye Hospital (AEHL) merger** — A separately listed group company being merged into Dr. Agarwal's Health Care at a swap of 23 AHCL shares for every 2 AEHL shares, expected to take 12–14 months and intended to simplify the structure and cut duplicate audit, compliance and GST costs.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Dr. Agarwal's Health earns its Strong (4-star) rating largely on the quality of the underlying business, which scores 77 on its own. The core of it is that patients choose this company and pay for it themselves: eye surgery is a purchase people cannot postpone forever, and 62-72% of patients pay cash out of pocket, picking the brand for clinical outcomes and technology rather than price. That shows up directly in the economics — after direct input costs the company keeps ₹91 of every ₹100 of sales, ₹27 of every ₹100 is operating profit, and margins have wobbled only about ±3.6 points a year. Just as important, customers effectively fund the business: cash comes back 76 days before suppliers need to be paid. That negative working capital, combined with cheap physical formats (primary clinics at ₹30-35 lakh of capex, 220 of 221 facilities leased) and a secondary facility costing ₹4.5-7 crore that goes on to earn ₹13-15 crore of revenue, is what pays for the expansion: ₹275 crore of the ₹310 crore FY26 capex was growth spending, with ₹380-400 crore guided for FY27 to add 40 surgical centres and 20 clinics. The network now stands at 269 facilities in 155 cities, built out methodically — 182 in the South, 47 in the West, six centres in Delhi NCR since the May 2025 entry with 16 more planned, and 19 in Africa. Growth is coming from both new sites and old ones: 148 greenfield additions since FY23 and 26 new cities in FY26, alongside 14% same-store growth at facilities that pre-date FY22.

The multibagger screen is the weakest of the three legs at 60, and it is weak for a specific and honest reason: what the company does well is well-evidenced, but several classic patterns simply do not apply to a services network like this one. Brand Strength is the one clear STRONG FIT, and it is the same trait that drives the quality score — brand is cited as the reason new centres fill up faster, daily walk-ins rose from about 8,000 to 10,000 (up 25%), 30 lakh patients came through in FY26, and there is three decades of heritage behind it including 360 journal publications. Pricing power exists, but not in the headline sense: list prices rise only 2-3% a year, roughly with inflation, and the real gain comes from selling patients a better procedure — high-end cataract mix went from 19.2% in FY24 to 26.3% in FY26 and femto/robotic cataract grew 87%. Innovation looks the same way: the company reliably adopts and monetises new equipment (SMILE up 19%, CATALYS, ELITA, LUMERA 300 installs, an in-house 'Neo' hospital software system), but it discloses no R&D spend and buys its machines from third parties, so it is a fast adopter rather than an owner of the technology. Every facility is company-owned with no franchising, which gives full control of price and patient experience and adds a steady ~21% of revenue from attached optical and pharmacy retail. What is missing pulls the score down: no market-share percentage is ever disclosed across seven calls despite the evident share gains, there is no subscription, renewal or contracted revenue of any kind (revenue is only surgical 67%, consultation/diagnostics 12%, retail 21%), no external referral channel is discussed since doctors are in-house employees, and there is no itemised cost-obsession culture behind the low-cost format. In short, every rupee has to be won again next year.

Risk is where the company scores best (96), and the pattern of the risk review is that seven of the eight channels come back LOW or NO SIGNAL for reasons that follow directly from the strengths above. Demand is age-driven and medically necessary rather than fashionable or dependent on customers' own capex, so cyclicality and fashion risk barely register; roughly 73% of surgeries are cataract. Government exposure is small and deliberately shrinking, from 12% at listing in February 2025 to about 9% group and 6% domestic by FY26. There is no customer or distributor concentration — revenue comes from 30 lakh individual patients — and the one concentration that exists, the South at 63% falling to 61%, is being actively diluted by the North and West build-out. Because the centres are leased rather than owned, a shift in equipment technology would strand relatively little. The single WATCH is Good Enough Goods, and it is precisely the mirror image of the company's main strength: cataract surgery is a standardised procedure, patients have weak product awareness, the surgeon steers the choice of lens and venue, and cheaper local eye hospitals and standalone ophthalmologists can compete on price. The same cash-paying, brand-choosing patient who supports the ₹91-per-₹100 gross margin today is the one who could be steered elsewhere tomorrow — which is why scale and density (269 sites, 1,000+ doctors, 360 publications) matter as the defence. The other soft spot sits on the softer scores rather than in the risk list: Industry Structure at 0.5 and Management at 0.83 out of 2, and a Corporate Culture rating of only PARTIAL. Guidance has genuinely been delivered — FY26 revenue, margin and the 52-55 facility target were all met, and ₹195 crore of IPO debt was repaid — but the August 2025 merger call drew repeated minority-shareholder questions, and that merger only reached the NCLT by May 2026. Netted out at 45% business quality, 30% pattern fit and 25% risk safety, the result is 77 out of 100: a well-run, cash-generative, genuinely expanding business whose main open questions are whether the brand can keep the standardised-procedure competitor at bay, and whether shareholders outside the family are treated as well as the operating record suggests.
