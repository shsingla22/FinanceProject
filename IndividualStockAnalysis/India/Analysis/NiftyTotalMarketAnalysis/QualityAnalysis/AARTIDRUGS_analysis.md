# Aarti Drugs (AARTIDRUGS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aarti Drugs is an Indian bulk-drug maker: its main business is manufacturing active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) — the chemical raw materials that go into finished medicines — which it sells to other drug companies in India and abroad. Alongside APIs it makes specialty chemicals and chemical intermediates (some sold to customers, some consumed in-house to make its own key inputs), and through subsidiary Pinnacle Life Science it makes finished formulations (tablets and dermatology products) sold largely on a contract-manufacturing basis in India and out-licensed to local partners in export markets. It earns money on the spread between raw-material costs and selling prices, so results swing with product prices ('rate variance'), volumes, and how fully its plants are running. Manufacturing sits at Tarapur in Maharashtra and Sayakha in Gujarat; roughly 63% of standalone sales are domestic and 37% exports (FY26), with regulated markets a growing share of those exports.

**The parts of the business:**

- **API business (standalone)** — The core operation — bulk drug ingredients across therapeutic categories, with antibiotics the largest (about 35–46% of API sales over the period), then anti-protozoals (~20%), antidiabetics like metformin (~13–17%), anti-inflammatories (~10–13%) and antifungals (~9–12%); together with specialty chemicals it forms the standalone business that is roughly 86–88% of total revenue.
- **Specialty Chemicals and Intermediates** — A smaller but higher-margin chemicals line (about Rs 57 crore in one early quarter, with management calling its gross and EBITDA margins better than the API average), expanded through a brownfield plant at Tarapur and a greenfield plant at Sayakha, Gujarat.
- **Formulation segment (Pinnacle Life Science)** — The finished-medicine arm — about Rs 330 crore of revenue in FY26 (roughly 12% of the group, up 16% year on year) with 65% from exports; it does contract manufacturing for the domestic market, owns its own dossiers and IP for international markets, and has a dedicated US FDA-approved oncology site that was still pre-revenue as of early 2026 but is expected to supply ~40% of formulation revenue within three years.
- **Backward integration — methylamines plant, Sayakha** — A captive plant started September 2025 that makes a critical intermediate for the company's own antidiabetic (metformin) portfolio instead of buying it outside; it ran at about 29% utilisation in the December 2025 quarter, just over 40% by March 2026 (~1,000 tonnes/month in that month), with management targeting 70%+ within a year to cut input costs and lift margins.
- **Salicylic acid facility, Tarapur** — Another in-house input plant, scaled to over 300 tonnes a month but described by management as a 'laggard' — production was voluntarily halted in early FY27 because it was loss-making at the variable-cost level pending delivery of an equipment piece, with a downstream derivatives plant already commissioned and awaiting scale-up.

### The story in depth

Aarti Drugs comes across as a well-run, plainly-spoken maker of bulk drug ingredients (APIs) and, increasingly, finished formulations. The strongest marks in the quality work are all about the people and the discipline: management scores well on candour for quantifying its own bad news — the cost of a slow product ramp-up, the margin hit from selling old inventory, missed capacity-utilisation targets — and on stewardship for growing entirely by building its own plants rather than buying companies, funding that building largely from cash generated by the business, keeping debt roughly flat at about INR540 crore, and continuing to invest through the downturn. It is a low-profile promoter family that has given four years of unvarnished operational detail. Underneath that, sales have compounded at roughly 8% a year with unusual year-to-year steadiness, exports have risen from 35% to 38% of sales with the better-regulated share within exports going from 66% to 73%, and the company has taken modest share — its slice of India's metformin capacity moving from around 12-13% to above 15%. The framework also thinks the timing is favourable: API prices appear to have bottomed, stabilising from September 2025, with fourth-quarter FY26 profits before depreciation and interest up 72% on the previous quarter and management calling an inflection.

The catch is that almost every strength here is the same fact as a weakness, seen from the other side. Growth has been won on volume alone against prices that keep falling — rate variance of minus 11% in FY25 and minus 5% in FY26 — so the 'Market Share Gainers' pattern only half-fits, and the same evidence is what kills 'Pricing Power' outright and drives the 'Good Enough Goods' risk to HIGH: the buyer is another drug maker with no attachment to the brand, and when the company did push prices through in FY23 it simply lost volume. The 'Toll Roads' pattern fails for the same reason — the ingredient is a big enough slice of the customer's cost that raising its price destroys demand. Likewise, the cost programme — backward integration into methylamines at Sayakha, a captive power boiler, energy savings — is what earns a partial fit on 'Low Price Plus' and is the main defence cited against Chinese competition, yet management itself concedes that new capacity resets the economics of even its protected niches, which is exactly why 'Cost to Replicate' is judged no fit at all and 'New Entrants' stays ELEVATED. And the price cycle that the framework treats as an opportunity is the same cycle that makes 'Cyclicality' ELEVATED: across four sampled years, results swung on crude, coal, currency and API prices rather than on how much the company sold. The genuine narrow advantages are real but small — sole Indian backward integration on a metformin intermediate, scale leadership over China in four or five products — and the move downstream into formulations (INR330 crore in FY26, 65% exported, own dossiers) is credible but stops short of owning the customer, hence a partial rather than full fit on 'Forward Integrators'.

Put together, the scorecard reads Mixed at 43 out of 100 — good management (+0.6) and growth (+0.5) sitting on top of a weak competitive position (-0.5) and unconvincing capital allocation (-0.33), with returns on capital and industry structure both flat. Two structural costs sit behind that: about 121 days of cash permanently trapped between paying suppliers and getting paid by customers, and an R&D budget too small to buy a way out — roughly INR7 crore on a INR2,500 crore base in FY22, which is why 'Innovation Dominance' is only a partial fit despite a steady stream of regulatory filings. The reassuring news is what is *not* wrong: the business is judged SOUND on resilience, and three of the eight risk channels — technology obsolescence, changing consumer tastes and fashion — are all LOW, because these are essential, repeat-purchase medicines sold to industrial buyers and tracked in tonnes, not trends. Government dependency and supplier concentration are flagged only as things to WATCH, though the supplier point is concrete: China shipment delays strained production in the third quarter of FY26 and a key metformin intermediate is still imported. The honest reading is a competent, honest operator with a durable demand base and real cost effort, whose earnings are ultimately set by a commodity price it does not control — which is why the case rests less on the company's quality than on whether the upturn management has called actually holds.
