# Aarti Drugs (AARTIDRUGS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Aarti Drugs is an Indian pharmaceutical manufacturer that makes bulk drug ingredients (APIs — the active chemical inside a medicine), specialty chemicals and intermediates, and finished formulations such as tablets. It mainly sells business-to-business — to other drug companies and customers who turn its APIs into medicines — with roughly 63% of standalone sales in India and 37% exported, and exports increasingly going to strictly regulated markets (73% of exports in FY26 versus 66% in FY25). It makes money by producing large volumes of chemicals at its plants in Tarapur (Maharashtra) and a newer greenfield site at Sayakha (Gujarat), so profits swing with product prices, raw-material and energy costs, and how fully the plants are running. Consolidated revenue was about INR 2,568 crores in FY26 (INR 721 crores in the March 2026 quarter alone), of which the API and specialty chemicals business ("standalone") is about 88%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **API business (standalone)** — The core business: manufacturing active pharmaceutical ingredients across therapy areas — antibiotics (about 35-38% of API sales, the biggest and the one whose weak demand has repeatedly dragged results), anti-protozoal (~20%), antidiabetic such as metformin (~15-17%), anti-inflammatory (~12%) and antifungal (~10-12%).
- **Specialty Chemicals and Intermediates** — A smaller but higher-margin chemicals arm (about INR 57 crores in the June 2022 quarter) that makes intermediates and specialty products, including salicylic acid at Tarapur, which management calls a laggard that was shut down in FY26 pending new equipment.
- **Formulation segment (Pinnacle Life Science)** — The finished-dosage arm run as a subsidiary under its own Managing Director, contributing roughly 12-14% of consolidated revenue (INR 330.5 crores in FY26, up 16%), with about two-thirds of its sales exported; it does contract manufacturing for the domestic market and owns its own dossiers/IP abroad, out-licensing to local marketing partners.
- **Oncology formulations** — A newer push within formulations with a dedicated US FDA-approved plant — still pre-revenue as of Feb 2026 with the first product due to launch that quarter, but management expects it to generate about 40% of formulation revenue within three years.
- **Backward integration (Sayakha methylamines plant)** — A captive plant, started September 2025, that makes the methylamine raw material for its own metformin (antidiabetic) production instead of buying it outside; it hit about 1,000 tonnes/month in March 2026 at just over 40% utilisation, with management targeting over 70% within a year to cut costs and improve margins.

### The story in depth

Aarti Drugs makes generic bulk drug ingredients and sells them to formulators, and the most reliable thing about it is the people running it. Management scores best of any area we looked at: growth has been entirely home-grown with no acquisitions, the big investment programme has been paid for largely out of the company's own cash, and borrowings have been held essentially flat (about INR 540 crore in FY26 against INR 541 crore in FY23) even while the company kept spending through a downturn. Disclosure is unusually candid — the fire at the N-198 unit, a INR 6 crore drag from scaling up the dermatology business, and calling salicylic acid 'a laggard' were all volunteered rather than extracted. The operating record backs this up in volume terms: sales have compounded at roughly 8% a year with very consistent year-to-year growth, the company's share of Indian metformin capacity has risen from about 12-13% to over 15%, exports have gone from 35% to 38% of sales with the better-regulated markets rising from 66% to 73% of those exports, and there are new certifications and a US FDA plant approval to go with it. Management also believes the pricing cycle has bottomed, with realisations steadying from September 2025 and crude-linked ingredient prices firming.

The difficulty is that almost every strength above is the same trait that creates the biggest risk. The company competes by being cheap and reliable, which is why the 'Low Price Plus' and 'Market Share Gainers' patterns register at all — fifteen-plus years of making its own raw materials, a cogeneration boiler, energy efficiency work, and a claim of cost parity with China in its top products. But being the cheapest competent supplier of an undifferentiated product is exactly what makes 'Good Enough Goods' the single highest risk in the review: the buyer is a formulator with no loyalty, so the price is set by whoever is cheapest, and the record shows it. Growth has been purely volume-driven against falling prices every year (roughly 11% negative price effect in FY25, about 5% in FY26), the company keeps only INR 37 of every INR 100 of sales after direct inputs, and management's own FY27 revenue target assumes no price increases at all. That is why four separate multibagger patterns — Pricing Power, Brand Strength, Toll Roads and Cost to Replicate — come back as outright no-fits rather than near-misses. The industry structure check is the worst score in the whole framework: the market is fragmented and open to entry, Chinese supply and new entrants reset prices directly, and the customer holds the negotiating power. Management concedes that methylamine margins will compress because 'the new entrant has come in', the company has asked for anti-dumping protection on salicylic acid after shutting that plant, and the ingredient is a big enough part of the formulator's cost that when prices do rise, customers simply defer buying — the opposite of a business that can pass costs through.

The capital story sits in the middle of these two forces and explains the Mixed verdict. The roughly INR 600 crore programme — two new plants and deeper backward integration — was genuinely aimed at growth rather than upkeep, and the resulting sole position in the home-made metformin intermediate is the main cushion against supply disruption from China and Europe. But the returns on that money have so far been poor: the Sayakha plant has run at only 29-40% of capacity and the salicylic acid unit was shut, while about 121 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. And the edge it buys is not permanent — the review's judgement is that a well-funded rival can build the same thing given capital and time. The escape routes are visible but unproven: the Pinnacle formulations business is growing fast (41% year-on-year in the fourth quarter of FY26, 69% of it exported) and owning its own product dossiers plus a planned US filing for metformin would move the company closer to the end customer, which is why 'Forward Integrators' scores partial rather than no-fit. Set against that, formulations still slipped from 14% to about 13% of the group, ingredient exports fell 7% year-on-year in the same quarter, and research spending of roughly INR 7 crore on a INR 2,500 crore revenue base against 3-4% for peers means innovation is not going to be the answer. The offsetting comfort is that the business is structurally durable rather than fragile — demand is non-discretionary medicine spread across six therapy areas with none above about 38% of ingredient sales, technology disruption and fashion risk are both rated low, and volumes have grown for five straight years through the price collapse. The overall score of 39 out of 100 (43 for business quality, 25 for pattern fit, 48 for risk safety) is best read as a sound but ordinary business whose fate rests on the price cycle turning rather than on anything it controls.
