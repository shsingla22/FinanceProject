# Thomas Cook India (THOMASCOOK) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Thomas Cook (India) Limited is an India-listed travel and foreign-exchange group that sells holidays and travel arrangements under the Thomas Cook and SOTC brands, and separately sells foreign currency, remittances and prepaid forex cards. Its customers are Indian retail travellers and students going abroad, about 1,100 corporate clients who buy business travel, MICE (meetings and events) and corporate forex, and overseas tour operators who buy ground-handling services for their clients. It earns money on the margin between what it buys travel inventory for and what it sells packages for (travel EBIT margins run about 3%-4%), on the spread and fees on foreign-exchange transactions (forex EBIT margins around 40%-50%), on interest earned by deploying customer float from the forex business into fixed deposits, and on room revenue at its Sterling resorts. It operates a retail branch, franchise and airport-counter network across India plus digital channels (website, the TCPay app, WhatsApp, and forex-card delivery through Blinkit in 12 cities), and owns destination-management businesses in Southeast Asia, Southern and East Africa, the Middle East and the United States. FY26 consolidated income was about INR 83,982 million with earnings before tax of INR 3,268 million.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Foreign Exchange / Financial Services** — Sells foreign currency, outward remittances and prepaid forex cards to travellers, students going abroad and corporates, and is the group's high-margin engine — FY26 revenue of INR 3,261 million (reported net) with EBIT of INR 1,493 million at a 46% margin, with retail making up roughly 80% of that revenue and the customer float it holds generating around 60% of group 'other income' through fixed deposits.
- **Travel and travel-related services — B2C (Holidays, under Thomas Cook and SOTC)** — Packaged leisure holidays for Indian consumers across domestic, short-haul and long-haul outbound trips, sold through branches and online; it is the smaller of the two travel halves (B2C is roughly 20% of the travel segment) but is the growth driver management talks about most, with outbound holidays at about INR 610 crores in a single quarter of FY25.
- **Corporate Travel and MICE** — Books business travel for corporate clients and organises meetings, incentives, conferences and events (including some government business in earlier years); corporate travel net revenue grew 21% in Q3 FY26 and MICE was called out as a strong contributor to India performance.
- **Destination Management Services (DMS) — international** — The B2B ground-handling arm that receives tourists sent by overseas operators and runs their local arrangements, operating as Asian Trails in Southeast Asia, Private Safaris in Southern and East Africa, Desert Adventures in the Middle East and Allied TPro in the US; together with India DMS, the B2B portfolio is about 80% of the travel segment.
- **India DMS (inbound)** — Handles foreign tourists coming into India for overseas partners; it grew 58% in Q1 FY25 but went flat in Q3 FY26 because of travel advisories and airspace/routing problems hitting key source markets.
- **Sterling Holiday Resorts** — A subsidiary that owns and operates leisure resorts in India, in heavy expansion mode (five resorts opened in calendar 2024 with eight to ten more planned that year), growing 19% in Q4 FY26 top line and 7% for the full year; in March 2026 the Board gave in-principle approval to demerge the group's resort business into Sterling so the parent can focus on travel and financial services.
- **DEI (Digital Imaging)** — A digital imaging business run as a separate subsidiary, with roughly 50% of its revenue coming from the UAE market — which made it one of the units hit hardest by the Middle East conflict in FY26.
- **Quess Corp shareholding** — A non-operating stake in listed Quess Corp whose mark-to-market swings move reported profit up and down; management repeatedly asks investors to strip it out, and the holding has been reduced over the period covered.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Thomas Cook India runs an asset-light, cash-generative business: every ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.03 of sales, roughly ₹21 of operating cash comes back on every ₹100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings, and only about 29 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Part of the reason is that customers fund the business themselves — the ₹16 billion of prepaid forex card float finances operations, and by February 2026 about 60% of other income came from fixed deposits on that float. On top of that sits the group's genuinely hard-to-copy asset: reach. Franchise and airport counters, Tier 2/3 last-mile outlets, an app and WhatsApp, quick-commerce forex card delivery through Blinkit in 12 cities, 800 education counsellors, NBFC tie-ups, 77 resorts and destination-management entities in around 19 countries. That reach is what turns into the clearest quantified strength in the whole record — share taken from a shrinking market, with education forex up 17% while RBI data showed the industry down 22%, and travel forex up 13% against an industry down 3%. Management scores well too: stable, long-tenured leadership, quarter-by-quarter guidance measured against outcome, and bad news disclosed promptly and specifically.

Those same strengths explain both the patterns that fit and the ones that do not. The disclosure habits and leadership stability make Corporate Culture the one clear strong fit, and the benchmarked share data makes Market Share Gainers a likely fit. Almost everything else lands as partial, and for a consistent reason: each attractive trait stops short of becoming a moat. The float looks like recurring, customer-funded revenue but is not a subscription or contract the customer must renew. Owned outlets and rising digital penetration look like forward integration, but digital is still only 21-23% of transactions after four years of investment — which is exactly why the disintermediation risk from online travel agents and now AI agents is rated elevated rather than low. Pricing power exists at Sterling (average room rates up 5% then 12% with occupancy also rising, RevPAR up 16-17%) and in forex, where EBIT margins reached 41-48%, but the core outbound travel business takes prices rather than sets them: long-haul volumes fell as airfares rose 30-50%, and management is re-engineering Europe products to be more affordable. The Toll Roads pattern is an outright no-fit, and the arithmetic shows why — forex gross margins of about 1.35-1.5% and travel EBIT of about 3.3% on a purchase that is a big, visible line in the customer's own budget. The very forex network that funds the balance sheet is also the product closest to a commodity, which is the same fact behind the Good Enough Goods risk.

The risks are mostly the flip side of what makes the business work. An asset-light distributor of other people's travel is highly exposed to events it does not control, and cyclicality is the one risk rated high: across all 16 calls, results moved on COVID recovery, elections, heatwaves and floods, and in FY26 the Pahalgam attack, Operation Sindoor and the US-Iran-Israel war. That shows up directly in the quality checks as operating margins of about ₹6 per ₹100 of sales swinging by roughly ±14.8 points a year, sales growth averaging about 10% but arriving lumpily, and the lowest-scoring area of all — industry structure, where low entry barriers and visible price competition are conceded. Leisure demand also rests on destination fashion that moves fast (China up tenfold, Vietnam up 30%, Japan up 15%, while UAE and CIS volumes went to zero), and the B2B side leans on a limited set of large partners, as FTI's bankruptcy showed with a USD 0.5 million provision and a ₹5 crore hit in a single quarter, alongside Desert Adventures' Middle East exposure halving in Q4 FY26. Policy cuts both ways: the TCS cut on overseas packages to a flat 2% is a tailwind, while visa regimes, airspace closures and a one-off ₹100 crore government MICE order that will not repeat move the P&L on their own. The offsets are real — portfolio breadth across forex, corporate travel, destination management and domestic resorts, roughly 8,000 corporate travel clients and 1,100 forex corporates — and resilience is judged sound. But the combined score of 44 out of 100 (61 on business quality, 33 on multibagger fit, 28 on risk safety) reads exactly as the record does: a well-run, well-distributed operator winning share in a structurally growing market, inside an industry that keeps handing the economics back to the customer.

## What to watch

- Digital share of transactions breaking meaningfully above 21-23% — the Technological Innovation risk stays elevated until the owned-distribution build (Forward Integrators) actually shifts volume away from the legacy branch and agent model.
- Forex gross margin holding or improving from the 1.35-1.5% range, and whether net revenue moves off flat — this is the test of the Good Enough Goods risk and of whether the ₹16 billion float is a franchise or just cheap funding.
- Whether operating margin swings narrow from the ±14.8 point range and growth becomes less lumpy — that would lift the two weakest checks (Profit margin, Persistence of growth) and soften the high Cyclicality rating.
- Continued benchmarked share gains against RBI LRS data in both travel and education forex — this is the single evidence base for the Market Share Gainers pattern, and it weakens the moment the comparison stops being disclosed.
- Further single-partner or single-geography shocks after FTI and the 50% Q4 FY26 drop in Desert Adventures' Middle East exposure — repeat hits would push Stakeholder Concentration from watch to a live problem in the B2B destination-management business.
