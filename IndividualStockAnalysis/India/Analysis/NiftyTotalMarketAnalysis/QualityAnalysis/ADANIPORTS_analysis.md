# Adani Ports and Special Economic Zone Ltd. (ADANIPORTS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: SERVICES*

Adani Ports and Special Economic Zone Limited (APSEZ) runs commercial seaports in India and abroad, charging shipping lines and cargo owners to unload, load, store and move goods — containers, coal, fertiliser, agricultural produce, LPG and other cargo — through its terminals. It earns revenue per tonne of cargo handled, so profit rises with volume and with the mix of higher-paying customers; management reports revenue per ton, port EBITDA margins around 70-72%, and domestic cargo of 451 million metric tons in FY26 at a 27.1% share of the Indian market. Around this core it sells inland logistics (rail, trucking, warehouses, agri silos, freight forwarding) that carries the same cargo to and from the ports, and marine services (a fleet of tugs and vessels, 136 by FY26) that assist ships in harbour. It also earns smaller amounts from leasing land in its special economic zone and from operating-and-maintenance contracts on ports it does not own, and it grows by winning long-term concessions to run terminals in India and overseas — Israel, Sri Lanka, Tanzania, Australia — funded by capex and debt kept at roughly 1.9-2.5x EBITDA.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Ports business (domestic ports)** — Owns and operates Indian seaports and terminals — Mundra, Hazira, Dhamra, Gangavaram, Krishnapatnam, Ennore, Kattupalli, Vizhinjam, Gopalpur and others — handling containers and bulk cargo for a fee per tonne; this is by far the biggest part, contributing about Rs 3,936 crores of Rs 4,786 crores of quarterly revenue in Q3 FY23 and about 91% of EBITDA, with margins near 70-72% and return on capital employed of 23% in FY26.
- **International ports** — Terminals and port concessions outside India — Haifa in Israel, CWIT Colombo in Sri Lanka, Tanzania, and the NQXT terminal in Australia — a smaller but fast-growing piece, with FY26 revenue up 34% and EBITDA up 180% as Colombo ramped up and the Australia deal closed.
- **Logistics business (Adani Logistics)** — Moves cargo inland to and from the ports using owned 'asset heavy' infrastructure (rail rakes, inland container depots, 2.9 million sq ft of warehousing, agri silos of 1.2 million tonnes going to 4 million) alongside 'asset light/asset zero' freight forwarding and trucking; it was roughly Rs 490 crores of quarterly revenue in Q3 FY23 with ~29% margins, grew 55% in FY26, and management targets about Rs 20,000 crores of revenue by FY31.
- **Marine services** — Described as the third business pillar — a fleet of tugs and support vessels (136 by FY26, including deployments in Mexico and Sri Lanka) that services ships at ports; small in absolute size but growing fast, with FY26 revenue up 134% and EBITDA up 125%.
- **SEZ and O&M** — Income from the special economic zone (land and related development) plus operations-and-maintenance contracts for third-party facilities such as the container terminal at Syama Prasad Mookerjee Port, Kolkata; the smallest piece, about Rs 360 crores of revenue and Rs 132 crores of EBITDA in Q3 FY23.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Adani Ports scores 68 out of 100 (Strong, four stars), and the three strands of the work tell one story rather than three. The business owns a set of physical locations that cannot be copied — deep-water sites held on decades-long government concessions, with Mundra running at 94% utilisation and 770,000 TEU a month, four ports ranked in the World Bank's Container Port Performance Index, and a portfolio heading from 653 million tonnes toward 1 billion tonnes by 2030. That single fact is why the quality checks award full marks for regional monopoly and switching costs, for customers buying reliability, and for growth capex earning rising returns (consolidated ROCE 16%, domestic ports up to 23%). It is also why the multibagger screen calls Cost to Replicate a strong fit and Toll Roads a likely one: port charges are a small, unavoidable slice of a shipper's landed cost, and the company lifted realisation by Rs.58 a tonne (+13%) and Mundra's realisation by 25% without losing volume.

The same trait that creates the moat creates the largest risk. Because every asset is fixed infrastructure sitting on a government concession or SEZ approval — Deendayal, Tanzania, the Kolkata O&M contract, Vizhinjam with the state, and the Gopalpur and Karaikal acquisitions all requiring sign-off — Government Dependency is rated HIGH RISK, and it is the only channel of the eight to be rated so. Being asset-heavy scores -2 on the quality framework (every rupee of assets produces 21 paise of sales) and drags the Return on Capital area negative, even though the returns themselves are rising. The distressed assets being absorbed — Karaikal out of insolvency, Gopalpur, the state port at Kolkata — are simultaneously the evidence for the Share Donators check and the Market Share Gainers pattern, and a reminder that the growth route runs through government processes rather than around them. Two further checks score -2 for the opposite of obscurity: this is a headline index constituent inside one of India's most scrutinised promoter groups, with calls dominated by questions on intercompany loans, auditor qualifications and litigation. That scrutiny is also why Management scores barely above zero despite a genuinely good delivery record — guidance beaten three years running, the 500 million tonne target promised in February 2023 and delivered in FY26, and net debt to EBITDA promised at 2.5x but brought to 2.1x then 1.9x, which is what earns Corporate Culture a strong fit.

The remaining risks are milder and mostly the flip side of strengths already counted. Pricing power is only a partial fit because the same long contracts that make revenue annuity-like also lock in price step-downs — the ten-year FCI agreements reprice downward, and some pricing has been given away voluntarily. Stakeholder Concentration sits on watch for the same reason: results visibly move on a handful of named relationships, with the Mundra joint-venture partner's volumes down 9% year on year, the 49% stake sale of the Colombo terminal holding to MSC, and the FCI repricing. Energy transition is flagged by management itself — 50GW of renewables added in a year against thermal coal, and households moving off LPG — while coal remains a large dry-cargo pillar; the mitigation is the same push into containers, liquids, agri and logistics that supports the Forward Integrators pattern, where logistics revenue grew 64% and then 55%. Technology, fashion and cheaper substitutes are all rated low: demand is functional cargo tonnage, technology is incremental and company-controlled, and both realisation per tonne and market share (27.1% domestically) rose together. Overall resilience is judged sound, and the lowest of the three component scores is risk safety at 64 — the record's own arithmetic saying the concerns are about the setting the business operates in, not about how well it operates.
