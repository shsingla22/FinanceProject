# Adani Ports and Special Economic Zone Ltd. (ADANIPORTS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: SERVICES*

Adani Ports and Special Economic Zone Limited (APSEZ) runs commercial seaports and the transport services around them. Its customers are shipping lines, exporters and importers whose cargo — containers, coal, fertiliser, agricultural goods, LPG and other bulk items — is loaded and unloaded at its terminals; it earns money mainly by charging per tonne of cargo handled, so revenue tracks both volume and the mix of cargo and customers (management tracks a 'port revenue per ton' figure). Its main base is India, where it operates a network of ports along the west coast (Mundra, Hazira), east coast (Dhamra, Gangavaram, Krishnapatnam), and south (Vizhinjam, Ennore, Kattupalli); it has also expanded overseas with terminals and marine operations in Israel (Haifa), Sri Lanka (Colombo), Tanzania, Australia (NQXT) and elsewhere. Alongside the ports it runs an inland logistics business (trains, warehouses, trucking, freight forwarding), a marine services fleet of tugs and vessels, and a special economic zone / land-and-port-services operation. In FY26 it handled 500 million metric tons of cargo in total (451 million in domestic ports, about a 27% share of the Indian market), with revenue growing 25% and net debt at 1.9x EBITDA.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Ports business (domestic)** — The core operation — Indian seaport terminals that load and unload ships, by far the biggest part: in Q3 FY23 it was about Rs.3,936 crore of Rs.4,786 crore total revenue and Rs.2,737 crore of Rs.3,011 crore total EBITDA, running at roughly 70-72% EBITDA margins and 23% return on capital employed in FY26.
- **International ports** — Overseas terminals and concessions — Haifa in Israel, the CWIT terminal in Colombo, Tanzania, and the NQXT terminal in Australia — a smaller but fast-growing piece, with revenue up 34% and EBITDA up 180% in FY26 as Colombo ramped up.
- **Logistics** — Moves cargo inland and around the ports via rail rakes, inland container depots, warehousing, agri silos, trucking and freight forwarding — described as the 'second business pillar', around Rs.490 crore of quarterly revenue in Q3 FY23, growing 55% in FY26, with lower margins than ports (roughly 29% EBITDA margin) and return on capital employed rising from 6% to 10%.
- **Marine services** — The 'third business pillar' — a fleet of tugs and support vessels (136 vessels at its FY26 peak, including deployments in Mexico and Sri Lanka) that assist ships in and out of berths; small but growing very fast, with revenue up 134% and EBITDA up 125% in FY26.
- **SEZ and O&M** — Leases land in its special economic zone and operates port facilities for third parties under management contracts (for example an O&M contract for a container facility at Kolkata's Syama Prasad Mookerjee Port) — the smallest piece, about Rs.360 crore of revenue and Rs.132 crore of EBITDA in Q3 FY23.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Adani Ports comes out of all three reviews as one thing above all: an unavoidable piece of India's trade plumbing. The quality framework gives its best marks to what customers actually get — reliability and throughput certainty (four ports in the World Bank Container Port Performance Index, service continuity through crises) and the convenience of an integrated network of ports, rail rakes, inland container depots, warehouses, agri silos, trucking and freight forwarding that no Indian rival can match. That same network is why the multibagger screen calls 'Cost to Replicate' a strong fit: finite deep-water coastline held under long-dated concessions, roughly ₹8,000 crore already sunk at Vizhinjam with ₹20,000 crore more committed for Phase 2, and a build toward 1 billion tonnes of capacity by 2030. The results line up behind the story — about ₹59 of operating profit per ₹100 of sales, domestic port margins around 70-72% held from FY23 to FY26, domestic port return on capital at 23% and consolidated at 16%, domestic market share up to 27.1%, container share up more than 2.5% and coal share up 1%, all while growing at roughly twice the pace of India's trade. Spending heavily on new capacity while returns went up, rather than down, is the single most encouraging fact in the file, and it is why the growth and customer-benefit areas score highest.

The interesting part is that the very traits producing those strengths are the ones producing the risks. Being asset-heavy is what makes the moat expensive to copy — and it is also the framework's worst single mark, with every ₹1 of assets generating only ₹0.21 of sales, dragging the return-on-capital area to the bottom of the scorecard even though the reported returns are rising. Government-awarded concessions are what keep new entrants out (rated low risk, with no structurally different competitor discussed across fourteen calls) and are simultaneously the highest-rated risk in the review: growth depends on concessions, approvals and contracts being granted and honoured — Deendayal, Tanzania, the Kolkata operations contract, a Myanmar sale held up on conditions — with only partial relief from spreading the exposure across states, overseas ports and non-concession logistics. Size cuts the same way: being the country's largest port operator is what makes the network valuable, and it is also why the company scores badly on staying out of the limelight and cannot rely on being overlooked, sitting inside the country's most scrutinised group with analysts probing governance, audit qualifications and litigation. Nothing here is hidden — it is the same asset base seen from two ends, which is why the risk review still lands on 'sound' overall.

Where the story thins out is on the softer, more repeatable sources of profit, and this is what keeps the combined rating at 62 out of 100 — decent rather than exceptional. Pricing power is only a partial fit: realisation per tonne rose 13% and a busy-season surcharge was passed on, but management credits most of the gain to a richer customer and cargo mix rather than raising prices, and FY26 saw free storage given away and a contractual price step-down. Recurring revenue is thin — no renewal or maintenance streams are discussed, and the one contracted arrangement disclosed, with FCI, actually steps pricing down after ten years and dented logistics margins for several quarters. Reputation is institutional rather than consumer, and the delivery record is genuinely strong (the FY23 promise of 500 million tonnes and debt at 2.5 times earnings was beaten at 1.9 times) but sits alongside leadership churn. Those partial patterns line up with the watch-level risks: results still move visibly on a handful of named relationships, including a joint-venture partner's volumes falling 9% in one quarter and a single dry-cargo swing in FY26, and management itself flags two slow shifts in what customers ship — cooking moving from LPG to electricity, and a record 50 GW of renewable build-out working against imported thermal coal — with the answer being a deliberate tilt toward containers, liquids, agri cargo and logistics. So the moat is physical and real; the question is how much of the profit growth comes from that moat versus from mix and from cargo the country may need less of later.

## What to watch

- Government Dependency (highest-rated risk): whether pending concessions, approvals and contracts — Deendayal, Tanzania, the Kolkata operations contract, the stalled Myanmar sale — keep converting on time, since this is what growth actually rests on.
- Capex for growth vs return on capital: whether the ₹20,000 crore Vizhinjam Phase 2 spend and the push to 1 billion tonnes keeps returns rising (23% domestic, 16% consolidated) or finally dilutes them, given every ₹1 of assets still produces only ₹0.21 of sales.
- Pricing Power (partial fit): whether realisation gains come from actual price increases rather than customer and cargo mix, especially after the free storage given away and the FCI contract's ten-year price step-down.
- Stakeholder Concentration (watch): whether quarterly results stop swinging on single relationships — the 9% drop in joint-venture volumes at Mundra, the one dry-cargo customer that moved FY26 — as new ports and asset-light logistics broaden the base.
- Shifting Consumer Preferences (watch): whether the tilt toward containers, liquids and agri cargo outruns the decline management itself flagged in thermal coal and LPG volumes as renewables and electric cooking scale up.
