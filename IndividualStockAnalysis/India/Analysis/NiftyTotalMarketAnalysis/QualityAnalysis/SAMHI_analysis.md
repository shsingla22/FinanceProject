# Samhi Hotels (SAMHI) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

SAMHI Hotels is an Indian company that owns hotel buildings and runs them as a portfolio — management calls itself a "branded hotel ownership and asset management company," and at the time of its 2023 IPO it was India's third-largest owner of hotel rooms with about 4,800 rooms (about 4,900 operational by end-2025, plus roughly 1,900 more rooms under development or rebranding). It does not use its own consumer brand: it puts global brands such as Marriott, Hyatt and IHG on top of its hotels and lets those partners manage or franchise them, while SAMHI supplies the capital and squeezes out the profit. Its money comes from selling hotel rooms night by night — what it calls "asset income" (INR 1,279 crores of total income in FY26, with hotel-level "asset EBITDA" margins around 36-41%) — and its growth engine has been buying older, underperforming hotels cheaply, renovating and rebranding them, then lifting their revenue per available room. Its guests are overwhelmingly business travellers in India's big office and airport cities: Bangalore, Hyderabad, Pune and Delhi NCR alone account for about 76% of asset income, and management tracks grade-A office absorption and airline passenger traffic as its two demand indicators.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Mid-scale (Holiday Inn Express)** — Its cheapest, no-frills rooms — around 1,500 rooms at IPO selling at roughly INR 3,800-4,000 a night; SAMHI owned about 71% of all Holiday Inn Express hotels in India, and this is the part hurt most by the FY26 GST change because many of its rooms sell below INR 7,500.
- **Upper mid-scale (Fairfield by Marriott, Courtyard)** — The largest block by rooms — about 2,000 rooms at IPO, with SAMHI owning over 40% of all Fairfield by Marriott hotels in India; it is being topped up with additions such as 80 extra rooms at Fairfield Sriperumbudur and upgrades of older assets into Courtyard by Marriott.
- **Upscale and upper upscale (Sheraton, Hyatt Regency, Westin, W)** — The highest-rate, most profitable rooms — about 1,000 rooms at IPO and roughly 42% of revenue by FY26, which management is deliberately doubling toward 2,000+ rooms (W Hyderabad, Westin-Tribute Bangalore Whitefield, a 700-room Westin/Fairfield project in Navi Mumbai) to push the revenue mix to about 60% and lift average room rates.
- **ACIC portfolio** — A block of six hotels with 962 rooms plus a Navi Mumbai land parcel bought in August 2023, initially a drag on growth while it was moved from Marriott franchise to Marriott management and its cost base fixed; its hotel-level margin was brought up to near 40% by FY25.
- **Acquisition and turnaround (the core strategy)** — Not a separate segment but the way most of the company was built — all but roughly 700 of its rooms were bought rather than built, then upgraded in product, brand and management; it is paired with "asset recycling," selling weaker hotels such as Four Points Chennai and Caspia Delhi to fund better ones.
- **GIC upscale platform** — A joint venture launched in FY26 in which sovereign investor GIC committed about INR 750 crores for a 35% minority stake in a 1,000-room upscale platform (INR 600 crores already received), giving SAMHI outside equity to fund upscale projects and cut debt — net debt fell from INR 1,967 crores to INR 1,450 crores during FY26.
- **Variable-lease (capital-light) hotels** — A newer, low-capital way to add hotels by leasing space in someone else's building rather than owning it — a 162-room hotel with Ingka Centres (IKEA Group) at Noida and a 260-room hotel at One Financial District, Hyderabad; management says the majority of its current pipeline is of this type and can be funded from operating cash flow.
- **RARE India (experiential leisure)** — A small FY26 entry into holiday rather than business travel — a 70% stake in a curated platform of 73 hotels and about 1,000 rooms, described as under discussion for affiliation with Marriott Bonvoy's Outdoor Collection.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

SAMHI is an owner of hotels rather than an operator of a hotel brand, and almost everything good in these records flows from one repeatable move: buy an underperforming, under-managed independent hotel at a discount to what it would cost to build, put a Marriott, IHG or Hyatt name on it, and reprice the rooms. The evidence that this works is unusually consistent. The Pricing Power pattern is a strong fit — same-store room rates rose 15.9% in the December 2025 quarter while occupancy actually fell 1.6 percentage points, meaning growth is coming from charging more rather than filling more, and rebranded assets have roughly doubled their rates (Trinity Bangalore from about INR 5,900 to INR 8,000-10,000). The quality checks agree from a different angle: after direct input costs the company keeps INR 92 of every INR 100 of sales, cash arrives 321 days before suppliers must be paid, and the deliberate shift of revenue from 42% to roughly 60% upscale and upper-upscale shows the mix being managed, not drifting. Sitting underneath it all is a genuinely growing end market — record office absorption, GCC and technology investment, and quarterly airline passengers rising from 65 million to 77 million — plus an abundant supply of weak competitors for SAMHI to take rooms from, which is what makes it a credible Market Share Gainer at 4,900 operational rooms with 1,900 more being built.

The problem is that the same three traits producing those wins also produce the main risks, and the records make the overlap explicit. First, owning the buildings is what allows SAMHI to buy cheap and capture the upside, but it also means every INR 1 of assets generates only INR 0.28 of sales — the weakest check in the framework, and the reason Return on Capital scores negative overall. Second, renting the brand is what delivers the doubled room rates, since the global operator brings the reputation and the booking system; but it also means SAMHI owns no consumer brand of its own, spends essentially nothing on its own marketing, and has been moving further away from the guest rather than closer (converting ACIC from a Marriott franchise to fully Marriott-managed). That is why Brand Strength and Forward Integrators are both a clear no-fit, and why supplier concentration — with Marriott increasingly dominant — sits on the watch list. Third, the demand the company underwrites off, office absorption and airline traffic, is exactly what makes cyclicality the single high risk: the current run of strong rates is explicitly attributed to a shortage of new hotel supply, and rooms are sold one night at a time with no contracted or recurring revenue to cushion a downturn. Management itself quantified INR 45-52 crores of revenue lost in FY26 to four unrelated one-off events, which is the clearest possible demonstration that this is discretionary, event-sensitive spending rather than an unavoidable cost for customers.

What keeps the picture balanced is the management record, and here two of the analyses point at the same evidence. Candor scores among the highest checks and Corporate Culture is a strong fit, for the same reason: promises were kept and bad news arrived early and with numbers attached. Net debt of 8.7 times earnings was pledged down to about 3.5 times and reached 3.0, revenue guidance of 9-11% was delivered at 12.3%, and it was management, not analysts, who put figures on the roughly 200 basis points of margin permanently lost to the GST change and on the event-driven revenue shortfall. That habit of disclosure is worth something precisely because the business itself is fragile in places — an investor is at least being told the truth about a cyclical, asset-heavy, brand-dependent company. The combined rating lands at 59 out of 100, or Decent, built from moderate business quality (57), moderate pattern fit (56) and a somewhat better risk position (68), with overall resilience judged sound. In plain terms: a well-run operator of a genuinely repeatable acquisition-and-rebranding playbook, executing into a favourable cycle, but one whose returns depend on heavy asset ownership, whose customer relationship is held by someone else, and whose earnings would fall with the corporate travel cycle rather than resist it.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality. The balance sheet and cash engine shows no financial stress — none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.8× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ✅ About 94% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about -3% to 8% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 3 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** SAMHI is an owned-asset hotel company whose demand management itself underwrites off two cyclical indicators — office absorption and airline passengers — and whose RevPAR upcycle (FY24–FY26) is explicitly attributed to a supply trough, while margins have swung on GST, one-off events (India-Pakistan conflict, floods, December airline crisis, Gulf crisis compressing FY26 revenue by INR 45–52 crores) and the whole model runs on heavy capex/leverage (net debt peaked ~INR 2,060 crores, 4.3x) added at the top of the cycle.

> "The new hotel supply continues to remain very low in key cities and creates a perfect environment for RevPAR growth." — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Demand is operations-linked corporate business travel (not customer capex); acquisition-and-turnaround of distressed hotels at a discount to replacement cost is a countercyclical buying discipline; deleveraging to 3x, 12-year average debt tenure, GIC platform capital and capital-light variable leases reduce financial cyclicality.*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±64 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from -41% to 36% (swinging ±25.7 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 34% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 5% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Government Dependency — WATCH
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Revenue is private-sector corporate demand, not government contracts, but a single tax-policy change (hotel GST moved from 12% with input credit to 5% without) permanently removed ~200bps of margin and INR 14 crores of H2 FY26 EBITDA, and projects depend on state approvals and authority payments (Kolkata/Whitefield hotels 'waiting for final approvals', Navi Mumbai extension payments and litigation).

*Silver lining: No revenue is regulator-set or subsidy-driven; management expects pass-through via renegotiated corporate rates and notes new upscale inventory sells above the INR 7,500 threshold, so the affected base shrinks as the mix shifts.*

## What to watch

- Cyclicality (HIGH RISK): office absorption and airline passenger growth stalling, or new hotel supply returning to core markets — the supply trough is what the current rate growth is explicitly attributed to.
- Asset turn (-2) and Return on Capital (-0.67): whether the INR 2,200 crores of committed growth capex actually earns the promised mid-teens returns, or simply adds more assets producing INR 0.28 of sales each.
- Pricing Power (STRONG FIT): room rates continuing to rise while occupancy is flat or falling — if rate growth stops without occupancy picking up, the core of the investment case weakens.
- Stakeholder Concentration (WATCH): the four cities producing about 76% of asset income, and Marriott's growing share of the brand licences on top of SAMHI's buildings.
- Corporate Culture (STRONG FIT) and Candor (+2): net debt staying near 3.0 times earnings and guidance continuing to be met — a missed or quietly revised promise would remove the strongest single support in the rating.
