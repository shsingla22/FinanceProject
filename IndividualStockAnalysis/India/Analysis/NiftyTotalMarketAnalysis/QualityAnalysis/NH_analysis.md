# Narayana Hrudayalaya Ltd. (NH) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: HEALTHCARE SERVICES*

Narayana Hrudayalaya (brand name Narayana Health) runs a chain of hospitals that treat patients — its stated mission is affordable, high-quality care — with a strong tilt toward complex, high-end "quaternary" work such as cardiac surgery, transplants (heart, liver, kidney, bone marrow), oncology, GI and robotic procedures. It makes money mainly by charging for treatment of patients admitted to or visiting its hospitals: revenue is discussed as patient volumes/footfall times average revenue per patient (ARPP), with bills paid by patients themselves, by insurers and "preferred payors," by government/scheme payors, and by international/medical-tourism patients; the group also runs pharmacies inside its own hospitals and clinics, a small outpatient clinic network, and a health insurance arm. Its base is India — clusters around Bangalore (its largest and most profitable campus, Health City), Kolkata (RN Tagore), the North/NCR, Mumbai, Jaipur, Raipur and Jammu — plus overseas operations in the Cayman Islands and, more recently, the UK. Consolidated quarterly revenue was about INR 13.4 billion in the June 2024 quarter at roughly a 24% EBITDA margin; growth comes from filling and upgrading existing hospitals plus new greenfield/brownfield builds (e.g. a ~INR 950-1,000 crore first phase in Kolkata's Rajarhat and land bought in Bangalore).

**The parts of the business:**

- **India hospitals (flagship, non-flagship and new hospitals, grouped into regional clusters)** — The core business: multispecialty tertiary/quaternary hospitals whose profits are led by the "flagship" centres in Bangalore and Kolkata (flagship EBITDAR margins around 30%, Bangalore ARPP above INR 250,000), with non-flagship units at roughly 18-19% and newer NCR/Mumbai hospitals starting near breakeven and taking 5-8 years to reach mature margins.
- **Cayman Islands / HCCI (Health City Cayman Islands, overseas subsidiary)** — A hospital business in the Caribbean run by a separate MD, earning around USD 32 million of revenue a quarter at high margins, expanded in 2024 with a new ~50-bed Camana Bay facility focused on day-care and short-stay surgery plus emergency, obstetrics and neonatal services the group didn't previously offer.
- **UK business** — A newer international stream (referred to alongside "Practice Plus") that by FY26 was material enough that management split earnings-call Q&A into 30 minutes on India and 30 minutes on the UK and Cayman; the excerpts give little further detail on its size.
- **NHIC – Narayana Health Integrated Care (clinics)** — A network of outpatient clinics with its own CEO, aimed at primary/preventive and continuing care; still small and in build-out stage (about INR 80 million of revenue and 51,000 patient transactions across 8 clinics in the June 2024 quarter) and being merged into the listed entity to cut costs.
- **Insurance (health insurance / Arya subscription plans)** — An in-house health insurance offering, sold within the listed company, that management frames as a differentiator versus other hospital chains; together with the clinics it is still loss-making, at roughly INR 66 crore of combined losses in FY26 with no breakeven date given.
- **Pharmacy** — Pharmacies operated inside every hospital and clinic to dispense medicines to patients and walk-ins (plus home delivery for Arya subscribers); they sit inside the hospital or NHIC P&L of the listed company, and management has explicitly ruled out a standalone retail pharmacy chain for now.
- **Samyat Healthcare (procurement subsidiary)** — A 100%-owned subsidiary of the listed company that handles procurement on behalf of the group — a support function rather than a revenue line.
- **Digital / technology platform (ATHMA HIS, AADI, NAMAH, NH app, AI tools)** — In-house hospital software, mobile apps and AI tools used to book appointments digitally (over 60% of consultations in deployed units), speed up documentation, flag at-risk patients and improve doctor productivity; described as a driver of efficiency and margins, not sold as a separate product.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Narayana Hrudayalaya comes out of the three reviews as a genuinely well-run hospital group that earns a 66 out of 100 combined score, or four stars — 71 for business quality, 49 for multibagger fit and 76 for risk safety. What customers get is the strongest part of the story: patients travel across India for its bone-marrow transplant work and buy into JCI accreditation in the Caribbean, and the group has deliberately made itself easy to deal with through feeder clinics, its own insurance, more than 60% of consultations booked digitally and pharmacy home delivery. Bangalore Health City has built a near-monopoly position in specific high-end procedures, doing roughly 100 robotic cardiac surgeries a month against peers doing one or two a year, alongside 45 bone-marrow transplant beds. The finances behind this are unusually comfortable: cash comes in from patients 176 days before suppliers have to be paid, so customers effectively fund the working capital, and the balance sheet carried net debt at just 0.05 times equity. Management scores well on both honesty and staying power — it volunteers bad news about Gurgaon, the Mumbai children's hospital and unresolved UK costs rather than dressing them up, and it saw an eight-year Cayman project through a failed start to a working business. That combination is why 'Corporate Culture' is the single strongest pattern in the screen.

The most important thread running through all three reviews is that the same trait produces the best pattern fit and the sharpest limitation. The business is built on high-quality care at a low price at scale — a ₹1 crore surgical cover for around ₹10,000, 'unbeatable price' tertiary care in Cayman, and itemised cost savings down to energy and equipment bills. That earns a likely fit on 'Low Price Plus', but it is exactly why 'Pricing Power' scores no fit at all: headline price rises run only in the low-to-mid single digits, roughly matching cost inflation, and management says plainly that it is not aggressive on pricing. So the improvement in revenue per patient does not come from charging more; it comes from choosing better-paying payors, upgrading bed mix and taking on more complex cases, and even from deliberately holding back volumes. The risk review flags the same thing under 'Good Enough Goods' — with Kolkata conceded to sit permanently below Bangalore's realisations, margin gains have to be engineered through mix rather than price. It also explains why the group scores lowest of all on returns: ₹16 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital, and a brand built on affordability rather than the kind of image that lets a company charge a premium.

The risks are concentration and dependency, and here too the strengths and the exposures share a root. Profits lean heavily on very few assets — the Bangalore and Kolkata flagships drive group margins and a single Cayman hospital carries the overseas business — which is the flip side of the deep clinical scale that gives those sites their near-monopoly position. Government and scheme payors still move the numbers materially: receivable problems and caps on drug reimbursement forced NH to cut scheme volumes outright in the north cluster, which is why the 'Toll Roads' pattern fails — hospital care is a big negotiated line in the payer's budget, not a small unavoidable one. Competition is discussed defensively and repeatedly, with new hospitals in Sarjapur and North Bangalore, newer rivals blamed for a growth shortfall in the north, and Gurgaon a standing problem against larger competitors. The response to all of this — pushing into clinics, its own insurance arm and the UK Practice Plus platform — is the same move the screen credits as forward integration and partial recurring revenue, but it adds two more regulated exposures rather than removing one, and the group's own resilience reading is strained. Cyclicality, technology and changing patient preferences are all judged low risk, because demand is clinical rather than discretionary and NH is leading the shift toward daycare and shorter stays rather than being caught by it.

The verdict, then, is a high-quality operator with real and hard-to-copy clinical assets, a conservative balance sheet and candid management, whose ceiling is set by its own choice to compete on affordability and by how much of its profit sits in a handful of buildings and payor relationships.
