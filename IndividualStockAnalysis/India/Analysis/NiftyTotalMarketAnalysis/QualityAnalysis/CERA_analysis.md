# Cera Sanitaryware (CERA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Cera Sanitaryware is an Indian branded bathroom products company that sells toilets and other ceramic sanitaryware, taps and bathroom fittings (faucetware), tiles, and 'wellness' products such as higher-end bath fixtures. It sells mainly to household consumers doing new homes, renovation and upgradation — reaching them through a network of dealers and retailers (B2C was about 64% of revenue in Q1 FY25) — and also directly to real estate developers and institutions through a B2B projects channel, plus franchise and institutional tie-ups with names like McDonald's, JSW One Homes and Shalby Hospitals. It makes money by manufacturing sanitaryware and faucetware in its own plants in Gujarat (Kadi) and selling under its own brands at a markup, supplementing in-house output with outsourced and a small amount of imported product (imports from China were about 2% of sales in Q1 FY25); profitability swings with raw material costs (clay, brass), natural gas prices, trade discounts and advertising spend. Operations are entirely in India, spread across urban markets and Tier 2/Tier 3 towns, with named focus states including the Northeast, West Bengal, Bihar, Tamil Nadu, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Sanitaryware** — Ceramic bathroom fixtures (toilets, washbasins and similar) made in the company's own plant — the largest business at roughly 46%-54% of quarterly revenue across the calls, with plant capacity utilization ranging from about 76% to over 109%, and a greenfield expansion plant in progress since 2022.
- **Faucetware** — Taps, showers and metal bathroom fittings, also made in-house — the second-largest business at roughly 35%-43% of revenue, with monthly output grown from 1.8 lakh pieces (FY22) to 3 lakh, then 4 lakh after expansion, and 4.3 lakh in March 2026, with a further expansion to 5 lakh pieces planned by Q4 FY27 for about INR 5 crore.
- **Tiles** — Floor and wall tiles sold under the Cera brand, a smaller side category at roughly 9%-12% of revenue.
- **Wellness** — The smallest category at about 1%-2% of revenue, though growing fast in some quarters (up 29.4% year-on-year in Q3 FY26).
- **Cera (flagship brand)** — The main mass and mid-segment brand that management calls the primary driver of volumes and overall business performance.
- **Cera Luxe / Lustre** — Premium and contemporary sub-brands used to push into higher-priced bathroom products; in Q1 FY25, 44% of sales were premium, 32% mid and 24% entry-level.
- **Senator** — A luxury brand sold through a separate flagship-store retail format — 32 stores operational as of Q3 FY26 and 40 by end-FY26, targeting 60 by FY27 — still in the investment and brand-building phase with pre-operating costs weighing on margins.
- **POLIPLUZ** — A newer value-focused brand aimed at price-sensitive markets, distributed through 102 distributors and 1,120 dealers as of FY26 with a target of 200 distributors and 2,000 dealers by end-FY27; like Senator, it is still in buildup phase and not yet a meaningful revenue contributor.
- **B2B / project sales** — Direct supply to real estate developers for residential projects, described as an important support pillar during weak retail demand, with management being selective about projects to protect margins.
- **Retailer loyalty and dealer management programs** — Not a revenue line but a core distribution tool — an app-based retailer rewards scheme with over 28,000 enrolled retailers by Q3 FY26, being linked to a new dealer management system to track secondary sales and channel inventory.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

The core business earns its keep. Every ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.03 of sales, and out of every ₹100 of sales ₹16 is operating profit, with margins moving only about 1.3 points a year — steady enough that the return-on-capital area scores a perfect 1.0. This is genuinely asset-light: faucet output went from 1.8 to 4.3 lakh pieces a month largely through process change rather than heavy spending. Alongside that sits the single strongest scoring item in the framework, the weakening unorganised competitors in the Morbi belt, who are being squeezed by pollution rules, brass costs and gas-availability problems while CERA is insulated by subsidised GAIL gas. That gas is also part of what makes the business hard to copy — along with two decades of captive wind and solar covering 65% of power, a four-decade-old brand and deep tier-2/3 distribution. But the same cheap gas that hands CERA its cost edge is exactly why the government-dependency risk is flagged as one to watch: the supply is administered, covers 64–86% of plant needs, and the alternative Sabarmati gas costs roughly double. One favourable trait is doing double duty here — it is both the widest moat the company has and the concentration point of its cost structure.

The multibagger patterns tell a story of a company that had these qualities and is now struggling to demonstrate them. Almost everything lands at 'partial': pricing power was real in FY22–FY23, when 21% and 26% price increases were absorbed with no volume loss and never rolled back, but from FY25 prices were held flat, discounts widened, and the FY26 increases were purely to recover brass costs and still left a 1–2% gap. The brand pattern shows the same fade — advertising spend hit a record ₹63.2 crore in FY24 and fell to about ₹49 crore in FY26. Market-share gains did not persist either: revenue was flat from FY24 to FY26 and the ₹2,900 crore FY27 target is described as far out of reach, though faucetware still grew 18.2% and 24.3% in the last two quarters of FY26 in a weak market. The reason these patterns stall is the same as two of the named risks. The unorganised cohort that CERA is taking share from is also the 'good enough goods' threat — it is still roughly 45% of the category, the purchase runs through dealers, retailers and plumbers whom CERA must actively pay to influence through a 28,000-retailer loyalty programme, and FY26 margins fell. That paid-for channel influence is precisely why the 'friendly middleman' pattern only rates partial: rising trade discounts show the channel has to be bought, not owned. And the cyclicality risk, rated elevated, explains the flat revenue directly — demand is tied to the residential property cycle and to developer projects now at 39–40% of revenue, which produced a boom quarter in Q1 FY23 (+77.6% revenue, operating margin jumping from 8.9% to 15.5%) followed by three stagnant years.

Capital allocation is the weakest area at -0.4, and it comes from the same conservatism that makes the company safe. Being debt-free with ₹853 crore of cash, never diluting shareholders, never acquiring anything, and tightening the working-capital cycle from 78 to 64 net days is what earns the sound resilience verdict and is the stated cushion against the cyclicality risk — countercyclical advertising and roughly 60% consumer replacement demand rather than pure project spending. But the flip side is chronic under-investment: the greenfield sanitaryware plant announced in FY22 drifted for years, with land still under due diligence in FY25, and by FY26 spending had collapsed to routine maintenance of about ₹14.5 crore against that ₹853 crore pile. The one buyback, ₹130 crore at ₹12,000 a share in August 2024, was done near peak valuation and was small against an ever-growing idle balance with no stated distribution policy. Cash also stays stuck for about 140 days between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. This same gap between what is promised and what is delivered — the 16% growth and ₹2,900 crore by March 2027 guidance — is what holds the culture pattern to partial and the management area to zero. The overall rating of 54 out of 100, or 'Decent', comes out of exactly this split: real safety (78 on risk) sitting on modest business quality (55) and weak fit with the compounding patterns (33). The near-term encouragement is that the cycle may be turning, with sequential growth of 11.1% and 11.4% in the last two quarters of FY26 and a continued surge in April 2026.

## What to watch

- Whether the GAIL gas allocation holds — the government-dependency risk names it as an administered supply covering 64–86% of plant needs at roughly half the price of Sabarmati gas, and it underpins both the 'share donators' check and the cost-to-replicate pattern.
- Whether the ₹853 crore of cash finally gets deployed — the stalled greenfield plant and the ₹14.5 crore maintenance-only spend drive the -1 on capex for growth, and a real project or a stated payout policy would lift the whole capital-allocation area.
- Whether discounts stop widening and prices start rising ahead of costs again — the pricing power pattern fell from full strength to partial on flat prices, wider discounts and cost-recovery-only FY26 increases, and it is the same channel pressure behind the 'good enough goods' risk.
- Whether the recovery in Q3/Q4 FY26 (11.1% and 11.4%) turns into actual revenue growth rather than another false start — revenue was flat FY24 to FY26, the ₹2,900 crore FY27 target is already out of reach, and the elevated cyclicality risk means this can reverse with the property cycle.
- Whether the Senator store rollout (32 stores, targeting 60) and Polipluz's distributor network stop being a cost drag and start showing results — the forward-integration pattern is currently rated partial precisely because there is no disclosed payoff yet, and the advertising cut from ₹63.2 crore to ₹49 crore pulls the other way.
