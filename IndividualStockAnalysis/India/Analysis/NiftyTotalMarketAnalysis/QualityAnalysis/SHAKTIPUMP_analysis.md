# Shakti Pumps (SHAKTIPUMP) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Shakti Pumps (India) Limited makes and sells water pumps — especially solar-powered pumps for farmers — along with the motors, variable frequency drives (VFDs) and mounting structures that go with them, and it installs them as complete systems. Most of its money comes from India's government solar-pump schemes, chiefly PM-KUSUM (Component B/2.0) and state programmes such as Maharashtra's Magel Tyala, where the company wins tenders, installs pumps for farmers and is paid largely by state governments; that dependence shows up as very large receivables (about INR 1,276 crores at March 2026). It also exports pumps through dealers and distributors, and sells for cash through a retail dealer network in India. Consolidated revenue was a record INR 2,698 crores in FY26 with roughly 16% EBITDA margin, on 86,086 solar pumps installed, and the order book was about INR 1,000 crores as of July 2026; newer lines in solar rooftop systems and EV parts are still small.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Solar pumps / Solar EPC (PM-KUSUM and state schemes)** — The core business: winning government tenders and supplying plus installing solar-powered irrigation pumps for farmers, which management calls its "core growth engine" — 86,086 units installed in FY26 and 27,678 in Q1 FY27, and the source of nearly all revenue.
- **Exports** — Sells pumps overseas through a dealer and distributor network; described as a steady but much smaller contributor (about Rs 41 crores in a quarter back in FY23), and recently hit by Middle East geopolitical delays.
- **Cash / retail domestic sales** — Direct dealer-network sales to domestic customers who pay cash rather than through government schemes — small but growing at INR 24 crores in Q1 FY27, and valued because it improves working capital.
- **Solar Agri (conventional agricultural pumps)** — The older, non-EPC agricultural pump line, roughly Rs 22-23 crores a quarter in FY23 and flat at that time.
- **Component-C / grid-connected solar pumps** — Connecting farmers' pumps to the electricity grid so surplus power can be exported back to the DISCOM; started as a ~Rs 150 crore pilot in Ajmer, Rajasthan and is patent-backed but still early-stage.
- **Solar rooftop (Shakti Energy Solutions Limited)** — A newer subsidiary selling rooftop solar systems built around Shakti's own inverters, registered under the PM Surya Ghar scheme — INR 8 crores of revenue in Q1 FY27 versus INR 2 crores a year earlier.
- **EV parts** — An early diversification into electric-vehicle components, currently only at trial-order stage with new customers and contributing no meaningful revenue yet.
- **In-house manufacturing and backward integration** — Makes its own pumps, motors, VFDs and solar structures and is expanding capacity, plus building a 0.5 GW DCR solar module plant and a 2.2 GW integrated DCR cell-and-module project to supply its own panels.

### The story in depth

Shakti Pumps has real strengths, and they are mostly about volume and engineering rather than profit. It installs more solar pumps than anyone else in its schemes — 46% of KUSUM-2 installations in 2022, 86,086 pumps in FY26 (up 20%) and a 57.6% year-on-year jump in the June 2027 quarter — which is why the Market Share Gainers pattern is the one clear fit in the whole screen. Behind that sits genuine farmer-level brand pull (a Maharashtra portal for 25,000 pumps filled in 25 minutes) and a steady engineering record: 14 patents granted out of 29 filed, a patented pump that exports power back to the grid, and in-house motors, controllers and inverters that customers say generate about 10% more. Management also scores well on behaviour — growth has been organic with no acquisitions, capex is funded from equity and internal accruals rather than debt, and they walked away from a Haryana order and INR 200 crores of Maharashtra work rather than take losses, while cutting receivables by INR 420 crores and 77 days after promising to. The end market itself is genuinely expanding, with 17.5 lakh pumps sanctioned under the clean-energy push.

The problem is that the very thing producing those strengths is also the main source of danger, and the records say so in both directions. Around 78% of FY25 revenue came from government, the order book is described as mostly government business, and prices are set by lowest-bidder (L1) rate contracts — so the schemes that hand Shakti its volumes also hand the buyer maximum negotiating power. That single fact explains why four patterns fail outright: there is no pricing power (FY23 price rises did not cover raw-material inflation, and by FY26–27 a roughly 6% input cost hit could not be passed on), no toll-road economics (the pump is the farmer's whole capital item, not a small unavoidable input), no friendly middleman (price, not expert recommendation, decides the sale), and no recurring revenue (every sale is one-time equipment or project work with no service contracts or renewals). It also explains three of the four high or elevated risks — cyclicality, government dependency and buyer concentration — since revenue swings with state budgets and portals rather than with customers' day-to-day operations: quarterly revenue went from INR 113 crores to INR 568 crores in a year, and operating margins travelled 7% to 24% and back down to about 10%. The brand pull is real but, as the screen puts it, it delivers volume and demand rather than price. On the checks, industry structure is the worst-scoring area at the floor of the scale: entry is open, management openly welcomes new players, margins swing by nearly six points, and growth is entirely volume-led with per-pump realisation essentially flat (INR 248,153 against INR 248,374) and a further 3–4% knocked off by Magel Tyala tenders.

Financially, this shows up as a business that works hard for little return, which is why the combined rating lands at 30 out of 100, or Weak, and resilience is judged strained. About 134 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, receivables stand at INR 1,276 crores in place of the customer advances a stronger business model would collect, and every INR 100 of capital employed produced just INR 6 of operating cash. The four-year record is the clearest verdict on durability: new entrants arrived, tender prices fell and margins more than halved, meaning 40 years of brand and 14 patents did not stop well-funded competitors from competing the economics away — the cost-to-replicate test fails. That said, the records also point to a possible turn rather than a terminal decline. The company appears to sit near a visible cycle trough, with KUSUM 2.0 awaiting clearance from the Prime Minister's Office, backward integration into VFDs, structures and 2.2 GW of solar cells expected to add roughly 3% to operating margin, and early diversification into exports, rooftop solar, a cash-and-dealer retail business (INR 24 crores in the June 2027 quarter) and EV parts — all still small next to scheme revenue. The underlying demand shift also runs in the company's favour: diesel-to-solar substitution is accelerating with top-level political backing, and farmers buy for economic payback rather than fashion, so the risk here is about who captures the profit from that growth, not whether the growth exists.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality, Government Dependency, Stakeholder Concentration, Good Enough Goods. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ✅ Borrowings are 0.3× shareholders' funds — the balance sheet is not the problem.
- ⚠️ Only about 16% of recent operating profit became cash (was 65%) — profits are getting stuck in paper, an early stress signal.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 13% to 37% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Revenue and margins swing violently with scheme flow and input costs: Q1FY24 revenue Rs 113cr vs Q1FY25 Rs 568cr, EBITDA margin 7% → 24% → ~10% by FY26/Q1FY27; demand is tied to state budget cycles and government capex programmes rather than customer operations, and management repeatedly explains results by copper/steel/solar-panel prices. Management itself labels the FY26 squeeze 'cyclical'.

> "EBITDA margins faced pressure from lower realizations under the Magel Tyala scheme, a sharp increase in raw material prices and elevated logistic and freight costs" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Countercyclical investment continues (0.5GW and 2.2GW cell/module capex, capacity expansion) and a small but growing cash/retail dealer business (Rs 24cr in Q1FY27) that is operations- rather than tender-linked.*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±51 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 3% and 24% (±5.7 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 4% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 10%).

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** The business is essentially a policy vehicle: ~78% of revenue from government in FY25, order book described as 'B2G business, mostly government-related', pricing set by rate-contract/L1 tenders, execution gated by state budgets, portals, elections and DISCOM payment cycles, and the growth story hangs on when PMO clears KUSUM 2.0. Receivables of Rs 1,276cr (with Rs 560cr over 180 days) and orders walked away from in Haryana/Maharashtra show the working-capital cost of the dependency.

> "PM KUSUM 2.0 is ready for launch and could be announced as early as next week or next month. It is currently with the PMO and awaiting clearance from there." — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Partial diversification underway — exports, cash/retail dealer sales, rooftop and EV parts — but all still small relative to scheme revenue.*

### Stakeholder Concentration — HIGH RISK
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 1 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Demand is concentrated in a handful of state schemes (Maharashtra Magel Tyala, Rajasthan, Haryana, UP) with one buyer type — government/DISCOMs — and supply of the largest input was concentrated in a few named panel vendors on whom pricing and penalty exposure depended.

> "we were dependent on our vendors like Premier, Tata, and Adani for panel supply quantities and pricing" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Backward integration into panels/cells, VFDs, inverters and structures removes the supplier chokepoint; dealer/distributor network and export markets (Uganda, Middle East, Africa) are broadening the customer base.*

- ⚠️ Customers now take about 168 days to pay, up from 113 — lengthening collection terms are how weak bargaining power against concentrated customers shows up in the numbers.

## What to watch

- Realisation per pump: it is flat sequentially and already down 3–4% on Magel Tyala tenders — a further fall would confirm the failed Pricing Power and Cost to Replicate patterns and undercut the Market Share Gainers fit, since share is being defended on price.
- Whether KUSUM 2.0 clears the Prime Minister's Office: the entire 'cyclical market growth' check rests on this upturn arriving, and if it slips, the Cyclicality and Government Dependency risks (both high) simply repeat.
- Working capital: receivables of INR 1,276 crores and a 134-day cash cycle are the core weakness — holding the INR 420 crore / 77-day improvement would validate the Corporate Culture pattern, while a relapse would break management's main evidence of promises kept.
- Whether backward integration actually delivers the roughly 3% margin gain claimed under the Low Price Plus pattern; that is the one lever inside the company's control against the New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks.
- Non-government revenue — exports, rooftop, cash/retail (INR 24 crores) and EV parts: growth here is the only route to lowering the ~78% government revenue share and lifting the 6% cash return on capital.
