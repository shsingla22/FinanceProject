# Wework India Managem (WEWORK) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

WeWork India rents large Grade-A office buildings from landlords on long leases, fits them out into finished, ready-to-use offices, and then sells them as flexible workspace to companies of all sizes — enterprises, global capability centres (GCCs), Indian corporates, startups, SMEs and freelancers — without the customer taking on a long lease or upfront capital spend. It makes money mainly from recurring membership/desk fees for that space (revenue of about INR 698 crores in the quarter ended June 2026, roughly INR 2,477 crores for FY26), plus smaller streams from digital workspace subscriptions and add-on services such as parking, facilities, customisation and events. As of Q1 FY27 it runs 79 centres across eight Indian cities, about 9.1 million square feet and 133,600 desks, serving roughly 113,000 members at about 85% occupancy. Enterprise customers drive about 77% of revenue, and management describes the company as the largest branded flexible-workspace network in India, backed by the Embassy Group and WeWork Global.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Workspace-as-a-Service (core operations / core workspace)** — The main business of leasing, fitting out and renting furnished desks and offices — from a single desk to whole floors — under the WeWork brand; it is by far the largest part, at INR 603 crores of the INR 698 crore quarterly revenue (about 86%) in Q1 FY27, growing about 30% year-on-year.
- **Managed Office** — Custom, single-tenant offices built and run for a specific client, started only once the client has committed, so space fills immediately; management says it scaled to 26,000 desks across 1.7 million square feet in two years and contributed about 21% of revenue (roughly INR 530 crores annualised) as of Q3 FY26.
- **Digital Products (WeWork All Access, On-Demand, Workplace, Virtual Office)** — Subscription and pay-per-use digital products that let members book space across the network or hold a business address — small at under 4% of revenue (INR 26 crores in Q1 FY27) but very profitable at close to an 80% EBITDA margin, since they monetise the same square foot more than once.
- **Value-Added Services (VAS)** — Extra charges on top of the desk fee — parking, facilities, office customisation work and late fees — worth about INR 86 crores in Q3 FY26; it is lumpy because large customisation jobs come in bursts (INR 47 crores one quarter versus INR 9.5 crores the next).

### The story in depth

WeWork India comes out of these three reviews as a genuinely good business that has recently been run well, rated Strong at 66 out of 100. The quality framework's highest marks all point the same way: money spent on new centres and fit-outs is growth spending rather than upkeep — ₹456cr in FY26, guided to ₹500-600cr in FY27 — and the returns on it are showing up, with return on capital employed tripling to 28.6% and touching 45.1% in the fourth quarter, while the business generates ₹30 of operating cash for every ₹100 of capital and keeps ₹64 of every ₹100 of sales as operating profit. That spending has been funded out of the business itself: net debt of ₹529cr has turned into net cash, borrowing costs have fallen from 15.4% to 8.5%, credit ratings have moved up two to three notches, and only about 13 days of cash sits tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Sales have compounded at roughly 23% a year with little variation, and the market it sells into is genuinely expanding — flexible workspace stock has tripled in five years past 100 million square feet toward a projected 257 million by 2030, and flexible space has more than doubled its share of new office leasing to 27% in five quarters.

The multibagger screen finds the same traits, and this is where the picture gets more interesting: almost every pattern it identifies is a 'likely' or 'partial' fit rather than a confirmed one, and the reason a pattern falls short is usually the same reason a risk is flagged. The business is structurally a forward integrator — it leases plain Grade A shells from landlords and sells finished, ready-to-use workspace directly to occupiers through 79 centres and its own app, with Rivet extending that fit-out capability to enterprises and landlords — and that same model produces the cost discipline the screen calls Low Price Plus: rent per square foot up just 0.9% for FY26 and flat in the first quarter of FY27, overheads pinned near 8% of revenue, capex per desk at an all-time low of ₹1.3 lakh, and breakeven occupancy around 55%. But because the underlying assets are leased rather than owned and the format itself is not hard to copy, the Cost to Replicate pattern stays partial and the New Entrants risk is on watch, with management describing its own position as being one of the 'top one to two contenders' won deal-by-deal through competitive tenders rather than as a structural moat — which is also why the framework scores industry structure negatively. Pricing tells the same double story: across all four calls price is described as held rather than raised, with revenue-to-rent multiples of 2.6-3.0x maintained through record volume and no discounting, which reads as pricing resilience under the Pricing Power pattern and simultaneously as the defence against the Good Enough Goods risk, since a serviced desk in a Grade A building is not a complex product and cheaper regional operators, landlord-run suites and plain direct leases are credible substitutes. Brand works the same way — a well-known name, NPS of 78-79, over 110,000 members and more than half of desk sales coming from existing members expanding — but the name is licensed from WeWork Global, which is exactly what keeps the Brand Strength pattern at 'likely' rather than confirmed, and the framework separately marks the company down heavily for being the opposite of overlooked: newly listed, carrying one of the most publicised global brand names in its category, and covered by multiple brokers.

The risk review classifies the company's resilience as strained: seven of eight risk channels sit on watch and only fashion risk is judged low, because demand here is functional and contracted rather than taste-driven — enterprises sign 28 to 33 month commitments, renewals run at 84%, and growth is never credited to a trend. What holds the watch-list together is that the very shift powering today's growth is also the thing that could reverse. Enterprises abandoning long leases — five-year-plus commitments collapsing from 40% of enterprises to 8% — is what fills these centres now, but it is a live migration in preferences rather than a settled state. Demand is tied to office headcount, which is why questions about artificial intelligence and remote work were the single most-asked investor topic of FY26, enough that management commissioned an outside study to answer them; the company's response has been steady small-scale innovation rather than big bets, with the app and digital products such as All Access, On-Demand and Virtual Office contributing roughly ₹82cr in FY26 at about 80% margin — the same launches that earn the Innovation Dominance pattern a 'likely' verdict, though without any disclosed research spending behind them. The cushion against all of this is the contracted book: locked-in core revenue of ₹3,363cr, up 60% and growing at roughly 4.7 times the rate of locked-in rent, average commitment tenure stretching from 23 to 33 months for large enterprises, and customer deposits acting as a funding source — the evidence behind the partial Recurring Revenue fit. Concentration is disclosed as low (top 10 members about 22% of core revenue, no dominant sector, 113,000 members), yet individual quarters visibly swing on a handful of named mega-deals, with JP Morgan, T-Mobile and Amazon customisations producing ₹47cr in one period. On balance: real returns, real cost discipline and a real tailwind, sitting on leased assets in a format that is easy to enter and priced against good-enough alternatives.

## What to watch

- Whether return on capital holds near the 28.6% level and the 45.1% fourth-quarter mark as capex steps up to the guided ₹500-600cr — this is the single check ('Capex for growth, not maintenance') doing most of the work in the Strong rating.

- Locked-in revenue (₹3,363cr), the 84% renewal rate and average commitment tenure — if these stop growing faster than contracted rent, the cushion under the Cyclicality, Shifting Consumer Preferences and Technological Innovation watch items thins out.
