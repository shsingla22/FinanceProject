# Websol Energy System (WEBELSOLAR) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Websol Energy System is an Indian manufacturer of solar photovoltaic cells and solar modules (panels), with a factory operation it has been running for about 30 years and a new integrated plant planned in Andhra Pradesh. It makes money by producing solar cells and then either selling those cells to other module manufacturers or feeding them into its own module line and selling finished modules — almost entirely domestic-content-requirement (DCR) product, sold to Indian buyers such as module makers and solar pump players who in turn supply government schemes like PM-KUSUM and PM Surya Ghar. Cell prices run about 13-13.5 US cents per watt and module prices about 22-22.5 cents per watt, with cells carrying structurally higher margins than modules. FY26 revenue was Rs. 1,049 crores (up ~82%), EBITDA Rs. 429 crores (41% margin) and PAT Rs. 303 crores, with a confirmed order book of Rs. 1,161 crores it expects to deliver within roughly one financial year.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Solar cell manufacturing (Phase-1 Cell Line-1 and Phase-2 Cell Line-2)** — Two Mono PERC cell lines of 600 MW each, taking cell capacity from 600 MW to about 1.2 GW after Line-2 was commissioned in September 2025 (funded fully from internal accruals); this is the higher-margin half of the business, ran at about 90% utilisation in Q4 FY26, and cells were about 40% of the closing order book.
- **Solar module manufacturing** — A 550 MW module line that absorbs roughly 600 MW of the company's own cells and sells the finished DCR modules; utilisation rose from 64% in Q3 to a peak monthly 80%, and modules were about 60% of the Rs. 1,161 crore order book — bigger in revenue now, but with structurally lower margins that pulled blended EBITDA down.
- **TOPCon upgrade of an existing Mono PERC cell line** — An in-progress conversion (adding equipment rather than scrapping the line) costing about Rs. 250-270 crores, lifting that line from 600 MW to 750 MW and total cell capacity to 1.35 GW, targeting over 24.5% efficiency and G12R cell sizes, with commercial production expected February 2027 and only about half a month of production disruption.
- **Phase-3 / Andhra Pradesh expansion (Websol Renewables, wholly owned subsidiary)** — An approved integrated 4 GW cell-and-module facility in Andhra Pradesh to be built in 2 GW phases, with land allotted and a state incentive package approved; the first 2 GW TOPCon phase was costed at Rs. 1,600-1,700 crores on a 70:30 debt-equity basis in the February call, though by April management said it was still finalising sequencing, funding and timelines.
- **Backward integration into ingots and wafers (MOU with Linton)** — An early-stage feasibility tie-up with equipment technology provider Linton to explore making PV ingots and wafers in India — at the technical evaluation and R&D stage only, with no revenue attached, intended to give the company more control over its raw material chain.

### The story in depth

Websol earns money well today. Out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹41 is operating profit, and for every ₹100 of capital in the business it generated ₹34 of operating cash this year, with headline ROCE cited at 66%. The way that capital was raised matters as much as the return on it: the 600 MW Cell Line-2 was commissioned in under a year entirely out of money the business itself generated, the company turned net cash surplus with debt-to-equity falling from 0.55x to 0.19x, no acquisitions have been attempted, and management deliberately slowed the announcement of the 4 GW Andhra Pradesh facility to get the sequencing and funding right. That is why the two strongest scores in the framework are Management (0.83) and Return on Capital (1.33), and why the risk review still calls overall resilience SOUND — the balance sheet went into this expansion with strength rather than borrowings. The end market is genuinely expanding too: India has 150 GW installed and rising, and ALMM List-2, PM-KUSUM and PM Surya Ghar require domestically made cells.

The problem is that almost every strength traces back to the same two sources — adding capacity, and a government mandate — and those same two things are what the risk review flags as dangerous. Revenue rose 82% to ₹1,049 crore, but the framework scores Pricing, Mix and Volume at -2 because none of that came from charging more or selling a better mix: cell realisation fell from about 14 cents to 13-13.5 cents and module from 22.5-23 to 22-22.5 cents even as silver costs rose, and the mix shifted toward structurally lower-margin modules. So Market Share Gainers is only a PARTIAL fit (real volume, but no market-share figure and no named-competitor comparison), while Pricing Power is a flat NO FIT — the record shows price deflation, not price setting. The policy mandate that makes demand visible is also what makes Government Dependency a HIGH RISK: essentially the whole order book is DCR product whose demand and price premium exist only because of ALMM, the June 2026 DCR notification and the pump schemes, and the framework separately docks Geographical expansion to -1 for zero export exposure, Europe being aspirational talk and the US written off on tariffs. The single trait — being a protected domestic supplier into a mandated niche — creates the pattern and the risk at once.

The remaining patterns land the same way: something real is there, but it is not the durable kind. Toll Roads is PARTIAL because the regulatory anchoring is genuine but a solar cell is the dominant cost of a module rather than a trivial 1-2%, so buyers have every reason to push back on price. Innovation Dominance is a LIKELY FIT on cadence — 23.6% peak cell efficiency within three months of Line-2, a PERC-to-TOPCon upgrade targeting above 24.5%, PhD hiring, a 25% cut in silver consumption and an ingot/wafer MOU — yet the same technology treadmill is a HIGH RISK: management has cut the useful life of Mono PERC assets from 10-12 years to 3 years and is spending ₹250-270 crore to convert a line it built recently, so R&D here is process work on bought-in equipment rather than owned technology. Cost to Replicate is NO FIT because 30 years of experience and ramp-up difficulty are a timing lead, not an un-replicable asset, and management itself concedes large announced capacities are coming and that today's 40%+ margins are cycle-peak. Add the working capital check at -1 (about 111 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers) and a narrow customer base — module makers and solar-pump players, where an industry liquidity squeeze already deferred an offtake — and the arithmetic follows: 45% × 57 on business quality, 30% × 11 on multibagger fit, 25% × 12 on risk safety gives 32 out of 100, a Weak one-star rating. This is a well-run, well-funded, high-returning business operating inside a protected window, and the rating is a judgement about how long that window stays open, not about how the company is being managed.
