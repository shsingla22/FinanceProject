# IMFA (IMFA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Indian Metals & Ferro Alloys Limited (IMFA) is an Odisha-based metals and mining company that makes ferrochrome, an alloy that stainless steel mills melt into their furnaces, and it describes itself as India's leading fully integrated ferrochrome producer. "Fully integrated" means it does the whole chain itself: it digs chrome ore out of its own mines (Sukinda and Mahagiri, with leases running to 2049 and 2055), burns coal in its own captive power plants to make the large amounts of electricity smelting requires, and smelts the ore into ferrochrome at its plants at Therubali and Choudwar, with 190 MVA of furnace capacity and about 204.55 MW of captive power before recent expansion. It earns money by selling roughly 60,000–68,000 tonnes of ferrochrome per quarter, mostly on long-term offtake contracts with prices reset monthly or quarterly rather than spot deals, at realizations that ranged from about INR 87,000 to INR 1,21,000 per tonne over the period discussed; profit is essentially the gap between that price and its cost of ore, coke and power. Around 90% of sales are exports with about 10% domestic (management says it is now pivoting toward more domestic sales), and it is growing through a new Kalinganagar plant plus the INR 610 crore purchase of Tata Steel's Kalinganagar ferrochrome plant, which management says makes it India's largest ferrochrome maker and sixth largest globally with capacity above 0.5 million tonnes.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Ferro Alloys Business Unit** — The core money-maker: smelts chrome ore into ferrochrome and sells it, mainly exported to stainless steel producers under long-term contracts, running at roughly 63,000–68,500 tonnes of production a quarter and driving essentially all of the reported revenue and profit.
- **Mines Business Unit** — Runs the company's own chrome ore mines (Sukinda and Mahagiri), raising roughly 1.58 lakh to 2.65 lakh tonnes of chrome ore per quarter to feed the smelters — it is a cost-control and security-of-supply arm rather than a separate sales business, and management says the reserves last as long as the leases, to 2049 and 2055.
- **Power Business Unit** — Generates about 256–260 million units of captive electricity a quarter from coal-fired plants (plus 4.55 MWp of solar) to run the furnaces; since smelting consumes roughly 4,000 units of power per tonne of ferrochrome, coal and power cost swings are one of the biggest levers on profitability.
- **Kalinganagar greenfield project ("KNR 1")** — A newly built smelting plant with furnaces being switched on around July 2026 — an expansion of ferrochrome capacity, not yet contributing to results in the period covered.
- **Kalinganagar acquisition from Tata Steel ("KNR 2")** — The purchase of Tata Steel's Kalinganagar ferrochrome plant for a base INR 610 crores, adding 99 MVA of furnace capacity; the deal closed 27 February 2026 and it contributed about 2,200 tonnes in Q4 FY26 and is running at roughly 6,000 tonnes a month thereafter.
- **Ethanol project** — A small diversification outside metals, delayed several times and now expected to commission in Q2 FY27, with management saying it will start adding to the top line only from FY27.
- **Renewable power tie-ups** — Contracted hybrid renewable supply of 70 MW from JSW Energy and 65 MW from Enfinity Global, totalling 135 MW, intended to cut carbon emissions and lock in stable power prices rather than to be sold as a business.

### The story in depth

IMFA is a fully integrated ferrochrome producer whose clearest strength is the people running it. Management scores at the top of the quality framework, and the same evidence drives the one pattern that is a STRONG FIT: corporate culture. The promoter-led team has been in place across FY22–FY26, disclosure is consistently candid (they volunteer delays and their causes, admit underground mining will raise costs, and refuse to guess at the Supreme Court mineral-tax outcome), and the growth has been funded conservatively — INR170 crore of a INR470 crore term loan was left undrawn, with the rest coming from internal accruals. Underneath that sits a genuine cost culture — captive chrome ore, captive thermal power, cheaper coal linkages worth INR400–500 a tonne, rail rakes, 135 MW of hybrid renewables — but the framework and the screen agree this only gets partial credit, because a low-cost position in a commodity is a cushion, not a moat. Competitive advantage is the weakest quality area of all, and the screen rejects pricing power, brand, recurring revenue and toll-road economics outright.

The growth story and the share-gain story are the same story, and it is currently working. Sustained growth capex — the KNR1 greenfield with two furnaces, the INR610 crore purchase of Tata Steel's Kalinganagar plant (closed February 2026 and already producing about 6,000 tonnes a month within weeks), plus ethanol and an underground mine — takes output from roughly 265,000 tonnes in FY26 toward about 400,000 in FY27 and 500,000 in FY28. IMFA is tiny against a roughly 16 million tonne world market, so there is room, and the supply it is stepping into is being vacated by others: global ferrochrome output fell 9.4%, South African producers shut capacity permanently, Indian non-integrated smelters cut back on ore costs, and Tata Steel simply exited by selling to IMFA. That is why 'share donators' scores at the top and 'market share gainers' is a likely fit. But the expansion runs straight into the single biggest risk. Ferrochrome is priced off the Samancor/Merafe benchmark with a negotiated discount; management itself attributes profit swings to price alone, and realisations moved from INR89,000 to INR121,000 in Q1FY23, down to about INR87,000 by Q4FY25, then back to INR118,000–120,000 in FY26. Cyclicality is rated HIGH RISK, and every extra tonne of new capacity is another tonne exposed to a price the company does not set.

The same traits that protect IMFA also expose it. The captive Sukinda and Mahagiri chrome ore leases running to 2049 and 2055 are the one asset a funded newcomer cannot simply buy — the reason 'cost to replicate' earns partial credit — yet those leases are exactly why government dependency is rated HIGH RISK: state-granted mineral rights, royalties that rise with notified ore prices, coal e-auction costs, and an unquantified mineral-tax judgment. Likewise, the retreat of weak competitors that hands IMFA share is the same industry reset flagged under new entrants (WATCH): Chinese ferrochrome output rose from roughly 650,000 to 950,000 tonnes a month on imported South African ore, and South Africa approved special ore export pricing — a cost-curve shift that hurts non-integrated players more than IMFA, but reshapes the industry regardless. Customer and supply concentration is a WATCH for the same reason it is being fixed: around 90% of sales were exports to a handful of named relationships, and metallurgical coke comes from Colombia, described as a limited source — the deliberate move toward a 60-40 export-domestic mix, worth about 200,000 tonnes to Indian mills, is both the 'geographical expansion' strength and the mitigation for that risk. Finally, about 218 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, the framework's worst working-capital score, which makes a volume-led expansion hungrier for cash than the headline tonnage suggests. Resilience is judged SOUND and the business is well run, but with six of eleven multibagger patterns showing no fit at all, the combined rating lands at 46 out of 100 — Mixed, two stars: a capable operator riding a cycle rather than a compounding franchise.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 1 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Ferrochrome is a benchmark-priced commodity and the calls show earnings swinging with it across the full 5 years — realization moved 89k→121k (Q1FY23), fell to ~87k by Q4FY25, then back to 118k–120k in FY26, with coke, thermal coal and power costs swinging in parallel; management repeatedly refuses to forecast beyond one quarter. Long-term offtake contracts fix tonnage but not price (repriced monthly/quarterly), so they smooth volume, not margin, and the company is simultaneously adding ~1,000 cr of capacity (KNR1, KNR2, underground mine) into a price upcycle.

> "the market is so volatile and so many factors are impacting, so many things are happening in the world today, so very difficult to estimate or give any prediction for Q3" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow-product demand — ferrochrome is a consumable input to ongoing stainless-steel operations, not customer capex; ~70-75% sold on long-term contracts/offtake with assured tonnage and virtually no spot sales; fully integrated captive ore and power keeps IMFA a top-five lowest-cost producer, and it expanded (KNR1, KNR2 acquisition) while non-integrated peers cut back*

- ✅ Sales fell in only 3 of the last 11 years with swings of ±17 points — no pronounced boom-and-bust signature in revenue.
- ✅ Operating margin stayed between 6% and 31% (±7.2 points a year) — swings within the normal range, no extreme boom-and-bust profile.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 47% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 5% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure; this risk has no numeric fingerprint to cross-check, so it is judged from the calls alone.

**What the calls show:** The business is anchored in state-granted mineral rights and policy-set input costs: mine leases valid to 2049/2055, chrome royalty rising with notified ore prices, the Supreme Court mineral-rights tax ruling that management could not quantify, coal e-auction vs linkage pricing, environmental clearances gating the KNR2 33 MVA furnace, and a ~INR370 crore claim stuck with the government over the Utkal 'C' coal block for years. Industry pricing is also being reset by a foreign government's utility subsidy (Eskom/NERSA tariff for Glencore and Samancor) — exactly the mechanism the risk describes.

> "very difficult to quantify anything at the moment because judgment itself is not fully pronounced, whether it will be retrospective or prospective" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Revenue itself is not sold to government or set by an Indian regulator; captive mines and captive power reduce reliance on state-allocated inputs, and renewable PPAs (135 MW hybrid) plus fresh coal linkages give 25-29 year price stability*
