# InterGlobe Aviation Ltd. (INDIGO) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: SERVICES*

IndiGo (InterGlobe Aviation Ltd.) is an Indian low-cost airline that sells seats on scheduled passenger flights, mainly to ordinary Indian travellers flying within India plus a growing number flying abroad. It earns money by filling a large fleet of single-aisle Airbus A320/A321 aircraft at low fares and high load factors — revenue is described in terms of yield (fare per passenger-kilometre) and unit revenue per seat-kilometre, against a cost base dominated by jet fuel and dollar-linked lease and maintenance costs, which is why fuel prices and the rupee-dollar rate swing its profits so much. Its home market is India, where it flew to 88 domestic destinations over 400+ direct routes (FY24) and served more than 123 million passengers in FY26, alongside about 100 international routes to 33 overseas destinations across Asia, Africa, the Middle East and Europe. Total income was around 895 billion rupees in FY26 (versus about 712 billion in FY24), and results have swung from large post-Covid losses to an 82 billion rupee profit in FY24 and back to a 23.9 billion rupee loss in FY26 after currency depreciation and a December operational disruption.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Domestic and short-haul passenger flying (the core airline)** — Scheduled low-fare flights connecting Indian cities — management calls it "the heart of IndiGo," anchored by the Airbus A320/A321 single-aisle programme, with the stated intent to fly to every Indian airport of a certain size (over 10 million domestic passengers carried in May 2026 alone, its best-ever month).
- **International network / "internationalization"** — Flights from Indian cities to overseas destinations across Asia, Africa, the Middle East, the UK and Europe, expanding via new routes and larger aircraft (A321XLR and 30 ordered A350-900 widebodies); the Middle East and Europe together were said to be roughly 18% of total capacity and about 160 daily flights in FY26.
- **Codeshare partnerships** — Tie-ups with foreign carriers such as British Airways, Qantas and Malaysia Airlines that let IndiGo sell onward connections beyond its own network — passengers flown through codeshares grew about 45% in FY24, though it is small relative to the main flying business.
- **Premium / business product** — A tailor-made business-class-style cabin launched on the busiest business routes from late 2024 to capture growing demand for premium travel in India — an add-on to the low-cost core rather than a separate business.
- **CarGo (air cargo / freighters)** — A freight operation that started commercial flying with its first two dedicated freighter aircraft in October 2022 — described as an exciting opportunity but a small side business next to passenger flying.
- **Engine MRO and material services** — A newer, forward-looking effort to build in-house engine maintenance, repair and overhaul plus long-term parts supply, supported by a 2026 memorandum with CFM International for over 1,000 LEAP-1A engines; presented as investment ahead of growth rather than a current earner.
- **Fleet and aircraft order book** — Not a revenue line but central to the story: a pending order book of close to 1,000 aircraft for delivery out to 2035 (including a record 500-aircraft A320neo family order), giving at least one new plane a week and a target of a 600-plus aircraft fleet by the end of the decade.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

IndiGo's appeal starts with reach and cost. The quality framework gives its best marks to distribution and convenience: 97 domestic and 45 international destinations, 400+ direct routes, a presence at effectively every meaningful Indian airport, first-mover positions at new airports, and an 11 million member BluChip loyalty ecosystem tied to banking, hotel and lifestyle partners. That footprint sits on top of an industry that is genuinely growing — industry passengers rose 14.5% to 184 million in FY24, with rail-to-air substitution and management repeatedly describing India as under-penetrated. The company turns capital into cash well, generating ₹28 of operating cash for every ₹100 of shareholders' funds plus borrowings, and it collects from customers about as fast as it pays suppliers, leaving only about three days of cash tied up. It is also spending heavily to grow rather than merely to stand still: a 500-aircraft Airbus order, 30 A350s, a pending orderbook of roughly 1,000 aircraft running to 2035, aircraft ownership through GIFT City, a corporate campus, and an MoU for more than 1,000 LEAP engines with in-house maintenance.

The multibagger screen finds no outright winners, only four 'likely fits' and five partials — and the same traits that produce the fits also produce the risks. The order pipeline of about one new aircraft a week for a decade, the slot and airport positions including first-mover status at Jewar, and the engine and maintenance tie-up are what make 'Cost to Replicate' a real barrier — but that single-airframe Airbus fleet and single Pratt & Whitney engine choice is also exactly what the risk review flags as extreme supplier concentration, after a powder-metal engine defect grounded a peak of mid-70s aircraft and forced expensive damp leases and lease extensions across multiple years. Similarly, the relentless focus on unit cost that earns the 'Low Price Plus' fit — CASK discipline in every call, CASK ex-fuel ex-forex down 18.1% in one quarter, older aircraft to be grounded in FY27 — is the same low-cost identity that leaves the company exposed to the 'Good Enough Goods' risk, where air travel is a price-transparent commodity, online travel agents and fare aggregators frame the choice, and loyalty is thin. Even the strongest-looking pattern is qualified: yields rose 21.3% with only a 1.3 point loss in load factor and guidance of more than 25% PRASK growth, but the analysis reads this as fuel pass-through and tight industry supply rather than durable brand power, noting PRASK actually fell 4% in FY26. The consolidated 'duopoly-plus' industry structure where discipline is holding is doing much of the pricing work, not the brand.

The offsetting weaknesses are what pull the rating down. Growth has averaged about 18% a year but is lumpy year to year, and the share-gain story stalled in FY26–FY27 with no explicit market-share figures ever disclosed. The company is the opposite of an overlooked idea — 19 heavily attended analyst calls, constant press coverage and regulatory scrutiny. Brand and culture score only partial: management owns bad news promptly and keeps a conservative balance sheet with roughly ₹390 billion of free cash and aircraft loan prepayments, but the December 2025 operational collapse showed the trust is testable, and the dividend was withheld. The risk review lands at STRAINED resilience with two high risks. Earnings swing violently despite steady passenger demand — a net loss in FY23, ₹82 billion of profit in FY24, a ₹23.9 billion loss in FY26, and a Q1 FY27 loss with fuel cost per seat-kilometre up around 80% year on year and EBITDAR margin falling from 28% to 15.6%. Much of that swing is outside management's control: fuel prices are notified monthly by state oil marketing companies, the government capped domestic fuel increases at 25% and then withdrew the cap mid-quarter, airport charges escalate by contract, and new Labour Codes add cost. Set against genuinely low technology, fashion and consumer-preference risks, the combination scores 65 on business quality, 42 on pattern fit and 28 on risk safety, for an overall 49 out of 100 — Mixed, two stars. The short version: a strong, hard-to-replicate distribution machine in a growing market, whose profits are hostage to fuel, government decisions and two suppliers.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 2 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across 19 calls results swing violently with fuel, forex and exogenous shocks despite stable passenger demand: net loss FY23, +82bn profit FY24, -23.9bn loss FY26, Q1FY27 loss with fuel CASK up ~80% year-on-year and EBITDAR margin collapsing from 28% to 15.6% — while the company committed to ~1,000 aircraft and 1,000+ LEAP engines at the top of the up-cycle, the classic expansionary over-investment pattern.

> "EBITDAR came in at 38.3 billion rupees at a margin of 15.6% compared to an EBITDAR of 57.4 billion rupees and a margin of 28% for the same period last year" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Demand is flow/operations-linked (123m passengers, highest ever even in the loss year) rather than customer-capex driven, and countercyclical investment continues — 362bn free cash, aircraft loan prepayments and fleet ownership build-up during the downturn*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±47 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from -0% to 24% (swinging ±8.4 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ✅ Capital spending stayed near 2% of sales with no runaway expansion year (peak 6%).

## What to watch

- Whether industry price discipline holds — the 'Oligopoly' check and the 'Pricing Power' pattern both rest on it, and the 'New Entrants' risk is only on WATCH because rivals have behaved rationally for four years.
- Supplier concentration under 'Stakeholder Concentration' — any repeat of the Pratt & Whitney powder-metal groundings or slippage in the ~1,000-aircraft Airbus pipeline hits both the 'Cost to Replicate' barrier and near-term earnings.
- Fuel and policy pass-through under 'Government Dependency' — monthly ATF notifications, the withdrawn 25% cap, escalating airport charges and Labour Codes are what turned Q1 FY27 fuel CASK up ~80% and cut EBITDAR margin from 28% to 15.6%.
- Whether the FY27 yield gain (+21.3%, >25% PRASK guidance) persists once fuel pass-through and tight supply ease — FY26 PRASK fell 4%, which is why 'Pricing Power' is only a LIKELY FIT.
- Repeat operational failures after December 2025 — the 'Brand Strength' pattern and the 'Good Enough Goods' risk both hinge on whether the 20-year reliability promise survives, given thin loyalty in a price-transparent market.
- Resumption of growth and market-share gains — the 'Persistence of growth' check already scores -2 on lumpiness, and the 'Market Share Gainers' pattern notes passenger growth stalled across FY26–FY27.
