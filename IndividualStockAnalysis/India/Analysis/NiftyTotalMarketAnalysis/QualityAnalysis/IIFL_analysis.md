# IIFL Finance (IIFL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

IIFL Finance is an Indian non-bank lender (an NBFC listed on the BSE and NSE, headquartered in Mumbai) that makes small-ticket retail loans to ordinary households, small shopkeepers and micro-businesses across India through a large branch network — over 30,000 employees and more than 1,000 branches added in a single 15-month stretch — supported by WhatsApp, app-based do-it-yourself borrowing and door-step 'gold loan at home' service. Its main products are loans against household gold jewellery, affordable home loans and loans against property, microfinance loans to low-income women, and MSME/business loans, with roughly 95%+ of its book classified by management as 'core' retail products and nearly 90% of it secured. It earns money on the spread between what it charges borrowers and its cost of borrowing (average cost of funds around 9.1% in FY27's first quarter, funded by bank term loans, bonds, commercial paper and a $500 million social bond), plus fee income; it deliberately keeps a large share of loans off its own balance sheet by co-lending with banks and by selling loans down through direct assignment and securitisation, which lets it grow without using much capital. It operates only in India; the loan book crossed ₹1,15,523 crore by June 2026, with return on equity around 19.5% and gross NPAs at 1.6%.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Gold loans** — Short-term loans against customers' gold jewellery, described by management as the 'primary growth engine' — about ₹58,406 crore of the ₹1.15 lakh crore book (roughly half) by June 2026, backed by 62 tonnes of gold; the RBI barred new gold lending in March 2024 over assaying, cash-disbursal and auction lapses, which more than halved the book before the business was allowed to resume and rebuild.
- **Home loans / mortgages (IIFL Home Finance Limited)** — A separately run housing finance subsidiary doing affordable home loans and mid-sized loans against property; management said it was almost half the group's business back in 2022, has grown slowly and flat more recently with stress in a legacy 'micro LAP' portfolio, and Abu Dhabi Investment Authority put in ₹2,200 crore for a 20% stake in it.
- **Microfinance (IIFL Samasta Finance Limited)** — Tiny unsecured loans to low-income, largely rural and semi-urban women borrowers through a separate subsidiary; it has been the group's most volatile part — repeated restructuring, high provisions and election-period stress — but management describes it as recovering, and they are considering a stake sale or listing of it to raise capital.
- **MSME / business loans and digital finance** — Loans to small and medium businesses, increasingly sold through the existing branch network and digital channels (including a joint venture with SME neo-banking platform Open Financial Technologies); one of the four 'core products' but a smaller, faster-growing base on which management stays cautious because much of it is unsecured.
- **Co-lending and assigned loan book (capital-efficient / off-book model)** — Rather than holding every loan itself, IIFL originates loans jointly with banks or sells them on — ₹26,118 crore of assigned loans and ₹14,647 crore of co-lending assets as of June 2026, across 15 active bank partners — which management calls one of its three strategic pillars and the reason it can grow while capital adequacy in the parent NBFC sits near the regulatory edge.
- **AI-led operating model** — Not a lending product but a stated third pillar: using AI across lead generation, underwriting, collections, fraud detection and cross-selling, which management says is moving from pilot to measurable gains in productivity and credit cost.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

IIFL Finance earns its highest marks for how much business it squeezes out of its own capital. Roughly 35-40% of the loan book does not sit on its balance sheet at all — ₹26,118 crore sold down to banks and ₹14,647 crore shared with 15 bank partners — which is why return on assets runs near 3% and the return-on-capital area of the framework scores a clean top mark. Growth is the other bright spot: the loan book is up 38% over the year to ₹1.15 lakh crore, gold loans are up 150%, and management is guiding the cost of bad loans down from 2.7-3.0% to 1.5-1.7%, which drops almost directly into profit. The founder has run the business through the whole period and rebuilt it after the March 2024 regulatory ban without closing branches or laying off staff, and the branch and hiring build-out is genuine expansion spending rather than upkeep. But every one of these strengths carries its own qualifier in the record: the capital-light structure comes with parent capital adequacy near the regulatory floor, the market-share gain is largely recapture of the book lost during the ban, the trough recovery is riding an elevated gold price, and the earlier build-out destroyed returns for two years.

The multibagger screen returns no clean fit anywhere — three partial matches and four outright misses — and the reasons are the same ones that qualify the strengths above. The cost story is half-built: operating costs are guided to 3.3-3.4% of the loan book with an AI programme targeting an 8-20% cut, but the company borrows at 9.13% while competing against banks that fund far cheaper, so it is efficient without being genuinely low-cost. Pricing power is claimed in small-ticket retail and home loans, yet the same calls describe sub-cost teaser-rate wars in gold and new lenders building branch networks — which is exactly why the framework scores industry structure at -0.83 and rates barriers to entry and pricing discipline negatively. Management itself denies two patterns outright: it says the AI advantage is not defensible because 'everybody will get the AI right,' and it concedes competitors can build the same branch network freely. Culture fails the screen for the same facts that sink the stewardship check — the RBI cease-and-desist over purity testing, cash disbursals, auctions and loan-to-value breaches, followed by a group-wide income tax search.

The risk review turns those same traits into exposures. Gold lending is now about half the book, and it is simultaneously the engine of the market-share pattern and the reason cyclicality is rated high risk — management ties its growth directly to the gold price cycle, and the record already shows the book shrinking 12% in a single quarter. Government dependency is the other high risk and the sharpest illustration of how little protects this business: one RBI order halved the gold book from ₹26,000 crore to ₹12,162 crore and forced ₹15,700 crore of repayments. The off-book funding structure that produces the strong capital efficiency is the same thing flagged under stakeholder concentration, because when the regulator acted the banks froze credit lines. The branch network that counts as growth spending is the fixed-cost base flagged under technology risk as lending shifts digital. Four further risks sit on watch and two are low — demand is functional and repeat-purchase, and the digital channel shift is being captured rather than lost. On capital returns to owners the record shows no buybacks or distribution policy at all, and instead repeated dilution or preparation for it. The combined rating lands at 48 out of 100, a Mixed two-star verdict, with resilience unproven either way.
