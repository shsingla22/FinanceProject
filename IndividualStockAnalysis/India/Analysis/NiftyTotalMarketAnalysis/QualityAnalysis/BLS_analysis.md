# BLS Internat. (BLS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

BLS International is an Indian-listed outsourcing company that runs the paperwork side of government services. Its main business is visa and consular work: foreign governments hire it to set up and run visa application centres, where it collects applications, documents and biometrics from travellers on the government's behalf, plus related services like passport, attestation and apostille work; it earns a service fee on each application (net revenue per application was about INR3,302 in FY26, on 44.1 lakh applications), so revenue rises with travel volumes, new contract wins and higher service charges. Its second business, called Digital Services, works mainly inside India: acting as a 'business correspondent' for banks (opening accounts and handling deposits, transfers and passbook updates in mostly rural areas through a network of local agents), distributing loans, and running citizen-service and e-governance projects such as Aadhaar enrolment centres for state governments and UIDAI. It operates in 80+ countries for more than 40 client governments, on an asset-light model of ~155,000 touch points and ~45,800 channel partners. In FY26 it reported revenue of INR2,998 crores and profit after tax of INR724 crores, with visa and consular contributing INR1,840 crores of revenue and digital INR1,158 crores.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Visa & Consular Services** — Sets up and operates visa application centres and related consular services (attestation, apostille) for client governments worldwide, charging a fee per application; it is the profit engine — INR1,840 crores of FY26 revenue at a 40% EBITDA margin, versus roughly 80-85% of total revenue back in FY23, and it grew via the INR720 crore acquisition of Turkey-based iDATA and wins like a 5-year Slovak Republic contract covering 80+ countries and Indian visa centres in China for India's Ministry of External Affairs.
- **Digital Services (reported segment, run partly through listed subsidiary BLS E-Services)** — The umbrella for the India-facing businesses — banking correspondent work, loan distribution and citizen/e-governance services — which more than doubled to INR1,158 crores of revenue in FY26 (EBITDA INR81 crores) and is now the faster-growing but much lower-margin half of the company.
- **Business Correspondent (BC) banking business** — Acts as the outsourced last-mile arm for banks — opening accounts and processing deposits, transfers and passbook updates through local agents (CSPs) in areas banks don't cover — built on the 2022 purchase of an 88.71% stake in Zero Mass Private Limited (SBI's largest BC) for an enterprise value of INR135 crores; it handled gross transaction value of over INR1.1 lakh crores in FY26 and has crossed INR10,000 crores of aggregate balances in accounts it opened.
- **Loan distribution (Aadifidelis)** — A loan lead-generation and distribution business added by buying a 55% controlling stake in Aadifidelis Loan Solutions, with 8,600+ channel partners; it generated loan leads worth more than INR36,800 crores in FY26 and is cross-sold alongside the banking correspondent network.
- **Citizen / e-governance services** — Runs government-facing service counters and registration projects under contract — early examples were nine registration projects for the West Bengal government and 4,000 offices for Karnataka, and more recently a INR100 crore Aadhaar Permanent Enrolment Centre project for Bihar plus a roughly INR2,000 crore UIDAI mandate to upgrade Seva Kendras; it was described in FY23 as part of the 15-20% of revenue outside visa, and is now folded into the Digital segment.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

BLS runs a physically large, capital-light business: it handles visa, consular and citizen-service paperwork for more than 40 client governments across 80-plus countries, with over 155,000 domestic touch points and 45,800-plus channel partners. The quality review scores its customer benefits highest of any area (1.5 out of 2), because the two things it sells are exactly what buyers pay for here — reliability and trust in handling sovereign documents, and sheer physical convenience for applicants. That translates into unusually good economics: every ₹100 of capital in the business generated ₹31 of operating cash this year, alongside a 32% return on capital employed, 29% return on equity, zero debt and ₹903 crore of operating cash flow on ₹2,998 crore of revenue. Returns on capital score 1.33 and growth 1.17, so the business converts its footprint into cash and keeps expanding the footprint.

The multibagger screen scores 78 and its three strongest hits all trace back to the same footprint. Pricing power is a strong fit because service charges on new and re-tendered contracts keep rising while volumes also grow 10-18% — price up and volume up at the same time, with net revenue per application up 19% year-on-year to ₹3,383. Market share gain is a strong fit because applications went from 7.21 lakh in the first quarter of FY24 to 44.1 lakh in FY26 on wins from Slovakia, Cyprus, Italy, Portugal, Poland, the Indian Ministry of External Affairs, plus the iDATA, Zero Mass and Aadifidelis acquisitions — and that share is being won at higher prices, not lower ones. Forward integration is the third: management deliberately took visa centres back from partners and ran them in-house, which cut the cost of service and lifted visa margins from roughly 15-16% in FY23 to 23%, then 29%, then above 40%. The toll-road read follows from the fact that a visa fee is small relative to a trip but compulsory, in a market where only about half of governments have outsourced at all. Notably, the screen finds no fit for low-price competition or for innovation-led growth — no research spend is ever disclosed, and margin gains come from taking centres in-house and charging more, not from proprietary technology.

The same traits that create the patterns create most of the risk, and the risk review scores lowest of the three at 58. Government dependency is rated high risk precisely because the toll-road quality exists: contracts are awarded by governments, so the revenue is durable while held but is periodically put back out to tender, which is also why cost-to-replicate and brand strength only score partial — the barriers are real but the contracts are not permanent. The good-enough-goods risk sharpens this, since the government picks the provider while the traveller pays, and a cheaper bidder can win; in the State Bank of India correspondent business alone there are about 20 competing outsourcers. Cyclicality is elevated for a related reason: demand is a flow of applications exposed to outside shocks, and the calls document COVID, the China and Russia closures worth roughly 30% of pre-COVID visa volume, and the FY26 Middle East conflict — which fits the quality check showing operating margins swinging about 7.5 points a year, suggesting key costs sit outside management's control. Two further items are on watch: reliance on a short list of named counterparties (State Bank of India, the Ministry of External Affairs, UIDAI), and the possibility that fully digital government processing bypasses physical centres entirely, since the innovation shown so far is incremental. Internally, the weakest quality area is management at -0.4 with capital allocation at 0.4, reflecting heavy serial acquisition activity against an otherwise stable, promoter-led, debt-free, dividend-paying record with ₹1,434 crore of net cash. The combined rating is 68 out of 100 — Strong, four stars — with resilience judged sound: a high-return, cash-generative share gainer whose main vulnerability is that its customer and its regulator are the same party.
