# Crizac (CRIZAC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Crizac is a Kolkata-based, business-to-business company that recruits international students for foreign universities: it runs an online platform where independent education agents around the world submit student applications, and Crizac processes those applications end-to-end and passes qualified students to its partner universities. It earns a commission from the universities for each student who actually enrolls, so revenue follows the admission cycle — applications come in 6 to 9 months before the money is booked, making Q3 (application peak) and Q4 (enrollment/revenue peak) the big quarters. Management describes it as asset-light, tech-led and debt-free, funded entirely from its own cash; FY26 revenue from operations was about INR 10,422 million (up 22.7%) with PAT of INR 2,191 million and an EBITDA margin of about 27%. It connects roughly 15,000 registered agents (5,389 of them active) and more than 400 partner universities, sourcing students from 85+ countries into eight destination countries, with the UK by far the largest destination and India about half the application volume.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Core B2B student recruitment platform (Crizac Limited / Crizac UK)** — The main business — the proprietary platform that takes applications from agents, quality-checks and processes them, and delivers enrolled students to partner universities; it is essentially all of the revenue today, with roughly 90% of applications going to UK universities and the top 10 university partners accounting for 66% of revenue, and it processed 3.94 lakh applications in FY26 (up 43%).
- **Global Tree Careers (B2C counselling and immigration)** — A 51% stake bought in January 2026 in a direct-to-student counselling and immigration services business in India, which management says carries much higher margins than the B2B core (around 50%) and starts consolidating into the P&L from Q4 FY26 — a new, still-small direct-to-student arm.
- **StudiesPlanet (Latin America)** — A Latin American student recruitment agency acquired in October 2025 to open up LATAM as a new source region alongside Asia and Africa; described as an early-stage contributor to a more diversified revenue mix rather than a material earner yet.
- **New Zealand business / Medway Educational Consultant team** — A destination-market build-out operationalised in April 2026 by onboarding an entire consulting team, giving Crizac on-ground admissions capability in New Zealand — one of the newer destinations management expects to benefit as students shift away from the US and Canada.
- **US business (including Raj Consultant)** — A small operation, acquired in FY25 to diversify into the US market, which management repeatedly called 'very small' and held back by tightened US visa policy, with the hope of benefiting whenever conditions stabilise.
- **Ancillary / adjacent student services** — Newer add-ons around the student journey — an accommodation booking platform launched during FY26, plus stated plans for student loans, visa services and insurance — meant to earn extra revenue off the same agent and student flow at similar operating leverage; not yet quantified in the calls.
- **EduMentor (AI technology investment)** — A commitment of USD 2.5 million over five years, made in March 2026, to an AI platform for matching students to universities and providing mentorship and decision support — an investment in technology and conversion rates rather than a revenue line today.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Crizac earns its money as a middleman: education agents in 85-plus countries advise students, the students pay nothing, and the universities pay Crizac a commission per enrolled student. That structure is the source of almost everything good in the numbers. Every ₹1 of assets produces ₹1.19 of sales, the business is debt-free with about ₹470 crore of net cash, and it turned ₹49 of operating cash out of every ₹100 of capital employed, with return on capital employed of 48.6% and return on equity of 37.2%. Operating profit is ₹27 of every ₹100 of sales and has barely wobbled (about ±3.8 points), which reads as costs being genuinely under control rather than a lucky year. The quality scoring reflects this shape exactly: Return on Capital is the standout area at 1.67 and Competitive Advantage a clean 1.0. The 'Friendly Middleman' pattern is the only STRONG FIT in the whole screen, and it is the same fact set — the agent does the choosing, the university does the paying, nobody in the chain is price-sensitive on the student's behalf — that makes the returns possible.

The distribution network is the clearest durable edge and the engine of the growth patterns. Crizac has 14,000 registered and 5,389 active agents, up 36% in FY26, with on-ground teams in 11 countries, feeding roughly 400 institutional relationships an applicant pool a newcomer cannot assemble quickly. Applications rose 43%, global share is still under 1% against 80 lakh students moving worldwide, Ireland share is near 10%, and the UK government is targeting GBP 40 billion of education exports by 2030 — a genuinely expanding end market. But the growth patterns stop short of full marks for one connected reason: the funnel narrows. Applications grew 43%, enrolments only 14%, and revenue 22.7% against the roughly 30% management guided in August 2025, after which guidance was withdrawn in Q4. So 'Market Share Gainers' and 'Cost to Replicate' land at LIKELY FIT and PARTIAL rather than strong, and the same shortfall shows up as a Management area score of 0.0, a Growth area score of 0.33, and a negative mark on persistence of growth — sales have averaged about 56% a year but arrive lumpily. 'Pricing Power' is only PARTIAL for a related reason: margin did expand 172 basis points to 27%, but no price increase is ever quantified, realisation data is withheld, and an analyst's question about a ceiling on realisation went unanswered.

The risks are not separate from the strengths — several are the same trait viewed from the other side. Because the student pays nothing and the agent picks the platform, there is no consumer brand and no brand-driven pricing power (that check scores -1), cost of services runs at 64-70% of revenue, and universities can move volume to cheaper aggregators; that is precisely the 'Good Enough Goods' WATCH risk and why 'Brand Strength' rests only on institutional trust and five-plus-year tenure with top-30 partners. Because the model runs on agents, agent regulation cuts both ways: the UK Agent Quality Framework is credited with raising entry barriers and entrenching compliant platforms (supporting 'Toll Roads'), while the same dependence on visa and immigration policy — the UK migration white paper, post-study work visas cut from 24 to 18 months, Canada and US tightening — is a HIGH RISK on government dependency. Concentration is the other HIGH RISK and sits on both sides: the UK is about 97% of the destination mix, the top 10 university partners are 66% of revenue, and an analyst noted over 50% from three institutions in August 2025, while universities themselves work with multiple platforms including named peers ApplyBoard and Krishna Consultants. Management's answer to all of this is to move closer to the student and away from single-country reliance — 51% of Global Tree, StudiesPlanet in Latin America, EduMentor, a New Zealand team, accommodation brokerage and loan referral fees — but those are recent, small, and unproven, so 'Forward Integrators' stays PARTIAL and Capital Allocation scores only 0.4 despite a debt-free record and a payout raised to 64% against a committed 40%. Netting it out, the framework puts business quality at 65, pattern fit at 64 and risk safety at 44, for 59 out of 100 — Decent, three stars, with resilience judged SOUND on the strength of the cash-generative, debt-free balance sheet.
