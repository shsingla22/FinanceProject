# LIC Housing Finance Ltd. (LICHSGFIN) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

LIC Housing Finance is an Indian housing finance company (an HFC) that lends money to people to buy homes, and to a smaller extent to property developers. It borrows in bulk — mainly from banks (increasingly at floating, repo-linked rates), from the National Housing Bank and other financial institutions — and lends that money out at a higher rate, earning the spread; management tracks this as net interest margin, which ran about 2.68%–2.80% through FY26. Its total loan book was about INR 3,20,707 crores as of 31 March 2026, of which roughly 84–85% is individual home loans, and it competes head-on with banks on rate (new home loans offered from 7.15%–7.50% during the period). It operates in India through a branch and field network, with lending decisions now being partly automated via a 'straight-through process' machine-enabled credit appraisal launched in February 2026.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Individual Home Loans** — Home loans to individual borrowers — the core business at INR 2,70,893 crores or about 84% of the total book as of March 2026, with quarterly disbursements of roughly INR 11,000–16,700 crores; management says it is concentrated in the 'prime' and mostly salaried segment, where competition from banks is intense and growth has been slow (about 4% for FY26).
- **Non-housing individual loans (including LAP)** — Loans to individuals against property and other non-housing purposes — a much smaller but faster-growing line, with disbursements of INR 3,348 crores in Q4 FY26, up 25% year-on-year.
- **Project loans (developer loans)** — Lending to builders/developers for housing projects — the smallest piece, with disbursements of only INR 156 crores in Q1 FY26 and INR 847 crores in Q4 FY26, and it is where the company's large stressed accounts and settlement negotiations sit.
- **Affordable housing segment** — A newer push into lower-ticket affordable home loans, described as a 'foray' the company is growing slowly and cautiously because it is something it had not done in its 36-year history; management identifies it, along with self-employed borrowers, as where future growth must come from.
- **Borrowing / treasury function** — Raising the funds it lends out — over INR 45,000 crores of fresh bank and financial-institution loans plus more than INR 10,000 crores from NHB in FY26, with a deliberate shift toward floating-rate borrowing (52% of outstanding borrowings by March 2026 versus 45% a year earlier); cost of funds fell to 7.27% and is the main driver of profitability alongside lending rates.
- **Recovery and asset quality management** — In-house recovery verticals plus legal and settlement negotiations on bad loans, including selling a stressed asset to an ARC for INR 70 crores in Q4 FY26; Stage 3 (bad) loans fell to 2.16% of exposure by March 2026 with full-year credit cost of 18 basis points.

### The story in depth

LIC Housing Finance comes out of the three reviews with a combined score of 36 out of 100, or Mixed (two stars) — built from a weak business-quality score of 23 and a middling risk-safety score of 58. The single thing the company scores well on is honesty: the candour check earns a +2 because management repeatedly owns its mistakes without spin — being late to cut rates, the old project-finance book that once ran at 54% bad loans, missed growth guidance, and the frank admission that the widely assumed advantage of selling through LIC's agent army never actually materialised. That candour is also the only reason the Corporate Culture pattern lands at PARTIAL rather than failing outright, and it is why Management is the sole quality area above zero (+0.17). But the honesty cuts both ways: almost every damaging fact in this report comes from management's own mouth. Return on Capital scores the floor (-2.0) and Industry Structure is nearly as bad (-1.83), and the promise record undercuts the candour — double-digit growth was guided and not delivered.

The competitive picture is where the three analyses converge hardest. Eight of the eleven multibagger patterns are outright NO FIT, and they all trace back to one fact the company states plainly about itself: it lends against prime salaried home loans, competing head-on with banks that fund themselves at roughly 5-5.5% using current-account deposits while LIC Housing's own incremental cost of money is 6.94% — and it borrows from those very banks. That single disadvantage explains the whole cluster. Pricing Power is not just absent but inverted, with new home loan rates cut from 7.50% in August 2025 to 7.15% by December 2025 and a further 0.25 percentage point cut applied to the entire existing book — which is the same evidence behind the -2 on 'Pricing, Mix and Volume' and the -2 on barriers to entry. Cost to Replicate and Low Price Plus fail for the same funding reason. Brand Strength fails because the LIC name, by management's own account, has not converted into either business or premium pricing. Recurring Revenue fails on the Rs.12,778 crores of loans that walked out to other lenders in FY26 — roughly Rs.1,000 crores a month — which is also exactly the evidence for the -2 on switching costs, and it forced the company to create a dedicated retention department and offer cheaper 'rewriting' rates to hold customers it already had. The result shows up in the growth numbers: book growth decaying from 7% to 5% across FY26 quarters and landing at 4.4%, with the 'Share donators' check concluding that LIC Housing is itself the donor — losing prime borrowers to banks while ceding the faster-growing self-employed and affordable segments to other housing finance companies.

On risk, only one channel is High: Government Dependency, because policy sets both sides of the profit line — the RBI's repo rate moves determine what competitors charge and what the company pays, the RBI's stance on bank exposure caps how much it can borrow, over Rs.10,000 crores came from the National Housing Bank, and LIC is both parent and principal. That is the same repo-linked pricing regime the New Entrants channel (Watch) blames for turning banks into structurally cheaper rivals, and the same rate cycle the Cyclicality channel (Watch) says compressed margins, drove Rs.4,000 crores of loan transfers out in a single quarter and pulled book growth to 4.4% — one force, appearing under three names. Good Enough Goods is Elevated for precisely the reason Pricing Power, Brand Strength and Toll Roads all failed: an undifferentiated mortgage chosen on headline rate alone. And the trait that earns the Friendly Middleman pattern a PARTIAL — roughly 90% of business sourced through agents — is the very same trait that makes Stakeholder Concentration a Watch, since sourcing sits in one channel while incremental funding has been deliberately concentrated in banks (82% of FY26). The three Low-risk channels — fashion, shifting consumer preferences, technology — are low for the same reason the quality score is weak: a home loan is a plain, functional, long-tenure contract with nothing to fall out of favour and, on the evidence of four calls, nothing being disrupted. This is a business that is hard to kill and hard to win with. Resilience is recorded as Unknown, so the durability question is genuinely open rather than answered either way.

## What to watch

- Balance transfers out: the Rs.12,778 crores that left in FY26 is the core evidence for both the negative switching-costs check and the Elevated 'Good Enough Goods' risk — if the retention department and rewriting rates cut this sharply, the worst quality finding softens.
- Book growth versus the industry: the 'Gain in market share' check and the 'Market Share Gainers' pattern both fail on 4-5% growth for three straight years — a return to the guided double-digit growth without further rate cuts would change both.
- The funding gap: the 6.94% incremental cost of money against banks at roughly 5-5.5% is the single fact behind Low Price Plus, Cost to Replicate and the New Entrants risk — any durable narrowing would lift several verdicts at once.
- Whether price cuts stop: rates went from 7.50% to 7.15% plus a 0.25 percentage point cut on the existing book, which is why Pricing Power is scored as outright deflation — margins stabilising without new cuts is the test.

- The new affordable-housing vertical: the 'People matter' check scores -2 partly because management admits its own staff cannot run this business and must hire entirely from outside — whether that outside-hired team delivers is a direct read on the internal talent finding.
