# Aavas Financiers Ltd. (AAVAS) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: FINANCIAL SERVICES*

Aavas Financiers is an Indian housing finance company, headquartered in Jaipur and formerly called AU Housing Finance, that lends money to ordinary households to buy or build a home and against property they already own. Its customers are largely lower-income, first-time home buyers in smaller Tier-2 and Tier-3 towns — roughly 60% of the book is self-employed people whose income has to be 'assessed' rather than read off a salary slip, and it also lends for EWS/LIG affordable housing, women-owned homes and green-certified housing. It makes money on the gap between what it charges borrowers (portfolio yield around 13%) and what it pays for its own funding (borrowing cost around 8%), a spread of about 5%, borrowing from banks, the National Housing Bank, debt capital markets and by selling down loan pools; keeping bad loans very low (gross NPA around 1%, credit costs under 25 bps) is central to the model. It runs a branch-led, pan-India network that grew from 318 branches in 2022 to 440 across 15 states by mid-2026, with assets under management of about Rs. 239 billion and roughly 4 lakh customers served since inception.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Home loans** — Loans to individuals to buy, build or extend a house — the core product at about 71% of assets under management, and management flags 38% year-on-year growth in this segment in Q1 FY27.
- **Other mortgage loans** — Loans secured against property for purposes other than buying a home, making up roughly 29% of the book alongside home loans.
- **MSME lending** — A smaller, newer line lending to small businesses in underserved regions; the calls describe it mainly through funding milestones — a first-ever industry tie-up with an MSME-sector development institution in FY24 and NCD proceeds earmarked partly for it in FY26 — rather than as a large share of the book.
- **Assignment / securitisation (off-book loans)** — Aavas regularly sells down pools of its own loans to other lenders — roughly Rs. 700–900 crore a year — which brings in upfront income that is then reversed over the loans' behavioural life; it accounts for about 23–24% of the company's overall funding mix.
- **Co-lending** — A first-ever tie-up done in FY24 with a large public-sector bank to jointly fund loans and support long-term business growth; the calls mention it only as a new funding/growth avenue without sizing it.
- **Borrowing / liability franchise** — Not a customer-facing product but treated as a core capability: Rs. 207 billion of borrowings as of June 2026 spread across bank term loans, National Housing Bank refinance, assignment/securitisation and debt capital markets, deliberately kept longer-tenure than its loans and ~76% linked to fast-repricing benchmarks so falling rates feed through quickly.
- **Collections and credit ('credit-first') function** — A dedicated in-house risk, credit and collections organisation with its own chief officers, presented in every call as the thing that keeps 1+ day-past-due below 4% and gross NPAs near 1% — management treats it as central to the business, not a back-office task.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Aavas is a branch-based housing lender to self-employed, informally-documented borrowers in smaller Indian towns, and the quality work says the end market is the best thing about it: structural end-market growth was the single highest-scoring check, backed by policy support and lifetime lending crossing Rs.400bn to 4 lakh customers. Around that sit two genuine positives — disciplined balance-sheet stewardship across all 21 calls (no commercial paper despite a top short-term rating, borrowings running longer than loans, capital adequacy near 44%, no acquisitions) and well-executed expansion from 318 branches in June 2022 to 440 across 15 states, including a deliberate 30-month go-slow before lending normally in each new state. But the same picture explains the weak scores. This is a balance-sheet lender carrying Rs.234.5bn of loans funded by Rs.204bn of borrowings, so returns are structurally capped near 3.5% on assets and 13-14% on equity — hence the worst area score of all, Return on Capital at -1.0, and a negative asset-turn check. Industry structure scores -0.83 because barriers are low and pricing is not rational: the gap between lending and borrowing rates fell from 5.81% in June 2022 to 5.06%, with the company cutting its lending rate twice in FY27 and passing it straight to customers.

The eleven multibagger patterns land the same way — eight partial fits and three outright misses, with no pattern fully present. The partial fits all trace back to the same handful of real strengths. Owning 440 branches and pushing back toward direct sourcing gives a partial Forward Integrators read, and the ten-to-twelve years of branch-level local knowledge (management says a new state needs roughly 30 months before underwriting behaves normally) gives a partial Cost to Replicate read — but both are the same branch build that shows up positively in the capex-for-growth check and negatively in the pricing check, because growth is being bought with branches and volume rather than earned on price. Cheap, diversified funding (cost of funds down 62bps in FY26, 73-76% of borrowings repricing quickly) plus clean loans (bad loans around 1%, credit costs of 13-25bps) gives a partial Low Price Plus read, and it is the same trait behind the favourable rate-cycle check. The technology rebuild — Salesforce, Oracle Flexcube, account aggregator, predictive analytics, sanction times down to 8-9 days — gives a partial Innovation Dominance read, though it is process and funding innovation, not research. The three misses are the honest ones: no Recurring Revenue because nothing in 21 calls resembles renewals or service contracts; no Pricing Power because spreads and yields are falling and management concedes book yields are converging down; and no Market Share Gainers because loan-book growth decelerated from 24% in FY23 to 22% in FY24 to 15% in FY26, with the new CEO framing his job as recovering lost ground.

The risk review is the more forgiving half of the rating — three risks are low and five are only on watch, producing a risk-safety score of 80 against a business-quality score of 47, which is how a mediocre business arrives at an overall 59 and a Decent, three-star verdict. Crucially, the watch-list risks are mostly the mirror image of the strengths just described. The fast-repricing, diversified funding that makes Low Price Plus partly work is exactly what makes earnings swing with the RBI rate cycle — 125bps of cuts help today, but rate hikes in FY23 forced lending rates up before. The intermediated sourcing behind the partial Friendly Middleman read (connectors, DSAs, Mitra/eMitra, CSC, roughly 13% of disbursement) is the same channel that lets customers be poached, showing up as the Good Enough Goods watch: a commoditised loan at roughly 13% against cheaper bank pricing, with about 6% of the opening loan book transferring out every year. The technology rebuild that supports the innovation pattern is itself the technology watch item, since analysts have asked directly whether pending system go-lives could interrupt lending. Low barriers feed both the industry-structure check and the New Entrants watch. Two things sit outside this neat symmetry: management continuity broke down badly over the period — the founding MD left, a successor came and went, a third CEO arrived in FY26 alongside an interim finance chief, and the promoters sold to CVC — which is why the long-term-stewardship check scores -1 and why the culture pattern is only partial (asset-quality promises were kept for four years, growth promises were not, with 22-25% guidance becoming 15%). And the whole business sits inside an RBI and NHB frame that moves reported numbers directly, including a rule change that added 26bps to bad loans and an MD appointment that required regulatory approval. Resilience could not be established from the record.
