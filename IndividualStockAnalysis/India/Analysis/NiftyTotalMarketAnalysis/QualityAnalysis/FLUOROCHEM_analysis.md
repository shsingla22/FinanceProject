# Gujarat Fluoroch (FLUOROCHEM) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Gujarat Fluorochemicals (GFL) is an India-based chemicals maker that turns basic chemicals into fluorine-based products — plastics and rubbers called fluoropolymers (PTFE, PVDF, PFA, FKM), refrigerant gases (R-22, R-32, R-125), specialty chemicals, and commodity chemicals like caustic soda and chloromethanes. It sells to industrial customers rather than consumers: semiconductor makers, auto and emission-control companies, air-conditioning and cold-chain players, solar and clean-energy users, and increasingly electric-vehicle and battery-storage manufacturers. It makes money by selling these materials by the tonne, mostly at higher margins in the specialty grades, and is heavily export-oriented — management repeatedly cites the U.S., EU, Japan, Korea and India as markets, and blames U.S. tariffs and Red Sea shipping delays for weak quarters. Manufacturing is in Gujarat, India (Ranjitnagar/Dahej), with a new greenfield battery-materials plant being built in Oman; consolidated revenue runs roughly INR 1,100–1,400 crore a quarter, and the company describes itself as virtually debt-free while spending heavily on capacity expansion.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Fluoropolymers** — Makes PTFE plus the higher-value 'new fluoropolymers' (PVDF, PFA, FKM, micropowders) used in semiconductors, auto emission control and solar film — this is the biggest and fastest-growing part, at INR 848 crore of a INR 1,358 crore quarter in Q4 FY26 and growing 14–19% year-on-year.
- **Fluorochemicals (refrigerants and specialty chemicals)** — Sells refrigerant gases (R-22, R-125, and R-32 whose production started March 2026) for air conditioning, commercial refrigeration, cold chain and AI data-centre cooling, plus specialty chemicals; it swings a lot with seasons, R-22 production quotas and Chinese competition, and was the main drag in Q3 FY26 when segment revenue fell 33% year-on-year.
- **Bulk Chemicals** — The commodity base — caustic soda, chloromethanes/MDC and fluoromethanes — which feeds the company's own downstream plants (backward integration) and is described as low-growth and price-driven, with revenue falling when caustic and chloromethane prices soften.
- **Battery Materials (GFCL EV Products Limited)** — A newer subsidiary building an integrated set of lithium-ion battery inputs — LiPF6 salt (commercial sales began December 2025), LFP cathode active material, fluoropolymer binders and a planned natural graphite anode — aimed at EV and grid energy-storage customers; it earns almost nothing yet but is the company's big bet, with INR 6,000 crore of planned capex through FY28, an IFC investment of INR 430 crore and an $82 million sovereign-fund commitment, and full earnings expected only around FY29.

### The story in depth

The strongest thing about Gujarat Fluoroch is that its customers are buying certainty rather than a cheap price. The quality framework gives full marks to what customers get out of the relationship and to the growth on offer: audited facilities, months-long qualification, and a supply chain that does not depend on any single country, all feeding end markets that are genuinely expanding — semiconductors, EV and grid batteries, data-centre-driven storage, hydrogen and cooling. Sitting on top of that is a rare piece of luck: legacy Western fluoropolymer producers such as 3M, Solvay and Chemours are walking away from the business, leaving already-qualified volume for a small challenger to pick up. Three of the eleven multibagger patterns lean on exactly this — customers stick because qualification runs nine to twelve months and buyers keep only two or three approved suppliers (Toll Roads), the plant base is hard to copy because of full backward integration to fluorspar and clean-class semiconductor assets (Cost to Replicate), and fluoropolymers grew 14-19% in FY26 as rivals exited (Market Share Gainers). None of the three scored a clean pass. Each was marked partial for the same reason: management describes the gain but never puts a number on it, the share capture has been promised repeatedly across four years of calls, and the qualification barrier protects the position without letting the company set its own price everywhere.

The traits that make the business defensible are also what make it expensive and slow. Being hard to replicate means being asset-heavy: every ₹1 of assets currently produces only ₹0.42 of sales, and even the 2x asset turn management targets on new plants is something it admits it may not hit. Roughly 401 days of cash sits trapped between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, up from 109 days in Q1FY23 — the opposite of a business its customers fund. Against that, capex is very large and rising: ₹3,150 crore for FY27 and ₹6,000 crore cumulative on battery materials by FY28, promised at 2x asset turns and 25%-plus margins, while return on capital has already fallen from 35%. That is why the framework scores capital allocation, management and return on capital all at zero despite full marks on customers and growth. The same story shows up in the risks. Pricing power is real only in specialty fluoropolymers, where a price rise was taken in FY26 and margins held through cost inflation; everywhere else — refrigerants, chloromethanes, caustic, specialty chemicals — prices are set by the market, and cheaper Chinese material has already compressed those lines, which is why both new entrants and good-enough substitutes are rated elevated. Refrigerants add a further layer the company does not control: Montreal Protocol quotas cut R-22, allow R-32 capacity only to December 2027, and a swing in US tariffs from 50% to 18% moved both volumes and realisations. Reported sales growth of about 3% a year on average, lumpy from year to year, and margins swinging roughly ±5.8 points, are what all of this adds up to — a peak 34% margin quarter in Q1FY23, 22% by Q1FY25, and revenue down 1% in Q3FY26.

The battery push is where the strengths and the risks meet most directly. It is the clearest expression of the growth story and the largest single capital commitment, and it is also the source of two of the flagged watch-level risks: the entire LFP capacity is pre-committed to a handful of anchor customers under off-take agreements, so losing one qualification matters a lot, and the underlying chemistry is still moving — analysts asked back in 2022 whether LiFSI would displace LiPF6, and the company has since kept adding chemistries (LFP cathode, PVDF binders, now natural graphite anode) rather than settling on one. R&D reliably turns into new grades and new verticals, but no spend figure is ever disclosed, which is why the innovation pattern also stops at partial. Management deserves credit for candour — the same leaders have been in place across five years and bad news is delivered plainly, from a plant incident to Red Sea deferrals to tariff hits — but the delivery record against its own promises is what holds the culture pattern back too. Netting it out, the combined rating is 37 out of 100, a Mixed two-star verdict: 59 for business quality, 42 for risk safety, and zero for multibagger fit, because not a single one of the eleven patterns scored a clean yes. The business itself is judged sound and its demand base is functional and specification-driven rather than fashion-led; the open question is not whether the demand is there, but whether the enormous capital going in earns a decent return on the way out.

### Government Dependency — HIGH RISK

**What the calls show:** Refrigerant volumes and prices are set by Montreal Protocol quotas (R-22 quota cuts, R-32 capacity permitted only up to December 2027), US tariffs moving 50%→18% swung both volumes and realisations, and a chunk of expected fluoropolymer growth vanished when the Finance Ministry declined DGTR's antidumping recommendation. ## What to watch

- Working capital: the cycle has stretched from 109 days to about 401 days (Cost of working capital check, and the reason the Recurring Revenue pattern is a no-fit) — a reversal here would change the capital story fastest.
