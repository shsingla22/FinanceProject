### Bucket 1 — Business quality (34-check framework)

Of the 34 quality checks, 29 could be genuinely re-tested on both views: 9 improved, 3 regressed, 17 unchanged. 2 checks carry the long view's verdict forward unchanged (the window could not re-test them). 1 was newly assessable in the recent view.

| Area | Long view | Last one year | Change |

**Checks that improved:**

- **Candor** (Management): Weak → Excellent. Now: Management repeatedly volunteers unflattering detail and caps expectations — explaining that the 31% EBITDA was inflated by amortisation and would settle at 26-27%, disclosing the ALH grounding and LCA supply-chain failure, and conceding LCA delivery has slipped 'multiple times'. | The long view had said: Candor is weak on the issues that matter most — the FY26 CMD twice deflected direct analyst questions on what is holding up LCA Mark-1A deliveries, calling problems 'refinements' and hiding behind technicality, after guidance had slipped repeatedly (first delivery promised for February 2024 in the 2022 call, still undelivered in May 2026).
- **Geographical expansion** (Growth): Weak → Good. Now: Credible early international expansion — Dorniers delivered to Guyana within 10 days of contract, 2 more Hindustan-228 ordered, and entry into the global civil supply chain via the Safran LEAP rotating-parts agreement — but exports remain ~1.5% of revenue. | The long view had said: Geographic ambition promised in 2022 (Malaysia LCA tender, offices in Malaysia, Egypt, Middle East, North America, EASA/FAA certification) has produced almost nothing four years later — exports remain Rs 501 crore, essentially two Dornier aircraft to Guyana repeated year after year.
- **Profit margin** (Return on Capital): Good → Excellent. Now: Out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹30 is operating profit. Margins have moved about ±0.7 points a year — very steady, a sign costs are under control. | The long view had said: Out of every ₹100 of sales, ₹30 is operating profit. Margins have moved about ±5.8 points a year — sizeable swings, suggesting key costs are outside management's control.
- **Research and development** (Capital Allocation): Good → Excellent. Now: R&D at INR2,794 crores (8.4% of revenue) plus a 15%-of-PAT R&D corpus has converted into a stream of certified products — ALH Dhruv NG maiden flight, HTT-40 series production sortie, Shakti civil engine DGCA certification, 223 IPRs filed in FY26. | The long view had said: R&D is consistently high (7-8% of revenue, Rs 2,794 crore or 8.4% in FY26, 15% of PAT ring-fenced as R&D reserve, 2,842 IPRs filed) and has yielded a genuine indigenous platform stable (LCA, LCH, LUH, HTT-40, ALH Dhruv NG), but efficiency is dented by repeated slippage — LUH design/software setbacks and LCA Mark-1A iterations still unresolved in May 2026.
- **Out of limelight** (Management): Neutral → Good. Now: Management communicates in a plain, technocratic PSU register with no personality-driven narrative, though the company itself is highly visible via Maharatna status and ministerial inaugurations. | The long view had said: Management is career-bureaucratic and substance-focused in tone rather than celebrity-driven, but the calls repeatedly foreground ceremonial visibility — Maharatna status, Raksha Mantri inaugurations, ministerial presence at maiden flights.
- **Barriers to entry and rationality** (Industry Structure): Good → Excellent. Now: Profit margins have barely moved over the years (±0.7 points) — consistent with rational pricing and few new competitors. And from the calls: Barriers are extreme — certification regimes (CEMILAC, DGCA), decade-long development cycles, forging presses and 2,842 cumulative IPRs — with stable government ownership and no evidence of discount-driven competition. | The long view had said: Profit margins swing by ±5.8 points — a hint of price wars, cost shocks, or new entrants disturbing the industry. And from the calls: Barriers are extreme — certification (CEMILAC, DGCA), decades of design capability, 1,226 granted IPRs and state ownership — with no new entrants and no discounting; contracts carry escalation and ERV pass-through clauses rather than price wars.
- **Independent, long-term and tenacious** (Management): Neutral → Good. Now: Multi-decade indigenous programmes (LCA Mk1A/Mk2, IMRH, AMCA) are pursued with tenacity and capacity built ahead of orders, but independence is structurally limited by government ownership, a single customer and state-appointed leadership. | The long view had said: Long-term vision on indigenisation is real and pursued across a decade, but independence is limited — HAL is a government-owned entity whose orders, approvals (AoN, CCS) and even LCA design authority (ADA) sit outside its control, and it depends on GE for engines.
- **Disciplined stewards** (Management): Good → Excellent. Now: Entirely organic growth with no acquisitions, a large net-cash balance sheet (interest income of INR2,566 crores), internally funded programmes (INR4,000 crores IMRH, INR2,000 crores naval UH), and counter-cyclical procurement launched ahead of contract signature. | The long view had said: Stewardship is organic and prudent — no large M&A, self-funded R&D (Rs 4,000 crore IMRH, Rs 2,000 crore UHM from internal resources), proactive counter-cyclical procurement and pre-contract manufacturing, and a large net cash position with no leverage discussed.
- **Asset turn** (Return on Capital): Poor → Weak. Now: Every ₹1 of assets produces ₹0.25 of sales — a fairly asset-heavy business. And from the calls: The moat is exceptional but the business is not asset-light — inventory ballooned from 159 to 263 days on 18-36 month manufacturing cycles and INR33,050 crores of revenue sits on a heavy, expanding fixed-asset and working-capital base. | The long view had said: Every ₹1 of assets produces ₹0.25 of sales — a fairly asset-heavy business. And from the calls: The business is structurally asset-heavy and turning slower — revenue of ~Rs 33,050 crore on a Rs 14,000-15,000 crore capex programme, with inventory days deteriorating from 159 to 263 and 18-36 month manufacturing cycles, though the moat itself is strong.

**Checks that regressed:**

- **Oligopoly (vs duopoly)** (Industry Structure): Good → Weak. Now: This is not a stable oligopoly but a monopoly supplier facing a monopsony buyer — the MoD/armed forces hold all negotiating power, which management acknowledges by framing pricing around affordability and cost optimisation. | The long view had said: HAL faces no domestic rival in integrated military aircraft manufacture and treats private firms as suppliers (L&T, VEM, Alpha Tocol, Tata TASL, PTC) rather than competitors, but its single end customer — the Government of India — holds enormous negotiating power over pricing and order timing.
- **Capex for growth (not maintenance)** (Capital Allocation): Excellent → Good. Now: Capex is explicitly growth-oriented (third LCA line at Nashik, Tumakuru LCH, LCA Mk-2, GE-414 and IMRH engine infrastructure) with INR12,000 crores planned to 2030, though FY26 spend of INR2,386 crores ran below the ~INR3,000 crore/yr plan and incremental returns are still unproven given LCA delivery slippage. | The long view had said: Sustained, explicitly growth-oriented capex — Rs 14,000-15,000 crore five-year plan reaffirmed across FY24 and FY25 calls, executed at Rs 2,386 crore in FY26, funding new LCA lines (Nashik third line commissioned Oct 2025), Tumakuru helicopter factory, forging presses and GE-414/IMRH engine infrastructure against a Rs 2.54 lakh crore order book.
- **Assurance benefits** (Customer Benefits): Excellent → Good. Now: The customer is buying certainty of national-security supply backed by military type certifications, and management frames the relationship as trust rather than price — though repeated LCA Mk-1A and HTT-40 slippage weakens the reliability claim. | The long view had said: The customer buys mission reliability and sovereign assurance of supply above price — HAL invests ahead of contracts to guarantee delivery (2 Dorniers to Guyana within 10 days, 15 RD-33 engines and the first AL-31FP within a month of contract signature) and frames indigenisation as insurance against foreign supply disruption.

*Unchanged: Convenience benefits; Customer types (retail vs corporate); Structural end-market growth; Technology advantage; Cyclical market growth; Intangible benefits; Halo effect (caution); Share donators; Distribution advantage; Partial monopoly / broken competition / switching costs; People matter (talent development); Cost of working capital; Return on capital (headline); Gain in market share; Pricing, Mix and Volume; Security by obscurity; Mini-monopolies.*

### Bucket 2 — Multibagger patterns (11 patterns)

The long view matches 4 of the 11 winning patterns; the one-year view matches 5. Genuinely re-tested pattern verdicts: 1 strengthened, 2 weakened, 7 unchanged; 1 carried forward unchanged from the long view.

*Foundation test: PARTIAL on the long view, PARTIAL on the one-year view.*

**That strengthened:**

- **Brand Strength**: PARTIAL → LIKELY FIT. Why now: There is institutional standing — Maharatna status (first defence PSU, Oct 2024), national-champion positioning, and customer trust invoked directly — plus name-stretch into adjacencies (SSLV licence with ISRO, SJ-100 civil aircraft MoU, LEAP parts for Safran). But this is sovereign-mandate standing rather than brand power: no A&P spend, no distributor leverage, and no premium pricing attributable to the name. (The long view had said: Management repeatedly invokes HAL's institutional heritage and trust (Maharatna status, 24,000 employees, 'trust of our armed forces'), but there is no A&P spend, distributor leverage, premium pricing or brand-stretch evidence — the 'brand' language is rhetorical rather than commercially demonstrated.)

**That weakened:**

- **Pricing Power**: LIKELY FIT → QUANT SIGNAL. Why now: The calls discuss margins (EBITDA held at 30-31%) but never price increases, elasticity, premiumisation or price realisation on renewals; pricing is set contract-by-contract with a monopsony government buyer and is simply not addressed. Insufficient evidence either way. (The long view had said: Conditional/contractual rather than brand pricing power: escalation and exchange-rate-variation clauses are embedded in every manufacturing and ROH contract, and the FY24 Change Order 3 repricing added Rs. 1,502 cr — but prices are set by negotiation with a single monopsony customer, not raised at will.)
- **Friendly Middleman**: PARTIAL → NO FIT. Why now: Sales run directly to the paying customer — IAF, Army, Navy, Coast Guard, ISRO — with contracts signed and platforms delivered to the end user, not recommended by any intermediary professional. No trade training or channel-relationship investment appears anywhere in the four calls. (The long view had said: No evidence in any of the six calls of a prescriber/installer channel; HAL sells directly to the Indian armed forces and government agencies.)

*Carried forward from the long view unchanged (the window could not re-test these): Forward Integrators (PARTIAL).*

*Unchanged: Innovation Dominance (STRONG FIT); Cost to Replicate (STRONG FIT); Recurring Revenue (Product Upfront) (LIKELY FIT); Low Price Plus (PARTIAL); Market Share Gainers (PARTIAL); Corporate Culture (PARTIAL); Toll Roads (NO FIT).*

### Bucket 3 — Risks (8 channels)

Genuinely re-tested risk verdicts: 1 eased, 2 worsened, 2 unchanged; 3 carried forward unchanged from the long view. Financial resilience: Sound on the long view, Sound on the one-year view.

**That eased:**

- **Technological Innovation**: WATCH → LOW. Why now: Across the four calls the innovation cadence is incremental and continuous rather than disruptive — R&D at ~8.4% of revenue, 223 IPRs filed in FY26 (2,842 cumulative), and a steady stream of derivative platforms (ALH Dhruv NG maiden flight, HTT-40 series production, indigenous Shakti civil engine certification, LCA Mk2/AMCA/IMRH in development). No analyst on these calls raised platform-level disruption of HAL's installed base. (The long view had said: HAL sits in a high-innovation industry where its portfolio is largely manned platforms and legacy Russian-origin airframes, and by 2026 analysts are asking about UCAVs and unmanned programmes; management answers with early-stage work (rotary UAV in ground test, CATS Warrior yet to fly). Offsetting this is a steady, funded small-scale innovation cadence rather than a bet-the-company platform shift.)

**That worsened:**

- **Stakeholder Concentration**: ELEVATED → HIGH RISK. Why now: Both ends are concentrated: essentially one customer group (IAF, Army, Navy, Coast Guard) buys nearly all output, and on the supply side a single foreign supplier — GE for F404 engines — has repeatedly gated the LCA Mk1A programme, with the FY26 call dominated by analysts pressing on whether deliveries slip again. Management's answer rests entirely on GE's commitment. (The long view had said: Effectively one customer (the Indian armed forces via MoD) and single-source critical suppliers whose slippage directly caps revenue: GE engine non-delivery stalled the flagship LCA Mark-1A programme across FY25 and FY26, and Russian-origin AL-31FP/RD-33 content leaves supply exposed to the Ukraine conflict. Analysts pressed on this relationship in consecutive calls.)
- **Cyclicality**: LOW → WATCH. Why now: Revenue is anchored to the Indian armed forces' capital acquisition programmes (LCA Mk1A, LCH, ALH, Su-30 upgrades) — classic customer-capex exposure where order timing and platform availability swing the top line, as in FY23 when manufacturing was 'subdued' on lower platforms and FY26 when Tejas Mk1A and HTT-40 slipped. The offset is that repair & overhaul, an operations-linked flow business, is now the majority of revenue (INR20,524cr of INR33,050cr in FY26) and repeatedly plugs manufacturing shortfalls, and management keeps investing counter to the delivery delays (INR2,386cr capex, INR2,794cr R&D in FY26). (The long view had said: Across all four calls revenue and margins are strikingly stable (revenue ~Rs 30,105cr FY25 to Rs 33,050cr FY26, +7%; operating margin held at 27% then 30-31% for three straight years), and roughly two-thirds of revenue is repair-and-overhaul on an installed fleet — an operations-linked flow stream rather than a capex-swing one. The order book compounded through the whole period (Rs 85,000cr in 2022 to Rs 2,54,538cr in 2026) with lines booked to 2032-33.)

*Carried forward from the long view unchanged (the window could not re-test these): New Entrants (WATCH); Shifting Consumer Preferences (NO SIGNAL); Fashion Risk (NO SIGNAL).*

*Unchanged: Government Dependency (HIGH RISK); Good Enough Goods (LOW).*

**Financial resilience:** Sound on the long view (none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.) vs Sound on the one-year view (none of the 3 stress checks (leverage, cash conversion, returns on capital) is flagged.)

## How this comparison was built

Both sides ran the same three analysis engines (BusinessAnalysis, MultibaggerPattern, QualityRisks) through their respective orchestrators:

- **Long view (full AnalystSkill):** statements over ~a decade, the whole call timeline — engines ran with_calls.
- **Last one year (RecentAnalystSkill):** latest fiscal year (prior year as baseline only), last 12 months of calls — engines ran 6 items carried from the full-history analysis, with_calls.

Comparison rules: only verdicts assessed on both views are counted; one-year-lens silences are listed but never scored as regressions; every movement line carries both sides' evidence so the WHY of the change is visible, not just the direction. Research tooling; not investment advice.
