**Checks that improved:**

Now: As a lender the model is inherently balance-sheet-heavy rather than asset-light, though returns on those assets are high — ROA 5.8-6.0% on 2.9x leverage. | The long view had said: Lending is intrinsically asset-heavy — the balance sheet IS the product, with leverage of only 2.8x and ROA ~5.5-5.8% coming from spread not turnover — and the moat is a distribution/underwriting edge rather than something protecting a high-turn model.
- **Barriers to entry and rationality** (Industry Structure): Neutral → Good. Now: Pricing discipline held through the easing cycle — portfolio yield fell only 10-20bps while cost of funds fell 50bps, giving no evidence of a discount war, and HFC licensing plus branch reach limits casual entry. | The long view had said: Barriers are moderate — regulatory licensing, funding access and on-ground underwriting create some protection — but the company competes on price at the margin, passing on rate cuts to defend the book, and faces balance-transfer pressure from better-funded banks.

**Checks that regressed:**

- **Security by obscurity** (Industry Structure): Good → Weak. Now: Far from under-the-radar — a listed, broker-hosted quarterly call franchise in a sector explicitly flagged as attracting capital toward a Rs. 2.5 lakh crore market. | The long view had said: The Rs.5-25 lakh ticket, self-employed, Tier-3/4 self-construction niche is deliberately avoided by banks and large NBFCs due to underwriting cost, and management explicitly stays out of Tier-1/2 builder-tie-up markets.
- **Partial monopoly / broken competition / switching costs** (Industry Structure): Good → Neutral. Now: Switching costs are modest but not trivial — balance-transfer-out ran at 4.5% and had to be actively defended with data-driven retention in a falling-rate environment. | The long view had said: There is real switching friction in Tier-2/3 self-employed mortgage lending — collateral is already pledged, LTVs are conservative and BT-out actually fell to 4.5% during a rate-cutting cycle when refinancing incentives were highest — but this is friction, not a captive aftermarket.
- **Technology advantage** (Competitive Advantage): Good → Neutral. Now: Tech is competent and delivered on time (e-KYC, ML scorecard, digital sourcing) but is industry table-stakes rather than a deep, multi-product edge. | The long view had said: Genuine operational tech depth — Salesforce since 2013 as an early mortgage adopter, five in-house applications, 99% e-NACH, 98% e-sign, a business rule engine that overrides human bias, and new digital sourcing at 4-5% of disbursement — but it is process technology, not a multi-product proprietary tech edge.
- **Oligopoly (vs duopoly)** (Industry Structure): Neutral → Weak. Now: The affordable housing finance market is fragmented rather than a stable oligopoly, and borrowers retain negotiating power through refinancing, as the BT-out defence shows. | The long view had said: Affordable housing finance is fragmented and competitive rather than a stable oligopoly; management itself flags that rate cuts will intensify competition for volumes and that peers price below it on cost of funds.
- **Independent, long-term and tenacious** (Management): Excellent → Good. - **Customer types (retail vs corporate)** (Customer Benefits): Good → Neutral. Now: The customer base is correctly identified and served — self-employed retail in Tier-2/3 with identical HL and LAP behaviour — but the benefit delivered is access and service, not the intangible pricing power the retail match ideally requires. | The long view had said: The customer base is retail (98% women borrowers, >80% self-employed, average ticket Rs.10 lakh), and the benefit offered — access, speed, hand-holding collections — is well matched to that cohort, though the offering is assurance/convenience rather than the intangible brand pull the framework prizes for retail.
- **Distribution advantage** (Competitive Advantage): Excellent → Good. Now: A granular 300+ branch Tier-2/3 network built at 40-45 branches a year gives real reach where in-person collection is vital, though comparable affordable-HFC peers operate similar models. | The long view had said: The 300+ branch Tier-2/3 network built over 15 years with 98% in-house sourcing and in-house collections is precisely the reach rivals cannot economically replicate in a segment where on-ground servicing and collection is the business.

Now: Borrowers gain access and speed rather than paying a premium for certainty — faster credit decisions are framed as a cost and inclusion benefit, not a trust-based pricing lever. | The long view had said: The customer values speed and certainty of sanction — a six-day turnaround from login to disbursement in a segment where formal credit is otherwise inaccessible — which is why the borrower tolerates a ~14.8% rate rather than shopping purely on price.
- **Mini-monopolies** (Industry Structure): Neutral → Weak. Now: No monopoly-like position — the company competes in a crowded affordable housing finance field where peers are cited on the same call and customers can refinance away. | The long view had said: No monopoly-like position exists in the customer's mind — the BT-out rate of 4.3-5.5% to banks and larger NBFCs shows customers do leave for cheaper rates, and management concedes competition intensifies when rates fall.

## How this comparison was built

Both sides ran the same three analysis engines (BusinessAnalysis, MultibaggerPattern, QualityRisks) through their respective orchestrators:

- **Long view (full AnalystSkill):** statements over ~a decade, the whole call timeline — engines ran with_calls.
- **Last one year (RecentAnalystSkill):** latest fiscal year (prior year as baseline only), last 12 months of calls — engines ran 5 items carried from the full-history analysis, with_calls.

Comparison rules: only verdicts assessed on both views are counted; one-year-lens silences are listed but never scored as regressions; every movement line carries both sides' evidence so the WHY of the change is visible, not just the direction. Research tooling; not investment advice.
