# Indus Towers (INDUSTOWER) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Indus Towers owns and operates passive telecom infrastructure in India — the towers, power systems and site equipment that mobile networks physically sit on — and rents space on those towers to telecom operators, who mount their own antennas and radios on them. Its customers are a small number of large mobile operators; the calls repeatedly name Bharti Airtel and Vodafone Idea as its two major customers (and past promoters), so revenue is highly concentrated. It makes money from long-term contracts (Master Service Agreements, renewed in 2022 on a roughly 10-year framework) that charge rent per tenant per site, plus extra 'loading' charges when a customer hangs more equipment on an existing tower — such as 5G radios — and it also recovers the cost of running the sites, where diesel, electricity and rent are its main expenses. It operates pan-India, from metros to remote areas like Lakshadweep, hill regions of Kerala and defence establishments, and by March 2026 ran about 264,500 macro towers carrying about 428,000 co-locations.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Macro towers (tower rentals / co-locations)** — The core business: large ground- and rooftop-based towers rented out to operators, where the same tower can carry more than one customer ('co-locations') — about 264,500 macro towers and 428,000 co-locations as of March 2026, with an 'industry-leading' tenancy ratio of 1.62 tenants per tower, making this overwhelmingly the main source of revenue.
- **Loading (additional equipment on existing towers)** — Incremental charges when a customer adds more radios or antennas to a tower it already occupies — driven by 5G rollout and network densification (industry 5G base stations rose from ~450,000 in 2024 to ~531,000 by early 2026), and repeatedly described as a growing 'fillip' to revenue that needs little new capital.
- **Lean / leaner towers** — Slimmer, lower-cost tower structures used where a full macro tower doesn't fit or isn't needed, reported separately from macro towers — over 14,000 lean towers by March 2026, taking the total portfolio including their co-locations to roughly 442,000, so a small but steadily growing add-on.
- **Non-tower segment — IBS, small cells and DAS** — In-Building Solutions and Distributed Antenna Systems that provide coverage inside buildings and in dense or enclosed spaces such as metros (e.g. DMRC), tunnels and highways, plus small cells for urban decongestion — management says it reached a leadership position in IBS in Q4 FY26 and expects growth, but gives no revenue figures, so it is presented as a smaller emerging business.
- **Site energy and operations (uptime, solar, diesel reduction)** — Running and powering the sites so networks stay up — a cost centre and service promise rather than a separate product, with uptime around 99.97-99.98%, about 42,400 sites given solar access by March 2026, and diesel use falling roughly 4-7% year-on-year even as tenancy grew; management treats energy cost reduction as one of its four strategic pillars.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### Cost to Replicate — STRONG FIT
*"Could a newcomer copy this?"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence (no computable check applies — the calls decide).

**What the calls show:** A pan-India grid of ~264,500 towers on finite, right-of-way-approved sites with landlord contracts and 99.977% uptime is not rebuildable by a funded newcomer in a few years, and management articulates the switching barrier concretely — moving a grid degrades the operator's own end-customer experience and costs real money. The caveat is that this replication moat has not converted into pricing power against a concentrated customer base.

> "no operator would like to change their grid because it impacts the customers, end customers and customer satisfaction... it costs a good amount of money to shift these towers" — *management, on an earnings call*
