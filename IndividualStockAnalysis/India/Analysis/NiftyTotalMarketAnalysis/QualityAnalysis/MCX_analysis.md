# Multi Comm. Exc. (MCX) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

MCX runs an electronic exchange in India where people buy and sell commodity derivatives — futures and options contracts on things like gold, silver, crude oil, natural gas and base metals such as nickel — rather than trading the physical goods directly. Its customers reach it only through registered member brokers, and they fall into three groups: hedgers (mostly Indian MSMEs and corporates wanting to lock in prices), speculators and retail investors who take the other side of that risk, and institutions such as mutual funds running silver/gold ETFs, domestic financial institutions and foreign portfolio investors. It earns money essentially from trading activity on its platform, so revenue tracks turnover: FY26 consolidated revenue from operations more than doubled to INR 2,302 crores with PAT above INR 1,300 crores, as average daily futures-and-options turnover rose about 2.5x (to roughly INR 7.5 lakh crores a day by Q3 FY26). It operates only in India, is headquartered in Mumbai, is listed on BSE, works under SEBI's rules, and positions itself as the domestic venue for players too small to hedge on the much larger international exchanges.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Bullion (gold and silver futures and options)** — Contracts on gold and silver, including Gold Mini, Gold Ten futures, smaller-denomination contracts and silver monthly options expiry; management calls it the largest driver, at about 69% of average daily turnover in Q3 FY26 and growing more than 4x in FY26.
- **Energy (crude oil and natural gas)** — Cash-settled contracts on crude oil and natural gas, described as MCX's other big volume pillar that also grew strongly off a high base, and the segment where foreign portfolio investors are currently allowed and contribute a double-digit share.
- **Base metals and other commodities** — Contracts on metals such as nickel, where one trading lot is large (a nickel lot worth roughly INR 35-40 lakhs with margins of seven-eight lakhs), which management says limits small-investor participation compared with equities.
- **Index products** — Options on MCX's own commodity indices, such as monthly options on the MCX iCOMDEX Bullion Index, launched to widen the product range and improve liquidity and risk management for participants.
- **Clearing and settlement (MCX Clearing Corporation - MCX CCL)** — A separate subsidiary with its own MD and CEO that clears and settles trades, sets and collects margins (recomputed seven to ten times a day) and enforces penalty rules if a member fails to deliver.
- **Physical delivery and warehousing** — Contracts that end in actual delivery of gold and silver, handled through warehouses whose costs are covered by what participants pay for those services; management says increased delivery in Q3 FY26 added no extra cost to the company.
- **Membership, distribution and participant onboarding** — Growth in registered member brokers and their client codes (UCCs) is how MCX widens its reach; new members joining and brokers making the commodity screen feel like their equity screen were cited as the main drivers of the recent jump in traded UCCs, with FPIs at about 2-3% of overall average daily turnover.
- **Technology platform** — The trading and settlement systems MCX runs — a Deutsche Börse T7 trading engine paired with a TCS-built clearing and settlement back end, a customization that took over a year of delays and mock-trading rounds before going live — with ongoing capex to add capacity as volumes rise.
- **Market development and hedger education** — Ongoing outreach to build the user base — MSME programs with SIDBI, work with AMFI and SEBI to let mutual funds and ETFs use the platform, and the FY26 'Price in India: Hedge in India' drive — treated by management as the long-run engine of volume rather than a separate revenue line.

### The story in depth

MCX is, in plain terms, a tollbooth on India's commodity trading — and almost every strength in these records traces back to that one position. It is effectively synonymous with commodity derivatives in the country, holding both the pool of liquidity and the warehousing and delivery infrastructure participants must use (Mini-monopolies, score 2). That pool feeds itself: hedgers attract speculators, who deepen open interest, which pulls in more hedgers (Network effects, score 2). What the exchange actually sells is not a cheap trade but counterparty certainty — settlement guarantee, the settlement guarantee fund, margining seven to ten times a day, delivery integrity through volatile bullion markets — with price a secondary consideration (Assurance benefits, score 2). The economics that fall out of this are unusually good: every ₹100 of capital in the business generated ₹106 of operating cash this year, and technology spending put in ahead of volume growth has come back as revenue up 121% and EBITDA up 144% year-on-year. The multibagger screen reads the same facts and lands on Cost to Replicate as a STRONG FIT — a well-funded copycat with the identical rulebook has attacked bullion and energy and produced only shallow, one-day activity — with Toll Roads a LIKELY FIT, since the fee is a trivial slice of the value traded while the venue itself is indispensable.

The trouble is that the very things that make the toll road valuable also make it fragile in specific ways, and the risk review picks up the same traits from the other side. The fee-per-trade model that produces those margins is a direct function of commodity volatility: average daily turnover swung from roughly ₹2 lakh crore at the FY25 exit to ₹7.5 lakh crore in Q3 FY26, revenue rose 121% and profit 151%, and management itself concedes the cyclicality — hence Cyclicality as a HIGH RISK, and hence the quality framework marking Persistence of growth at -1, with sales averaging about 24% a year but arriving lumpily. The same concentration that creates the liquidity moat is a dependency: bullion alone is 69% of turnover, growth has leaned on a handful of newly onboarded discount brokers, and the whole trading and clearing stack sits on one vendor, TCS (Stakeholder Concentration, WATCH). The monopoly is also a licensed monopoly, so nearly every growth lever belongs to the regulator — electricity futures delayed over a year, foreign investor access to bullion pending a consultation paper, colocation still not permitted, settlement-fund contributions set by a SEBI formula (Government Dependency, HIGH RISK). And the market-development spending that scores well as a strength — the education drive, the MSME and bank outreach, the 'Price in India: Hedge in India' campaign — is necessary precisely because MCX cannot reach end clients directly; all flow, onboarding and client codes sit with member brokers, which is why Friendly Middleman and Forward Integrators come out as PARTIAL and NO FIT respectively rather than as an advantage.

Put together, the picture is a genuinely defended franchise with a narrow set of things that could undo it, and the combined rating of 53 out of 100 — 'Decent', three stars — reflects that split: strong business quality at 63, weaker scores of 44 on both multibagger fit and risk safety, with resilience judged SOUND. The competing exchange has listed near-identical bullion and energy contracts and shifted expiry dates to pull activity, and it already offers colocation in equities while MCX waits for permission — the New Entrants and Good Enough Goods risks are the same fact seen twice, since exchange contracts are replicable and brokers, not end clients, decide where orders go. So far the liquidity network has held, with bullion market share described as untouched over two years, which is why the screen calls MCX a defender rather than a share gainer (Market Share Gainers, LIKELY FIT, with no explicit rising-share numbers). Management's record cuts both ways: the forced migration off the legacy platform to TCS was a big-bang replacement with repeated delays, no fallback and admitted functionality gaps, but it was discussed candidly — including the one-sided contract and the absence of a Plan B — and ultimately delivered, which is why Technological Innovation sits at WATCH and Corporate Culture at PARTIAL rather than worse. Against that, there is no cost discipline to fall back on: in Q4 FY26 licence fees and other expenses grew faster than revenue and management explicitly declined to focus on cost reduction (Low Price Plus, NO FIT), so if turnover normalises, the cost base does not flex with it.
