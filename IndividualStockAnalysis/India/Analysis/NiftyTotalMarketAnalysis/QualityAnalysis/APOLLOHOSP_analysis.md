# Apollo Hospitals Enterprise Ltd. (APOLLOHOSP) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: HEALTHCARE SERVICES*

Apollo Hospitals Enterprise is an Indian healthcare company that runs a chain of large private hospitals plus the retail and digital businesses built around them. It earns most of its money treating patients — inpatient surgeries and procedures in specialties like cardiac, oncology, neurosciences, gastroenterology, orthopedics and transplants — with bills paid mainly by private insurance and by patients themselves (together 83% of inpatient revenue in FY26), plus a smaller stream from international 'medical value travel' patients. Alongside the hospitals it sells medicines through roughly 6,000 owned pharmacy stores and a distribution business, runs the Apollo 24/7 online health app (46 million users), and operates a network of clinics, diagnostic labs, day-surgery, maternity and fertility centres. It operates across India — metro, Tier 1 and Tier 2 cities — with consolidated revenue of INR 25,229 crore and profit of INR 1,942 crore in FY26.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Healthcare Services (Hospitals Division)** — The core hospital business — beds, surgeries and specialist care, measured by occupancy (68% group-wide in Q4 FY26) and revenue per bed; it is the biggest and most profitable vertical at roughly INR 3,268 crore of revenue in Q4 FY26 at ~24% EBITDA margin and ~25% return on capital, and management is adding about 1,400-1,500 new beds across Hyderabad, Kolkata, Bangalore, Gurgaon and Pune.
- **Apollo HealthCo (pharmacy distribution + Apollo 24/7 digital)** — The medicine business — wholesale/retail pharmacy distribution through thousands of stores (private-label and generics ~15-16% of pharmacy sales) plus the Apollo 24/7 app for online medicine orders, doctor consults and diagnostics; it crossed INR 10,000 crore of annual revenue in FY26, with the pharmacy side steadily profitable (INR 195 crore EBITDA in Q4 FY26) while the digital arm ran losses that management has narrowed almost to breakeven, and the whole unit is being demerged and merged with Keimed to target a INR 25,000 crore revenue run-rate at 7% EBITDA.
- **AHLL (Apollo Health & Lifestyle)** — The 'retail healthcare' arm outside hospitals — diagnostic labs, primary-care and specialty clinics, day-surgery, and the Cradle maternity, fertility and Spectra formats; it is the smallest vertical (INR 489 crore revenue in Q4 FY26, growing ~20-24% a year with margins improved to 15.3%), and in May 2026 its Cradle and Fertility units (INR 450 crore revenue) were valued at INR 1,550 crore and combined with Cloudnine, leaving Apollo a 9.9% stake in that platform.

### The story in depth

Apollo Hospitals comes out of all three reviews as one of the strongest names the framework has scored — 86 out of 100, or Outstanding, built from 76 on business quality, a perfect 100 on multibagger pattern fit, and 88 on risk safety, with overall resilience judged Sound. The single thread running through every part of the record is the same one: rising private insurance coverage is pulling Indian patients out of small, sub-scale nursing homes and into organised hospital chains, and Apollo is positioned to catch them. That one force explains the top quality scores (structural end-market growth, gain in market share, and a named pool of weak 'share donators'), and it explains three of the strongest patterns — Market Share Gainers, Friendly Middleman (insurance brokers, corporate HR desks and 150 newly onboarded doctors all steering patients in), and Pricing Power, where a 2% hike in Q1FY23 became revenue per occupied bed of Rs 59,523 by FY24 and an explicit 3% tariff plus 5% realised price by FY26, with volumes still growing 5-7%. Price rises that stick while volumes keep growing are what a company with genuine pricing power looks like, and the steady 15% operating margin moving only about ±1.9 points a year says the same thing from the cost side.

The most interesting connections are the places where a single trait is simultaneously the source of a strength and the source of a risk. First, the same insurance system that delivers the patients also sets the rules: 83% of in-patient revenue coming from insurance and self-pay is what keeps Government Dependency merely a 'Watch' rather than a threat to the revenue base, but it is also exactly why the September 2025 GST changes knocked Rs 30-35 crore a quarter off pharmacy GMV and pushed out about Rs 7 crore of insurance commission revenue. Second, the forward integration into roughly 6,000 pharmacies and the Apollo 24/7 platform with 46 million users is the standout structural move of the period and a big part of the Cost to Replicate barrier — but it is also the entire source of the two live risks. Venture-funded e-pharmacy rivals sit in that vertical, where FY23 saw 18% discounts and a deliberate overspend to reach the number two position, and the private-label generics push at 15.5% of sales is at once the main driver of the targeted FY27 pharmacy margin step-up to 6.5-7% and the reason cheaper generics can attack Apollo the same way. Third, the scarcity that protects the hospitals also slows them: Rs 450 crore for 5.67 acres in Gurugram and a 24-month build keeps competitors out, but it is why growth requires Rs 900 crore projects and phased commissioning of 1,400-1,500 new beds to avoid diluting a 25-26% return on capital.

The weak spots are honest and mostly structural rather than operational. Industry Structure is the lowest-scoring quality area at 0.67, dragged down by the fact that Apollo is the opposite of an overlooked company — a large-cap index name whose calls are attended by Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, BofA, UBS, HSBC and Macquarie, in a sector awash with capital. Two patterns fall short of full marks for related reasons: Innovation Dominance is only a likely fit because robotics, TAVI/TAVR and CAR-T are adopted technology rather than anything Apollo owns, and Recurring Revenue is only partial because management never discusses how many of those 46 million digital users actually repeat. And the deliberate premium positioning — 68%-plus surgical mix, revenue per patient up to Rs 1,87,208, a push into high-acuity specialties — is why the Low Price Plus and Toll Roads patterns are outright no-fits: hospital care is a large, visible line on the insurer's bill, so tariffs get negotiated rather than simply set. What offsets all of this is management. Leadership has been stable throughout, the digital build was carried for four years through open analyst scepticism, taking annual losses of roughly Rs 550 crore down to Rs 16 crore of quarterly cash losses, and the record on candor is unusually clean — pre-announcing a slipped breakeven, explaining the GMV restatement, correcting its own misstated number live on a call. Guidance given has largely been delivered: the promised move from 60% to 70% occupancy landed at 68%.

## What to watch

- New Entrants risk: Apollo 24/7 is only near cash breakeven (losses down to Rs 16 crore). If discounting from venture-funded e-pharmacy rivals re-widens, the Forward Integrators pattern turns from an asset into a cash drain.
- Good Enough Goods risk: the FY27 pharmacy margin step-up to 6.5-7% rests on private label at 15.5% of sales. If cheaper generics and discount platforms squeeze that, the promised margin improvement does not arrive.
- Pricing Power pattern: the FY26 split of 3% tariff and 5% realised price alongside 5-7% volume growth is the core proof point. Price increases that stop sticking, or volumes that stall when they are taken, would undercut the strongest pattern in the file.
- Government Dependency risk: policy has already cost Rs 30-35 crore a quarter of pharmacy GMV via the GST change and deferred about Rs 7 crore of insurance commission. Further insurance-regulator or tax shifts would widen a swing factor the review currently calls bounded.
- Capex for growth check: 1,400-1,500 new beds are being commissioned in phases against a 25-26% return on capital and 68% occupancy. If the new beds fill slowly and returns fall, growth capex stops being the strength this check scores it as.
