# UTI AMC (UTIAMC) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

UTI AMC is an Indian asset manager that invests other people's money for a fee: it runs UTI Mutual Fund, a family of investment schemes covering active equity, fixed income (debt), hybrid, and low-cost passive products like ETFs, index funds and 'smart beta' funds. Its customers are mainly Indian retail savers — reached through bank branches, national distributors, independent mutual fund distributors and its own branch network across 699 districts, with a deliberate push into smaller 'B30' tier-2 and tier-3 towns — plus institutions such as the EPFO and CMPFO provident-fund bodies, government and private-sector pension money under the NPS, and overseas investors. It earns money by charging an annual management fee on the assets it manages (the calls cite a weighted average AMC yield of about 40 basis points, i.e. roughly 0.4% of assets a year), so revenue rises and falls with market levels and net inflows. Operations are centred on India, with international arms selling India-focused funds to institutions in 38 countries via offices/licences in Ireland, Paris, GIFT City (Gujarat) and a new US registration; group assets under management were about Rs 23.4 lakh crore as of March 2026, of which the mutual fund itself was Rs 3.88 lakh crore.

**The parts of the business:**

- **UTI Mutual Fund (domestic mutual fund business)** — The core business — pooled investment schemes sold to retail and institutional investors in India, whose quarterly average AUM grew from Rs 2.24 lakh crore in mid-2022 to Rs 3.93 lakh crore by June 2026, making it by far the main driver of the company's fee income.
- **Active equity and hybrid funds** — Stock-picking and mixed equity-debt schemes (equity QAAUM was Rs 84,777 crore in March 2024); management adds products to fill gaps, such as the UTI Balanced Advantage Fund launched in 2023 and the UTI Multicap Fund that raised about Rs 1,000 crore in FY26.
- **Passive business — ETFs, index funds and smart beta** — Low-cost funds that simply track indices, described as a fast-growing franchise where UTI claims a leadership position in smart beta; ETF plus index fund AUM was Rs 24,897 crore in FY26, with a pipeline of new launches including sectoral and thematic products.
- **Fixed income / debt and liquid funds** — Bond and short-term cash-management schemes; the calls highlight consistent performance with no credit rating downgrades in Q1 FY27 and a record AUM milestone for the liquid fund.
- **SIP franchise (systematic investment plans)** — Not a separate legal business but the company's key recurring-inflow channel, where investors commit a fixed monthly amount — SIP AUM grew from Rs 17,788 crore in June 2022 to Rs 45,595 crore by June 2026, and 76% of new SIP registrations in FY26 came through digital channels.
- **UTI Retirement Solutions (pension fund management)** — Manages National Pension System money for government and non-government subscribers under a PFRDA licence; AUM reached Rs 4.02 lakh crore in FY26 (up 11.8%, with private-sector pension assets up 46%) and management calls it an important strategic growth area, though its own profit is small (Rs 10.7 crore in Q1 FY23).
- **PMS / institutional portfolio management division** — Runs segregated mandates for large institutions — UTI was re-appointed as one of the portfolio managers for the EPFO (third consecutive term) and one of two for the CMPFO (second term), each for five years.
- **UTI International (offshore business)** — Sells India-focused funds to overseas institutions — pensions, insurers, banks and asset managers across 38 countries — with Rs 25,990 crore of AUM in mid-2022, a flagship Ireland-domiciled Indian Dynamic Equity Fund (USD 970.6 million), a French licence and Paris office for Europe, and management fees of about Rs 32 crore a quarter in FY23.
- **UTI Capital / alternatives (AIF, private credit and private equity)** — The smallest arm, running closed-end alternative funds such as the Structured Debt Opportunities Funds and Multi Opportunities Funds (total AUM Rs 1,551 crore in mid-2022, loss-making then); by FY27 it had returned capital on two private-credit funds, launched MOF II and SDOF 4, hired a head of private equity, and is building GIFT City vehicles so foreign investors can access the domestic funds.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

UTI AMC sits in an end market that is plainly expanding — industry assets grew from Rs 37.7 lakh crore in June 2022 to Rs 84.2 lakh crore in June 2026, folios reached about 28 crore, and monthly SIP contributions run at Rs 32,087 crore — and that structural tailwind is the single highest-scoring item in the quality work. The company converts it into a genuine annuity: fees accrue on installed assets, and its own SIP book grew from Rs 17,788 crore in Q1FY23 to Rs 45,595 crore by Q1FY27 across 29.53 lakh active SIP folios, which is why the Recurring Revenue pattern registers as a partial fit. The economics that flow from this are respectable — Rs 48 of operating profit out of every Rs 100 of sales, an 18% cash return on the capital employed, and only about 17 days of cash tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Management behaves the way owners would want: growth is organic with no debt or acquisitions, headcount was cut from 1,402 to 1,248 through a voluntary retirement scheme, the supervisor-to-field ratio moved from 1:1 to 1:5, cost per transaction fell 31%, branches were added at what the company calls net zero incremental cost, and shareholders receive a stated, repeatable Rs 40 per share dividend at roughly a 95% payout. Calls stay low-key and operational, with even the incoming CEO deflecting credit to the platform — the opposite of a promoted story.

The trouble is that several of these same traits are simultaneously the strengths and the risks. Distribution is the clearest case: sales are overwhelmingly placed by intermediaries — bank, national and independent distributors choose the scheme while the investor pays — which makes Friendly Middleman the only strong pattern fit in the whole screen, and it is exactly why the New Entrants channel is flagged, since those distributors take 50-85% of the fee, aggressive competitors have normalised higher commission payouts, and direct plans are already about 30% of equity and hybrid assets. Cost discipline is the second case. The push into low-cost passive products supports the Low Price Plus and Innovation reads — Smart Beta leadership, a five-product smart-beta shelf, G-Sec ETFs, consistent low tracking difference — but it is also precisely the Good Enough Goods risk: index and ETF assets earn about 8 basis points against 72-73 basis points for active equity, and index/ETF assets of Rs 1,76,673 crore are now nearly double active equity assets of Rs 95,824 crore. Growth is therefore volume-led into the thinner-margin product, which is why Pricing Power and Toll Roads are both scored no fit and why blended yield has fallen steadily from the 40-41 basis points of FY23 with management guiding to further dilution since 2022. Pricing is not really the company's to set in any case: the regulator ended B30 accrual commission in February 2023, which management said accounted for three quarters of the yield drag, and later expense-ratio and exit-load changes had to be absorbed and passed on to intermediaries — the reason Government Dependency is the one channel rated high risk. Profit margins swinging about 6.7 points a year point the same way: key costs sit outside management's hands.

What ties the weak scores together is that scale and heritage are not converting into share. Gross-sales share fell from 8.5% in Q1FY23 to 7.79% in FY24 to 6.1% in FY26 and 5.5% in Q4FY26, equity assets grew 5.46% against industry growth of 12.6%, and NPS share slid from 27.37% in FY22 to 24.16% in Q1FY27 — so Market Share Gainers is a clear no fit, the market-share check scores negative even though at only 5-6% share there is obvious room to grow, and the growth area of the quality framework scores zero. Sustained marketing — investor awareness programmes, a newspaper tie-up, a Google partnership reaching 10 crore individuals — has not turned into sales either, which is why the advertising check is negative and why Brand Strength and Cost to Replicate stall at partial: the 60-year name, 699-district footprint and repeat institutional mandates are real and hard to rebuild, but they buy neither a higher price nor a bigger share while a well-funded newer competitor takes both. Demand is also performance-sensitive and market-sensitive rather than contractual — the 2022 call ties share erosion to slipping rankings in flagship schemes, only 50-60% of fixed income assets sit in the top two quartiles by FY26, and profit swings with markets, with FY24 described as a stellar year on a 75% rise in consolidated profit. Concentration adds to it: EPFO alone accounts for Rs 10.63 lakh crore of the Rs 12.15 lakh crore portfolio management book and is re-won only through open bidding every five years. Leadership has churned heavily across three chairmen and multiple senior changes, holding Corporate Culture to partial despite honest, itemised disclosure of one-offs. The combined rating lands at 43 out of 100 — Mixed, two stars — from 57 on business quality, 25 on multibagger fit and 38 on risk safety, with resilience judged sound. In plain terms: a well-run, cash-generative, shareholder-friendly business taking a steadily smaller slice of a fast-growing pie, at a steadily lower price per slice.

Reversal in the gross-sales share trend (Market Share Gainers pattern and the 'Gain in market share' check) — a stop to the 8.5% to 5.5% slide, or a quarter where equity growth at least matches industry growth.
