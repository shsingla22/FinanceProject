# AWL Agri Busine. (AWL) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

AWL Agri Business (formerly Adani Wilmar) is an Indian packaged-food and edible-oil company that buys agricultural raw materials — crude palm, soya, sunflower and mustard oil, wheat, rice, sugar and castor — refines or processes them at its own plants, and sells them mostly in branded consumer packs under names like Fortune, Kohinoor, King's and Raag. Its customers are ordinary Indian households, reached through about 950,000 directly-served retail outlets, over 60,000 rural towns, e-commerce and quick-commerce, hotels/restaurants/caterers (HoReCa), plus branded exports to 30-plus countries and a Bangladesh subsidiary. It makes money on the spread between raw-material cost and selling price on very large volumes — roughly 6.8 million tonnes and over ₹74,000 crore of revenue in FY26, at about ₹11,000-12,000 gross profit and ~₹3,500 EBITDA per tonne, giving ₹2,300+ crore EBITDA and just over ₹1,000 crore profit. Because it carries big inventories of imported, dollar-priced commodities, its profits swing with global oil prices, the rupee and hedging, which management repeatedly flags as the main risk.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Edible Oil** — Refines and sells branded cooking oils (palm, soya, sunflower, mustard, including premium cold-pressed 'Pehli Dhaar') — by far the biggest part, roughly ₹17,500 crore of the ~₹21,000 crore Q4 FY26 revenue and historically the source of almost all segment profit (₹604 crore of ₹669 crore standalone EBITDA in Q1 FY25), with about a 19-20% national market share and over 30% share online.
- **Food & FMCG** — Branded staples — wheat flour/atta, basmati and other rice, plus sugar, soya nuggets, besan, poha, suji, rawa and maida (now ~30% of the segment's volume) — the designated growth engine at roughly ₹1,500 crore a quarter (~₹6,000 crore a year), but thin on profit (only ₹31 crore EBITDA in Q1 FY25) because gross margin is ploughed back into distribution and trade schemes; ~6% share in wheat flour and ~12% in basmati, where it is the number-three player.
- **Industry Essentials** — The bulk, business-to-business leg — castor products and oleochemicals (oleo is about 30% of the segment at high-single-digit margins, with specialty chemicals now 7-8% of oleo volumes) — the smallest of the three segments (₹48 crore EBITDA in Q1 FY25) and the most exposed to commodity swings, with castor hit by weak macros in FY26.
- **Alternate channels (e-commerce, quick commerce, HoReCa) and branded exports** — Not a reporting segment but management's stated 'channel of the future', spanning online grocery, quick commerce (~30% of alternate-channel volumes, growing 65% year-on-year), hotels/restaurants/caterers and exports; alternate channels alone ran at about ₹4,800 crore of trailing-12-month revenue and grew 42-43% year-on-year.
- **Acquired and subsidiary businesses (Kohinoor, G.D. Foods, Omkar Chemicals, Bangladesh)** — Bolt-ons that ride AWL's distribution: Kohinoor basmati (bought May 2022, growing 32-39% year-on-year), G.D. Foods (bought April 2025, ~15% revenue growth on 54% material margins), Omkar Chemicals, and a Bangladesh subsidiary — together they explain why consolidated profit runs slightly ahead of standalone.

### The story in depth

AWL is, at its core, a very large staples distributor that happens to make edible oil, flour, rice and sugar. The quality work scores its distribution and accessibility highest of anything in the business — about 970,000 direct outlets and 2.6 million in total, 63,000 rural towns, HoReCa in roughly 48-plus towns, and newer channels such as e-commerce and quick commerce growing 43-65% a year. That reach is exactly what the multibagger screen picks up as its one clear hit: Market Share Gainers is a strong fit, with edible-oil share moving from about 18.4% to 19.5% and up another 60 basis points in the March-2026 quarter, more than 30% share on e-commerce and quick commerce, and basmati climbing from 5.1% toward the 9-12% range after the Kohinoor purchase. The same reach is what makes acquisitions work — Kohinoor went nationwide and GD Foods scaled without cutting price. Management scores well on candor for owning setbacks in plain numbers, and the financial engine is genuinely efficient: every rupee of assets throws off about three rupees of sales, only about a day of cash sits between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and the business generated roughly 34 rupees of operating cash per 100 rupees of capital employed.

The catch is that the same trait producing those wins also caps what the company can charge. After direct input costs AWL keeps only about 10 rupees of every 100 rupees of sales, and gross margin is quoted per tonne (roughly Rs.11,500-12,000) on commodity-linked prices — which is why the quality framework marks intangible benefits at its worst score and rates competitive advantage at zero. The screen reaches the same conclusion from the other direction: Pricing Power is a clear no-fit, because in late 2022 the company had to pass a 50% price crash straight through to consumers to defend share, at a cost of about Rs.150 crore of gross margin, and by FY26 the industry was shrinking pack sizes from 1,000g to 750g to hold price points. Cost to Replicate is also a no-fit, and Toll Roads fails because edible oil is one of the biggest single lines in a household food budget, so shoppers switch on price. Brand Strength only partly fits for the same reason — rising advertising spend and the Fortune name buy volume and shelf share, but food-segment profits get consumed by that spend rather than converting into a price premium. In short, this is the Low Price Plus model of scale and turns (about Rs.74,000 crore of revenue on roughly Rs.2,300 crore of operating profit, a 3-4% margin, 6.8 million tonnes), and it earns its return through velocity, not margin.

The risk review lines up with those gaps rather than adding new ones. Good Enough Goods is the single high risk, and it is the direct consequence of the thin gross margin: private labels and small repackers beat AWL on wheat flour whenever the cost of carrying harvest-bought grain disappears, and regional players return whenever prices deflate — the same forces behind the negative industry-structure score and the New Entrants watch flag. Cyclicality is elevated for a related reason: with margin measured per tonne, commodity swings hit profit hard, as the Rs.150 crore inventory loss and the FY24 hedge mis-alignment showed. Government policy is a watch item too, through import duties, price jawboning and the rice export ban that pushed volumes into low-margin government-to-government orders. Balanced against that, the risks tied to demand itself are all low — no single customer or supplier matters, demand is essential rather than fashionable, and management is pivoting ahead of health and channel shifts. Capital allocation is where the trade-off is least settled: growth capex keeps going in while plants run at only about 63% utilisation on 3-3.5% margins, which implies modest returns on the new money even though returns on the existing base are strong. The combined rating of 57 out of 100 — Decent, with resilience judged sound — reads as a well-run, hard-to-copy-in-reach distributor operating in a structurally unattractive product category, where share gains are real but each incremental point is bought with spend and price rather than banked as profit.

## Section 2 — Does it look like a long-term winner? (11 multibagger patterns)

### Market Share Gainers — STRONG FIT
*"Winning customers from rivals"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls confirm the pattern with clear, repeated evidence and the numbers agree (1 of 1 check computable supporting).

**What the calls show:** Explicit, repeated and mostly rising share numbers across four years: edible oil 19.2%→19.5% (FY23), 18.4%→~19% (FY25), +60bps in Q4FY26 with >30% share on e-com/q-com; basmati 5.1%→8.5%→~10% with Kohinoor, then +330bps to ~9-11.9% MAT; direct outlets 740,000 (FY25) → 970,000 (FY26), rural towns 30,000 → 63,000 — and gains were funded by distribution investment rather than price destruction.

> "one significant improvement, which we saw in the basmati rice where our market share improved by 330 basis points" — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Sales compounding at 15% while margins held or improved (4% → 3%) — growth that was WON, not bought with price cuts.

### Low Price Plus — LIKELY FIT
*"Cheapest — and still the best"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show some of the traits and the only computable check supports the pattern.

**What the calls show:** The model is genuinely scale-and-turns — ~₹74,000 crore revenue on ~₹2,300 crore EBITDA (roughly 3-4% margin), 6.8 million tonnes, 2.6 million outlets, and explicit affordability engineering via grammage — but the calls never show an itemised cost culture, and management concedes smaller players undercut them on wheat.

> "This is only to reduce the price and create affordability." — *management, on an earnings call*

- ✅ Keeps ₹10 of every ₹100 of sales after input costs and turns its assets 3.0× a year with 19% returns on capital — thin margins made up on volume, the low-price-winner profile.

*"Owns the shop, not just the product"*

## What to watch

- Whether market-share gains keep coming without further margin give-up — the Market Share Gainers pattern is the one strong fit, but if share only advances through grammage cuts and advertising that consumes food-segment profit, it stops being evidence of an advantage.

- Gross margin per tonne (Rs.11,500-12,000) holding through the next commodity swing — the Cyclicality risk is elevated and the Pricing Power no-fit rests on prior episodes where price crashes were passed straight through at the company's expense.
- Continued specific, quantified disclosure of setbacks — the candor score of 2 and the partial fit on Corporate Culture both rest on management naming its own errors; vaguer explanations of a bad quarter would weaken both.
