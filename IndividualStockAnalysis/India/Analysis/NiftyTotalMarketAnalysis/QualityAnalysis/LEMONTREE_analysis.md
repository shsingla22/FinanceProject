# Lemon Tree Hotels Ltd. (LEMONTREE) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

*Industry: SERVICES*

Lemon Tree Hotels runs a chain of hotels across India, selling rooms plus food, banquets and event space to business travellers, MICE (meetings, incentives, conferences, exhibitions) groups, leisure tourists, wedding parties and airline crew. It makes money two ways: from hotels the group itself owns or leases, where revenue depends on average room rate (ARR of Rs. 6,875 in FY26) and occupancy (73.5%), and from management and franchise fees charged to third-party hotel owners who put a Lemon Tree brand on their property. Management describes its core positioning as the mid-market segment, with a portfolio of seven brands spanning upper upscale, upscale, midscale and economy. As of March 2026 it operated 131 hotels with 11,811 rooms (22,581 rooms including the signed pipeline), and FY26 revenue was Rs. 1,452.7 crore with net EBITDA of Rs. 699.3 crore and PAT of Rs. 288.3 crore; the group is splitting into an asset-light branding/management company (Lemon Tree) and a hotel-owning company (Fleur Hotels).

**The parts of the business:**

- **Owned and leased hotels (largely held through subsidiary Fleur Hotels)** — The hotels the group actually owns, where it books the full room, food and banquet revenue and carries the debt — this is the bulk of revenue and, post-demerger, becomes Fleur Hotels with about 39 operational hotels and 5,600 rooms concentrated in the top 6 cities.
- **Managed and franchised hotels (asset-light, third-party owned)** — Lemon Tree puts its brands and management on hotels owned by other people and collects base and incentive fees; third-party fees were Rs. 73.9 crore in FY26 (up 23% YoY) and this is the main growth engine, with 55 contracts and 4,912 rooms signed in FY26 alone.
- **Management fees from Fleur Hotels** — The parent charges its own subsidiary for managing its hotels — Rs. 95.8 crore in FY26, up 8% — which together with third-party fees made total management fees Rs. 169.7 crore, and these long-term contracts are meant to survive the demerger as Lemon Tree's stable fee income.
- **Aurika (upper upscale brand)** — The group's highest-rate, highest-margin brand, including the roughly 1,000-room Aurika Mumbai at the airport and Aurika Udaipur (which hosted 21-25 weddings in one season); all future new supply is being planned under Aurika, with Nehru Place Delhi, Shimla and a 47-room heritage hotel in Varanasi in the pipeline.
- **Lemon Tree Premier / Lemon Tree Hotels (mid-market core)** — The main midscale and upscale brands that make up most of the room count in business cities like Delhi, Gurgaon, Hyderabad, Bangalore, Mumbai and Pune; Lemon Tree Premier was called the strongest performer of the seven brands in FY23.
- **Keys (Keys Select / Keys Lite, acquired end-2019)** — A lower-priced acquired brand that lagged for years and had up to 25% of its rooms shut for renovation, but which management says delivered roughly 25% RevPAR growth in Q3 FY26 once refurbished.
- **Renovation programme (owned portfolio)** — Not a revenue line but a major cost the calls keep returning to — Rs. 250-300 crore spent over three years upgrading about 4,100 owned rooms, which shuts inventory (700-800 rooms at a time) and, with technology spend and a GST change, cut FY26 EBITDA margins by roughly 580 bps.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Lemon Tree Hotels earns its "Decent" 64-out-of-100 rating on the strength of two things that show up again and again across all three reviews: it can charge more than it used to, and the people running it say what is actually happening. On pricing, the company keeps ₹94 of every ₹100 of sales after direct input costs, runs net EBITDA margins of 48-51%, and has pushed average room rates up about 11-12% — from ₹5,902 in Q2 FY25 to ₹7,487 in Q3 FY26. That is not an accident of the cycle; it is a deliberate switch from filling rooms to pricing rooms, visible as far back as holding ₹11,000 at Aurika Udaipur through weak demand in 2022, and it is why the Pricing Power pattern is a strong fit. It is reinforced by a conscious re-mix of guests away from year-round corporate contracts toward retail travellers, who now make up 45% of occupancy versus 33-34% before and pay about 1.25 times the corporate rate. On candour, the same chairman and CFO have run the business across the whole FY21-FY26 window and volunteer bad news without spin — negative Gurgaon RevPAR, weak Bangalore, a lost bid, the GST and renovation drag quantified — which is why both the Candor check and the Corporate Culture pattern score at the top. The cash cycle helps too: money comes in roughly 200 days before suppliers have to be paid, so guests effectively fund the business.

The second story is the shift to an asset-light model, where somebody else pays for the building and Lemon Tree collects a fee for running it. This is the single biggest structural change in the record and it explains three separate pattern verdicts at once. It makes Forward Integrators a strong fit — 55 hotels and 4,912 rooms signed in FY26 alone against a total portfolio that has grown from 84 hotels and 8,251 rooms in August 2022 to 268 hotels and 22,581 rooms, with the company owning its own reservation system, website, loyalty programme and distribution technology. It supports Market Share Gainers, in a mid-market segment that is still largely unbranded, and it is the reason the Recurring Revenue pattern registers at all: third-party management and franchise fees grew from about ₹50 crore in FY24 to ₹73.9 crore in FY26, with total fees of ₹169.7 crore, an annuity that grows as the installed base grows. The funded technology programme — a single data lake, next-generation sales tools, revenue management, loyalty, gen-AI chatbots, with a stated four-to-five-year runway — is being built explicitly to be sold into that managed network, which is why Innovation Dominance rates a likely rather than certain fit. But the very same pivot creates the sharpest single-point risk in the file: after the Fleur demerger, one counterparty accounts for ₹95.8 crore of the ₹169.7 crore of total fees, and the chairman, who sits on both sides, has told analysts the relationship is brand-agnostic with no automatic right to that business. The strength and the exposure are the same fact viewed from two ends.

The risks are mostly the flip side of the strengths rather than separate problems. Pricing power is real but has to be defended every quarter, because mid-market rooms are largely sold through online travel agents where the intermediary frames the price comparison — hence the Good Enough Goods watch flag and the ₹250-300 crore renovation and rebranding programme, accepted at the cost of near-term margin, to justify the premium. Growing inventory faster than the market invites the same treatment from others: roughly 2,000 keys across five competing hotels opened around Aurika Mumbai and held its RevPAR growth to 3% in a quarter, which is the New Entrants risk in miniature. The cost discipline is genuine and itemised — staff per room down from 0.95 to 0.63, food cost from 9% to 6%, power from 10% to 9%, other expenses from 24% to 19% — but the savings are kept and reinvested rather than handed to guests, which is why Low Price Plus is only a partial fit. Underneath everything sits the cycle: demand swings hard with the macro, from a triple-digit rebound off the COVID base to an FY26 hit from geopolitical tension, an airline shutdown and aviation capacity cuts, on top of a GST change costing 1.8% of revenue in one quarter and about 2% expected for FY27, plus a ₹31.3 crore one-off. That is the same lumpiness the quality framework penalises — roughly 16% average annual sales growth but uneven year to year — and it is why the industry-structure score is the only negative area, why the risk review calls resilience strained, and why a 16% cash return on capital and debt cut from ₹1,912 crore to ₹1,500 crore read as solid rather than exceptional. There is no toll-booth quality here and no protective middleman; management itself says the business sits at the front end of discretionary spending.

## Section 3 — What could break it? (8 risk channels)

**Overall:** the risks worth attention are Cyclicality. The balance sheet and cash engine shows one financial stress signal — 1 of the 3 stress checks is flagged — one warning light, not yet a pattern.

- ⚠️ Borrowings are now 1.4× shareholders' funds (up from 0.7×) — a leveraged balance sheet leaves little room when any of the risks above bites.
- ✅ About 84% of recent operating profit became cash — earnings are still backed by cash.
- ✅ Returns on capital improved from about 4% to 13% — the business is earning more on its capital, not less.

### Cyclicality — HIGH RISK
*"Boom-and-bust earnings"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show clear, repeated exposure AND 3 of 3 numeric fingerprints agree — that combination earns the top severity.

**What the calls show:** Across the 18 calls demand and margins swing hard with the macro: revenue +334% year-on-year off the COVID base in Q1 FY23 with net EBITDA up 4400%, then FY26 hit by geopolitical tension, the IndiGo shutdown and aviation capacity cuts, forcing a mid-March 2026 switch from price-led to occupancy-led pricing. Management itself frames the business as sitting at the front end of discretionary spending, and margins move with occupancy/ARR rather than being contractually stable.

> "these uncertainties also affect, because we are at the front end of discretionary consumption" — *management, on an earnings call*

*Silver lining: Flow/operations-linked demand rather than customer capex; countercyclical investment (Rs. 250-300 crore renovation programme and continued signings through the soft patch) plus a growing asset-light fee stream (Rs. 169.7 crore in FY26, +14%) that is less capital- and cycle-geared*

- ⚠️ Year-to-year sales growth swings by ±42 percentage points — boom-and-bust revenue, not a steady compounder.
- ⚠️ Operating margin has ranged from 18% to 51% (swinging ±11.5 points a year) — margins fluctuate a lot, the signature of a cyclical business.
- ⚠️ Capital spending spiked to 96% of sales in its heaviest year, versus a normal 36% — the over-investment-in-the-expansion pattern that starves future returns.

### Government Dependency — WATCH
*"Fortunes decided in Delhi"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Policy repeatedly moves the P&L without the company controlling it: the FY26 GST change alone cost 1.8% of revenue in Q3 and ~2% expected for FY27, plus a Rs. 31.3 crore one-off from the Labour Code and Delhi property tax. But revenue is not set by a regulator, tariff or subsidy, and there is no dependence on government orders or fixed infrastructure.

*Silver lining: Mix shift up-market (all future supply under Aurika, which is 'largely unaffected by this change') mechanically shrinks the GST exposure as ARRs rise above Rs. 7,500*

### Stakeholder Concentration — WATCH
*"Too few customers, suppliers or channels"*

**Why this verdict:** the calls show real but bounded exposure while the numbers stay quiet.

**What the calls show:** Post-demerger Lemon Tree's fee income leans heavily on one counterparty — Fleur contributed Rs. 95.8 crore of Rs. 169.7 crore total fees in FY26 — and Keswani, chairing both sides, told analysts the relationship is brand-agnostic with no automatic right to Fleur's assets, exactly the single-relationship renewal question the framework flags. ## What to watch

- Fleur fee dependence (Stakeholder Concentration risk): whether ₹95.8 crore of ₹169.7 crore in fees stays with Lemon Tree given the stated brand-agnostic, no-automatic-right arrangement.
- Conversion of the 22,581-room pipeline into fee income (Recurring Revenue pattern): third-party fees growing 23% off a ₹73.9 crore base, against the 3-3.5 year lag from signing to opening.
- Room rate holding up under new local supply (New Entrants risk and Pricing Power pattern): whether the ₹7,487 average rate keeps compounding after episodes like the 2,000 competing keys that capped Aurika Mumbai RevPAR growth at 3%.
- Payback on the ₹250-300 crore renovation and rebranding spend (Good Enough Goods risk): whether the stated two-year payback and repricing of Red Fox Delhi and Keys Select actually materialise in rates.
- Smoothness of growth and returns through the cycle (Persistence of Growth check, Cyclicality risk): whether the 16% cash return on capital and the debt reduction from ₹1,912 crore to ₹1,500 crore survive the ~2% FY27 GST drag and further discretionary-demand shocks.
