# Manorama Inds (MANORAMA) — The Analyst's Report

## About the business

Manorama Industries, set up in 2005, collects wild tree-borne seeds — sal, mango kernel, kokum and mowrah from Indian forests, and shea nuts from West Africa (Ghana, Burkina Faso, Togo, Benin, Nigeria) — and processes them into cocoa butter equivalents (CBE) and other specialty fats and butters, the ingredients that go into chocolate, confectionery and cosmetics. Its customers are the world's large chocolate, confectionery and personal-care companies, named on the calls as Ferrero, Mondelez, Nestle, Hershey, Mars, Barry Callebaut and Body Shop, in both export and Indian markets. It makes money by buying cheap forest-floor seeds through a network of thousands of collection centres and millions of tribal collectors, then upgrading them at its single integrated plant at Birkoni near Raipur, Chhattisgarh, through solvent extraction, refining, fractionation and interesterification, and selling customised high-value fats at rich margins — FY26 standalone revenue was INR 1,357 crores, up 76.1%, with a 27.1% EBITDA margin and INR 233.2 crores profit. Management calls this a 'waste to wealth', backward-integrated model, and is spending about INR 460 crores over two to three years to add capacity in India and a raw-material processing plant in Burkina Faso.

**The parts of the business:**

- **Cocoa butter equivalents (CBE) and specialty fats and butters** — The core business — sal-based and shea-based CBE, stearin, mango and other exotic fats made to each customer's recipe for chocolate, confectionery and cosmetics; management says these 'value-added products' are roughly 70%-75% of sales and drive the ~27% EBITDA margin.
- **Fractionation and refining operations at Birkoni** — The single integrated plant that does solvent extraction, refining, fractionation and interesterification; fractionation capacity went from 15,000 MT in FY21 to 40,000 MT in July 2024 (INR 212 crores invested to date) and is being debottlenecked to about 52,000 MT, running at 80%-85% utilisation.
- **Seed sourcing and seed export** — The sourcing arm that buys 80,000-1,00,000 MT of sal seeds a year in India (under 10% of the country's 15 lakh MT crop) and 40,000-80,000 MT of shea nuts in West Africa; the company also calls itself India's leading exporter of sal and mango seeds, and it is a Government-recognised Star Export House.
- **R&D and customised solutions (Milcoa R&D Centre)** — An in-house DSIR-recognised research centre at Birkoni, led by Dr. Krish Bhaggan, that develops new fats and applications jointly with customers — management treats it as the moat that lets it charge for tailor-made products rather than commodity fat.
- **Planned expansion projects (INR 460 crores capex)** — Announced but not yet operating: a 75,000 MTPA cocoa butter alternatives plant (forward integration), a new 75,000 MTPA fractionation facility, a 90,000 MTPA refinery, and a 90,000 MTPA seed-processing unit in Burkina Faso (backward integration), plus 19.40 acres of adjoining land bought and a new packing plant and laboratory already commissioned from internal accruals.

*Described strictly from management's own words on the earnings calls.*

### The story in depth

Manorama makes specialty cocoa butter equivalents and other exotic tree-borne fats, and the record describes a business that is hard to copy for reasons that took twenty years to build. It collects sal, mango and shea seed through a multi-decade network of tribal gatherers across thousands of forest collection centres in India and West Africa, then extracts, refines and fractionates at one integrated site. That gives it what the quality framework calls a mini-monopoly: it is the only supplier that can put the full basket of these fats in front of a customer with a formulation tailored to that customer's product. Because those formulations are co-developed and take two to three years to qualify, accounts like Mondelez, Nestle, Ferrero and Hershey stay put once approved. The same facts drive three of the strongest pattern verdicts — Toll Roads (a small ingredient that is functionally critical to a large customer's product), Cost to Replicate (the collection network plus the qualification cycle), and Corporate Culture (FY25 guidance of INR 750 crore, then FY26 guidance raised from INR 1,050 crore to 1,150 to 1,300, with INR 1,358 crore actually delivered). Growth has been real rather than promised: revenue moved from INR 133 crore in Q1 FY25 to INR 1,358 crore in FY26, operating margin sits near 26-27 rupees per 100 of sales, return on capital employed is 33.6, and fractionation capacity has gone from 15,000 tonnes in FY21 to 40,000 tonnes by July 2024 and on toward 52,000 tonnes, funded largely out of the company's own cash flow.

The interesting part is how often a single trait shows up as both a strength and a worry. The sourcing base is the clearest example: seasonal forest crops are exactly what blocks new entrants — there is no new global capacity and no easy way to buy access to the harvest — but they are also why the P&L swings like a commodity business (gross margin fell from 52.8% to 44.3% in a single quarter) and why roughly 334 days of cash sits stuck between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. That is the framework's one clearly negative check, and it explains why the Recurring Revenue pattern is a flat no: this business consumes working capital rather than being funded in advance by its customers. Customer concentration works the same way. The two-to-three-year qualification cycle that locks in the world's biggest chocolate makers also means the top ten customers are about 40% of sales and results are framed around a handful of named buyers — large procurement organisations with the leverage that implies. Even the government links cut both ways: the MoU with the Government of Burkina Faso underpins an INR 120 crore backward-integration plant and a Chhattisgarh MoU backs future capex, which strengthens the supply moat while making the company answerable to policy decisions it does not control. None of these carry a severe verdict — cyclicality, government dependency, stakeholder concentration and the good-enough-goods question are all rated watch rather than danger, and technology change, new entrants, shifting consumer preferences and fashion risk are all rated low.

Where the record is more guarded is on pricing and on what management does with the money. Pricing power is only a likely fit, not a strong one: in August 2024 contracts were rebooked 10-15% higher during a shortage and margins expanded 700 basis points over two years, but by January 2026 management said CBE is being locked at the same prices as before and attributed roughly 65 of the 73% growth to volume. Margin gains since then have come from mix — value-added products rising from 50-60% of sales to 70-75%, lifting operating margin from 20.1% to about 27% — which is a different and more fragile thing than being able to raise prices at will. This connects directly to the good-enough-goods concern: 27% margins on a fractionation process that is not especially complex invite the question of whether the economics are protected by genuine scarcity or by a favourable moment. Brand strength and innovation dominance land in the same likely-but-unproven place — there is real technical credibility (100-plus awards, a full certification stack, a DSIR-recognised R&D centre, an enzymatic ESOS/HPMF route, a Deputy CEO appointment) but no disclosed R&D spend and no consumer brand behind it. On the framework's own area scores, capital allocation is the weakest at 0.33 and management second weakest at 0.67, against much stronger marks for customer benefits, returns and industry structure. The combined rating of 76 out of 100 — 77 on business quality, 69 on multibagger fit, 84 on risk safety — reads as Strong with sound resilience: a genuinely defended niche in a 2 million tonne market growing 6-7% a year where the company still procures under 10% of India's sal seed supply, held back from a higher grade less by any single flaw than by a cash-hungry cycle, a concentrated customer list, and a pricing story that has not yet been shown to survive a normal market.
